\subsection{Preview: No-Go versus Mechanism}

\slides{Entropy tells you what will not happen. It does not hand you a machine.}

\slidesincremental{
* Shannon: a distribution, then a prohibition --- no algorithm named
* Physics talks: heat baths, pistons, frictionless doors
* Second law: entropy is produced *somewhere*; not when the Earth hits the Sun
* Week 2 develops this as entropy versus probability
}

\notes{Shannon's contribution, for this course, is less the proofs than the refusal to talk about mechanism. He writes down a probability distribution. He does not say which device emitted it. Under those circumstances the properties of entropy make a set of things impossible. That is what a capacity theorem is. The second law has the same shape: entropy must be produced somewhere. It does not tell you when organised motion will stop. Dynamics would. Entropy will not.}

\notes{That is also why I have come to doubt that probability is enough, even though most of my working life has been probabilistic modelling. Probability insists that we specify what *will* happen, including the parts of the support we do not care about. Entropy is the language in which we say what will *not* happen. Week 2 takes that contrast properly, including MacKay, no-go versus prescription, and the common distributions as entropies. Today we only need the distinction, because it is why the demon debate talks past itself: one side is arguing mechanism, the other is arguing a no-go.}
