\subsection{Old Books, and the Wikipedia Effect}

\slidesincremental{
* Rogers and Mayhew: engineering thermodynamics, father then son
* An out-of-print 1961 physics thermodynamics text --- often clearer than a modern one
* Wikipedia about 2006 versus Wikipedia now: intuition edited into correctness
* Not required reading; a warning about where intuition lives
}

\notes{Before the worksheets I want to say something about books. I was a mechanical engineer. The thermodynamics text was Rogers and Mayhew, *Engineering Thermodynamics, Work and Heat Transfer*. It was my father's copy from Sheffield in the 1960s, and it was still the assigned book when I did the same degree in the 1990s. Thermodynamics does not move quickly. I did not feel I understood it then, and I do not feel I understand it now. The diagrams of critical points and liquid--vapour mixtures are printed, not drawn in LaTeX, and the volume still smells like a 1960s book.}

\notes{When I have asked language models for a thermodynamics text they are, unsurprisingly, fluent in books they have absorbed. One recommendation was a physicist's thermodynamics book, printed in 1961, now out of print. I bought a copy. In places it explains more clearly than a modern textbook. I think of this as the Wikipedia effect. If you lived through 2006, Wikipedia articles then often explained what a thing *was* and what it was *for*. Later edits made them more correct, and often unreadable unless you already had the degree. The Gaussian process article is the example I use. Look at the 2006 revision if you want the intuition; look at the current one if you want the statement that will survive a referee. I wonder whether thermodynamics books have followed the same path: we have not understood the intuition much better, but some of the older books still carry it. I am not asking you to read these volumes. If a particular passage would help, I may scan it.}
