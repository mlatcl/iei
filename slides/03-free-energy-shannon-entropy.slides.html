Gremlin, Not Demon

Maxwell’s demon sorts. Laplace’s gremlin is the admission that complete knowledge is not available in practice.

Joseph Black and Heat as a Quantity

Joseph Black

Joseph Black (1728–1799). Black was a physician and chemist at Glasgow, then Edinburgh. Stipple engraving by James Heath after Henry Raeburn, 1800 (Wellcome Collection / Wikimedia Commons, public domain).
This is the Worksheet 1 follow-up. Clausius was not doing uncertainty. He was doing heat. We start with the person who made heat measurable. Do not yet write \(\mathrm{d}Q/T\). Portrait, dates, Glasgow then Edinburgh, Watt as a colleague. Then the stuck thermometer.

The Problem

Ice melts. The thermometer does not move.

  • Heat is entering the mixture
  • Temperature stays at the melting point until the ice is gone
  • If heat were temperature, that would be a contradiction

Heat Is Not Temperature

Temperature is the reading. Heat is what you add.

  • Mix two bodies of known mass and starting temperature; one final reading
  • Equal heat, equal mass: mercury rises more than water — different capacity
  • Ice melts with the thermometer stuck — heat can be hidden
Keep mechanism and maths apart. Mechanism: mix, or melt ice, or time a kettle. Maths: heat as an additive quantity independent of the thermometer. Capacity first, then latent. Students often still hear heat'' ashow hot’’.

The Ice Calorimeter

Black mixed and timed. This box weighs meltwater.

The calorimeter of Lavoisier and Laplace. Heat delivered is read as the mass of ice melted; the conversion factor is Black’s latent heat of fusion. Plate XI from Encyclopaedia Londinensis (1801–02), after H. Lascelles (Wikimedia Commons / Science History Institute, public domain).
  • Heat leaving the sample melts ice in the jacket
  • You weigh what melted — that is the heat
  • The conversion is Black’s latent heat of fusion
Be precise if someone asks who invented the calorimeter. Black made heat measurable. This apparatus is Lavoisier and Laplace, using Black’s latent heat of fusion as the scale. The picture is the mechanism: weigh what melted.

Latent Heat of Steam

A kettle on a steady fire. Time is the meter.

  • Clock the water from ice-cold to the boil; then clock it boiling dry
  • Boiling dry takes about four and a half times as long
  • That extra heat does not move the thermometer. Black calls it latent
Do not put \(810^\circ\mathrm{F}\) on the slide. That number is a quantity of heat in Black’s unit — the heat that would raise the same water by one Fahrenheit degree — not a temperature. The experiment is the kettle: time to the boil versus time to boil dry. Four and a half times as long, thermometer stuck. Watt and the modern figure live in the notes.

Sadi Carnot and the Fall of Heat

Sadi Carnot

Sadi Carnot (1796–1832), aged 17, in the uniform of the École Polytechnique. Portrait by Louis-Léopold Boilly, 1813 (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).
Military engineer, not a natural philosopher. Son of Lazare; Polytechnique; the book is 1824. Do not yet say second law or entropy. The question is engines.

Why Look at Heat Engines?

Steam already drives mills, mines, and looms. Nobody has a theory of it.

  • Watt’s engines are a British industrial fact
  • Engineers try higher pressure, and other fluids than steam
  • Carnot: is there a limit? Does the choice of fluid matter?

The Réflexions

Title page of R.~H. Thurston’s 1890 English translation of Carnot’s 1824 Réflexions sur la puissance motrice du feu. The original was printed in Paris by Bachelier, at Carnot’s expense, in six hundred copies.

A Heat Engine Is a Mill

Caloric falls from hot to cold. The same caloric arrives at the bottom.

Carnot’s own figure of the mill (1824). The cylinder \(ab\) holds the working body; \(cd\) is the piston. Body \(A\) is the furnace, body \(B\) the refrigerator — two reservoirs of caloric at different temperatures. Caloric taken from \(A\) is given up to \(B\). Vectorised from the plate in Réflexions sur la puissance motrice du feu (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).
  • Water mill: quantity of water, height of the fall; the same water arrives below
  • Heat engine: quantity of caloric, difference of temperature; the same caloric arrives in the condenser
  • Two reservoirs. Nothing is used up.
Point at \(A\) and \(B\). Furnace and refrigerator. Same caloric in, same caloric out. The piston is the mill wheel, not heat being turned into something else. Do not mention Joule. Students may want to correct him. Hold that.

A Short Life

Carnot dies in the 1832 cholera epidemic, aged 36. The book almost vanishes with him.

  • Six hundred copies, paid for by the author, unread
  • Clapeyron recasts the argument in 1834
Stop. Do not derive \(\mathrm{d}Q/T\). Next is Rumford’s cannon.

Count Rumford’s Cannon

Count Rumford

Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford (1753–1814). American-born, then in the service of the Elector of Bavaria. Portrait by Moritz Kellerhoven (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).
Flashback, not a new founding myth. Dates: 1798 paper, Munich arsenal. Do not say this killed caloric. Carnot still uses caloric in 1824.

The Cannon

A blunt borer, a brass cylinder, a box of water, horses turning the gun.

  • About \(18.8\,\mathrm{lb}\) of water, starting at \(60^\circ\mathrm{F}\)
  • The cylinder at about \(32\) turns a minute
  • After two hours and thirty minutes the water boils — with no fire

Rumford’s Apparatus

Rumford’s 1798 apparatus, as reprinted in The Complete Works of Count Rumford (1870). Fig.~2: the cannon in the boring machine, turned by the bar \(w\). Fig.~3: the same cylinder in the water box that is brought to the boil.
Fig.~3 is the mechanism: blunt borer inside, water outside. The heat is not coming from the chips, and it does not run out.

Inexhaustible

Furnishing heat without limitation …

  • The chips do not lose a hidden store of caloric
  • The heat continues as long as the work continues
  • Rumford’s conclusion: heat is a mode of motion
Quote: inexhaustible, therefore not a substance. Motion, not a number. Joule will supply the number. Carnot never had to face this, because his question was a cycle, not friction.

Mercury, Ice, Steam, and Air

Fahrenheit and Celsius

Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit (1686–1736) with a mercury thermometer. Anonymous engraving, 18th century (Wikimedia Commons, public domain). The instrument is mercury in glass; the scale is equal expansion of that mercury between two pins.

A degree is an equal expansion of mercury. Ice and steam are the pins.

  • Fahrenheit (1724): mercury; ice at \(32\), steam at \(212\)
  • Celsius (1742): the same two pins, later numbered \(0\) and \(100\)
  • Neither scale is heat. Both are the expansion of one liquid
Hold up the mercury tube. Two pins, ice and steam. Fahrenheit or Celsius is only how you number the interval. Do not call this heat. Black already split those. Thomson is about to ask what a degree should be if it is not this expansion.

The Air Thermometer

  1. Constant pressure: a sliding mercury index, open to the room; the volume of the trapped air is the reading. (b) Constant volume: a mercury manometer; the height \(h\) is the reading. Scheme by P. Peterlin (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0). Thomson’s 1848 comparison is with (a).

The laboratory standard is air, not mercury. Pressure is held at the room’s.

  • Trap a mass of air behind mercury that is open to the atmosphere
  • Slide the thread, or lower the cistern, until the mercury surfaces are level
  • Volume is then the reading; ice and steam still pin \(0\) and \(100\)
This is the apparatus. Mercury open to the room, surfaces level, pressure of the trapped air equals the barometer. Volume is the degree. Ice \(0\), steam \(100\), same pins as Celsius. Still air. Kelvin’s question is: what is a degree if it is not this?

Thomson’s Scale from the Mill

William Thomson

William Thomson (1824–1907), later Lord Kelvin. Photograph from The World’s Work, 1902 (Wikimedia Commons, public domain) — half a century after the 1848 scale.
Call him Thomson. Glasgow, 1848, age 24. Do not call this the Kelvin scale. Do not mention Joule. Fahrenheit already exists. He is not moving ice and steam. He is saying what a degree is.

What a Degree Is

The pins are a century old. He is defining the degree.

  • Fahrenheit, Celsius, air: a degree is equal expansion of one substance
  • Thomson, aged 24: a degree is an equal fall of caloric — the same motive power from the same quantity of heat, anywhere on the scale
  • Ice remains \(0\), steam remains \(100\). He has not moved the pins. He has defined the rungs.
Why bother, if ice and steam are already marked? Because a mercury degree and an air degree are not the same mill-height. He is making the rungs equal. He is twenty-four. Caloric is still conserved.

An Absolute Thermometer

A degree is a height of fall of conserved heat. Ice is still \(0\), steam still \(100\).

  • Take a unit of heat from a body at \(T\) on this scale
  • Let it fall to a body at \(T-1\); the same unit arrives
  • A perfect mill in between always gives the same motive power, whatever \(T\) is

Is There a Bottom?

In 1848 the mill has no bottom. Infinite cold is infinitely far down.

  • Air already suggests a wall: its volume would vanish at about \(-273^\circ\)
  • That number is Gay-Lussac’s expansion, not Carnot’s mill
  • Thomson still pins \(0\) at ice; on his mill scale you can always fall one more equal degree
Do not sell 1848 as discovering \(0\,\mathrm{K}\). The pins are already known. The air thermometer’s \(-273\) is vanishing volume. The mill still has no floor.

Heat Is Not Destroyed

He still thinks with Carnot. Heat is conserved. The mill turns because caloric falls.

  • A unit of heat that leaves the hot body arrives at the cold
  • Motive power is the mill, not heat being used up
  • ``The conversion of heat into mechanical effect is probably impossible, certainly undiscovered’’
Read the quote. He is twenty-four. Caloric still falls. Do not bring in Joule. Next is the paddle wheel.

Joule and the Mechanical Equivalent

James Prescott Joule

James Prescott Joule (1818–1889). Seated portrait, later in life (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).
Brewer, Manchester, private lab. Not Carnot’s question. Do not start a Mayer priority fight; one sentence is enough. The paddle wheel is the mechanism.

The Paddle Wheel

Joule’s apparatus for the mechanical equivalent of heat. A falling weight turns a paddle in water; the work is known, the rise in temperature is measured. Engraving from Harper’s New Monthly Magazine, August 1869 (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).
Mechanism: falling weights, paddle, thermometer. Maths: work against heat, a ratio. Contrast with Carnot’s mill still in the students’ heads.

A Number

A definite amount of work makes a definite amount of heat.

  • 1845, British Association at Cambridge: the paddle already gives an equivalent, still scattered
  • 1850, Philosophical Transactions: \(772\) foot-pounds to raise \(1\,\mathrm{lb}\) of water by \(1^\circ\mathrm{F}\)
  • Modern, on the same scale: about \(778\) foot-pounds, or \(4.186\,\mathrm{J}\) per calorie
Cambridge 1845 is BAAS, not the Philosophical Society. Thomson’s scale is the CPS paper. The number is still 890, not 772.

Not Carnot’s Question

Joule is converting work into heat. He is not running a mill between two baths.

  • Carnot: maximum work from a fall of conserved caloric
  • Joule: how much work makes how much heat
  • Conservation of heat cannot be the general law if the paddle is right
Mill versus paddle, one more time. Then the contradiction. Do not derive \(\mathrm{d}Q/T\).

The Contradiction

You cannot keep Carnot’s conserved heat and Joule’s equivalent as general laws.

  • If heat is conserved, none of it becomes work
  • If Joule is right, some of it does
  • What can be kept: a limit that depends only on the two temperatures

Clausius and \(\mathrm{d}Q/T\)

Rudolf Clausius

Rudolf Clausius (1822–1888). Photograph from the AIP Emilio Segrè Visual Archives, Lande Collection (Wikimedia Commons, CC0).
Portrait, 1850, two laws. Do not start with Boltzmann. The question is still engines: if heat is not conserved, what is the leftover identity?

Two Laws

Keep Joule’s equivalent. Keep Carnot’s limit. Drop conservation of heat.

  • First law: heat and work are interchangeable, at Joule’s rate
  • Second law: heat does not flow from cold to hot without a cost
  • A cycle cannot turn the heat of one bath entirely into work

The Ratio that Survives

After Joule, Carnot’s mill is a ratio of heats, not a conserved fluid.

  • Reversible engine: \(Q_h/T_h = Q_c/T_c\)
  • \(Q_h\) taken from the hot bath, \(Q_c\) delivered to the cold
  • Heat does not balance. Heat over temperature does
Put \(Q_h/T_h = Q_c/T_c\) on the board. Both \(Q\)s positive. The gap \(Q_h-Q_c\) is the work. The thing that matches is \(Q/T\).

The Loop

A reversible cycle is a closed path. If it is reversible, the integral is zero.

\[\oint \frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{T} = 0 \qquad \text{(reversible cycle)}\]

  • Every reversible loop integrates to zero
  • So there is a state function \(S\) with \(\mathrm{d}S = \mathrm{d}Q_{\mathrm{rev}}/T\)
One display, then state function. Contrast \(\oint\mathrm{d}Q \neq 0\) (work) with \(\oint\mathrm{d}Q/T = 0\) (no leftover transformation). Next slide is the sentence they should leave with.

A Reversible Cycle Produces No Entropy

You return to the start. Nothing has been created.

  • The working substance: \(S\) is a state function, so \(\Delta S = 0\) around any cycle
  • The baths: the entropy that left the hot bath arrives at the cold bath
  • Universe: \(\Delta S = 0\). A reversible cycle produces no entropy
Say the title sentence twice. System versus baths versus universe, in that order, slowly. Students will try to make \(S\) increase around a reversible cycle. Hold them to zero.

When the Cycle Is Irreversible

The working substance still comes home. The books do not.

  • Friction, a finite drop of temperature, a paddle in the mechanism
  • More heat is dumped to the cold bath than the reversible case
  • \(\displaystyle\oint \mathrm{d}Q/T < 0\), and the entropy of the universe increases

The Name

Clausius names entropy, 1865. English translation of the memoir: \(\int \mathrm{d}Q/T = S-S_0\), from Greek τροπή, transformation. He forms the word to sit beside energy.
Show the page. Transformation, not disorder, not uncertainty. Worksheet 1 payoff: this is what Clausius was measuring. Boltzmann’s count and Shannon’s \(H\) come later, and they reuse a functional form, not this operational meaning.

Not Uncertainty

Clausius’s \(S\) is the piece that fits the puzzle: heat divided by temperature.

  • Macroscopic: \(S\) from \(\mathrm{d}Q_{\mathrm{rev}}/T\); a reversible cycle produces none
  • Microscopic, later: Maxwell, Boltzmann, Gibbs count the same \(S\)
  • Information, later: Shannon, Jaynes reuse the functional form for a different job

Thomson Returns: The Scale After Joule

Thomson, 1851

The 1848 scale assumed conserved heat. After Joule that assumption is gone.

  • Clausius, 1850: two laws; Carnot’s ceiling without caloric conservation
  • Thomson, 1851: accepts Joule; heat is not a conserved fluid
  • What survives: a reversible engine still has a limit that depends only on the two temperatures
Call him Thomson still. 1851, not 1848. Clausius has already written the two laws. Thomson is rewriting the thermometer, not discovering the second law.

The Ratio, Not the Fall

Equal mill-degrees are replaced by a proportion of the two heats.

  • Reversible engine: \(\displaystyle\frac{Q_h}{T_h} = \frac{Q_c}{T_c}\)
  • The same identity Clausius wrote as \(\mathrm{d}S = \mathrm{d}Q_{\mathrm{rev}}/T\)
  • Absolute temperatures are now those for which this ratio holds

Now There Is a Bottom

Absolute zero is where a reversible engine would reject no heat.

  • \(T=0\) is \(Q_c=0\) for a perfect mill between \(T_h\) and the floor
  • The air thermometer’s \(-273^\circ\) was vanishing volume; this is vanishing rejected heat
  • Infinite cold is no longer infinitely far down the mill
1848: ice is zero, infinite cold at \(-\infty\). 1851: \(T=0\) is \(Q_c=0\). Do not linger on the later title Lord Kelvin — the paper is Thomson’s.

Molecules

James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879), Ludwig Boltzmann (1844–1906), Josiah Willard Gibbs (1839–1903). After Clausius the account becomes statistical: components of velocity, then occupation of energy, then free energy.

Maxwell–Boltzmann: Components Versus Speed

Each Cartesian velocity component is Gaussian. Speed \(v=|\mathbf{v}|\) is not.

  • Component: \(f(v_x)=\sqrt{m/(2\pi kT)}\,e^{-mv_x^2/2kT}\)
  • Speed: integrate the isotropic 3D Gaussian over angles
  • Jacobian \(d^3v = v^2\,dv\,d\Omega\) supplies the \(v^2\) factor
First look, as taught: Maxwell–Boltzmann is a Gaussian in the velocities. Then: the histogram of one component is Gaussian; the histogram of speed peaks away from zero. Board the spherical shell volume briefly. Both statements are Maxwell–Boltzmann.

Same Physics, Different Variable

\[ f(v_x)=\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{2\pi kT}}\,e^{-mv_x^2/2kT} \qquad\text{versus}\qquad f(v)\propto v^2\,e^{-mv^2/2kT} \]

  • The \(v^2\) is not an energy correction — it is geometry
  • Each component is the quadratic \(E=mv_x^2/2\) whose expectation we split next

The Gibbs–Boltzmann Distribution

Gibbs–Boltzmann Occupation

Same formula, three names: Boltzmann weights, Gibbs distribution, canonical ensemble.

  • \(p_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z\), \(\quad Z(\beta)=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}\)
  • \(\beta=1/k_B T\): coldness; \(T\): bath temperature
  • \(\log Z\) is the cumulant generating function of the energy
Introduce and use, do not derive. Board the formula. MaxEnt with fixed mean energy recovers the same \(p_i\).
Occupation of a two-state system as coldness increases. At low \(\beta\) both states are populated; at high \(\beta\) the ground state dominates.

Aside: Hopfield, Boltzmann Machines, Nobel 2024

The same energy-and-occupation grammar reappears in associative memory and early deep learning.

  • Hopfield (1982): fixed-weight energy; recall as settling
  • Boltzmann machine (1985): make those couplings learnable
  • Pairwise energy: \(E(\mathbf{s})=-\sum_i b_i s_i-\sum_{i<j}W_{ij}s_i s_j\)
Colour only. Nobel 2024 (Hopfield and Hinton) names the line. Do not divert into training algorithms or Landauer here.

Coldness and Temperature

  • \(T\): the bath, the thermometer
  • \(\beta = 1/k_B T\): coldness
  • Same occupation; entropy sits in a different place

Two Representations

  • \(T\)-first: \(F = U - TS\)
  • \(\beta\)-first: \(Z(\beta)\), then \(U = -\partial_\beta\log Z\)
  • Week 5: \(\beta\) is the Lagrange multiplier

Free Energy Decomposition

From Occupation to Accounting

From Gibbs distribution to energy bookkeeping.

  • \(p(x)=e^{-\beta E(x)}/Z\)
  • Equilibrium: inner energy is an expectation, \(U=\left\langle E\right\rangle\)
  • Algebra then defines \(F\) and \(S\)
LO1. Board the occupation with \(Z\), name Summe der Zustände, take the log, take the expectation. Watch the signs. Why the exponential: equilibrium. What \(\log Z\) is: available energy, the work you can get out. Do not invoke MaxEnt — that is week 5. The occupation is given; we are reading it.

Take the Logarithm

\[ \log p(x) = -\beta E(x) - \log Z \]

  • Solve for the microscopic score: \(E(x)=-k_BT\log p(x)-k_BT\log Z\)
  • \(1/\beta=k_BT\) converts coldness back to the bath’s temperature

Equilibrium Identifies \(U\) with \(\left\langle E\right\rangle\)

\[ U \equiv \left\langle E\right\rangle = \sum_x p(x)\, E(x) \]

  • \(E(x)\) is a score on configurations
  • \(U\) is what a calorimeter would call the energy of the system
  • The move is the ensemble: replace a number by an expectation

Name the Two Remaining Terms

\[ U = \underbrace{-k_BT\log Z}_{F} + T\underbrace{\bigl(-k_B\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)\bigr)}_{S} \]

  • Helmholtz free energy: \(F=-k_BT\log Z\)
  • Boltzmann entropy: \(S=-k_B\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)=k_B H\)
  • Hence \(U=F+TS\), or \(F=U-TS\)
Board \(F=U-TS\) and \(U=F+TS\) as the same equation. Helmholtz wrote \(A=U-TS\) (\(A\) for Arbeit). We use \(F\) because week 5 needs \(A(\theta)\) for the log-partition.

Two Temperatures, One Identity

  • \(T\)-first: \(F=U-TS\) — energy, then entropy’s cut
  • \(\beta\)-first: \(F=-\beta^{-1}\log Z\), then \(U=-\partial_\beta\log Z\)
  • Same numbers; week 5 makes \(\beta\) a Lagrange multiplier

Three-State Check

\(U\), \(S\), and \(F\) for the Worksheet 1 three-state system (\(k_B=1\)). The dashed curve is \(-\log Z/\beta\); it sits on \(F\), which is the identity just derived.
  • Prescription: \(p(x)=e^{-\beta E(x)}/Z\)
  • Accounting: \(F=U-TS\) with \(U=\left\langle E\right\rangle\)

The Carnot Cycle on \(T\)-\(S\)

The Fall of Caloric

Carnot compared the motive power of heat to a waterfall. He did not know whether the work was proportional to the height.

Carnot’s own plate, 1824. Cylinder \(ab\), piston \(cd\), body \(A\) the furnace, body \(B\) the refrigerator. This is the mill as he drew it: not a waterwheel, a piston and two reservoirs. Vectorised from Réflexions sur la puissance motrice du feu (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).
Do not rush the \(100\)–to–\(50\) question. The square is waiting. He did not have entropy. Point at \(A\) and \(B\): furnace and refrigerator. The piston is the mill wheel.

The Mill, Drawn as a Rectangle

Replace height by temperature and mass of water by entropy, and the mill is already a rectangle.

The same cycle twice. Left: a water mill on height against mass of water. Fill the bucket at the top, let it descend, empty it, return the empty bucket. The water is not consumed; the work is \(g\,\Delta h\,\Delta m\). Right: a reversible heat engine on \(T\) against \(S\). The conserved quantity in the reversible model is what we now call entropy. The vertical brace is Carnot’s fall — the difference between the reservoirs — not the downward stroke of the working body.
  • Height \(h\) \(\leftrightarrow\) temperature \(T\)
  • Mass of water \(\Delta m\) \(\leftrightarrow\) entropy transferred \(\Delta S\)
  • Fill at the top \(\leftrightarrow\) take in entropy at \(T_h\)
  • Loaded bucket descends \(\leftrightarrow\) isolated expansion (the working body cools; no heat transfer)
  • Empty at the bottom \(\leftrightarrow\) reject entropy at \(T_c\)
  • Empty bucket returns \(\leftrightarrow\) isolated compression
  • Work \(g\,\Delta h\,\Delta m\) \(\leftrightarrow\) work \(\Delta T\times\Delta S\)
Point at the vertical brace on both panels. That is Carnot’s fall. Easy mistake: taking the downward adiabat for the fall. The downward stroke lowers the temperature of the working substance without exchanging entropy. The fall is the difference between the reservoirs, across the whole cycle.

The Square

The ideal Carnot cycle on a \(T\)–\(S\) diagram is a square.

As taught 27 October, after Helmholtz, answering a question. Click through the legs as on the visualiser. Temperature up, entropy across. Clockwise is an engine.

  • Expansion at \(T_h\): entropy increases, volume increases
  • Isolated cool: adiabatic expansion, entropy holds
  • Contraction at \(T_c\): entropy comes back down (Watt’s condenser)
  • Isolated heat: adiabatic compression, the square closes
  • Work is the area: \(W=\Delta T\times\Delta S\)

The Fall Is Not the Downward Stroke

Carnot’s fall is the difference between the reservoirs. The downward adiabat is the working body cooling with no heat transfer.

Two annotations that are easy to conflate. The vertical brace is Carnot’s fall of caloric: entropy is taken in at \(T_h\) and given up at \(T_c\). The right-hand leg is not that fall. It is an isolated expansion of the working body; no heat crosses, entropy holds, temperature drops.
One click. Brace versus right-hand leg. Then the \(160\)/\(40\) numbers if there is time: his example, our retrospective efficiency.

Four-Stroke, and the Smaller Loop

The four-stroke is the implementation. The square is the theory. Same \(\Delta T\), same \(\Delta S\): you cut two corners.

A real engine between the same reservoirs, with the same entropy swing, does not shrink the square. It still visits the top-right and the bottom-left. Heat is not taken in along the whole top, nor rejected along the whole bottom, so those two corners are cut off. The remaining area over the square is the efficiency.
Four-stroke is a courtesy to anyone who did engineering. Do not overfit the mapping. Do not shrink the square: that would be a different fall. Point at top-right and bottom-left. Same \(\Delta T\), same \(\Delta S\); two corners cut. Land on: the square is the ceiling; the remaining area is what you actually get.

The \(p\)-\(V\) Chart

Engineers often draw the same strokes on a pressure-volume chart. In that case it no longer looks like a square.

The same Carnot cycle on a \(p\)-\(V\) diagram. The hot and cold legs follow isotherms \(pV=\mathrm{const}\); the isolated cool and isolated heat follow adiabats, which are steeper. The enclosed area is still the work, \(\oint p\,\mathrm{d}V\), but you cannot read \(\Delta T\times\Delta S\) off it by eye.
One slide, in passing. Point at the isotherms. Then: this is why we drew \(T\)–\(S\). Back to Clausius if this was the Helmholtz question.

Gaussian: Expected Quadratic Energy

A Quadratic Energy

\[ E(x)=\frac{x^2}{2},\qquad p(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2\pi}}\,e^{-\beta x^2/2} \]

  • Week 1: Gaussian as \(p\propto e^{-E}\) with a parabolic well
  • Now: the same \(E\), in contact with a bath at \(T=1/\beta\)
  • \(p=\mathcal{N}(0,T)\) — variance is temperature (\(k_B=1\))
Callback to the height-Gaussian parabola from week 1. One quadratic degree of freedom, not a covariance model. Equipartition is the headline: \(U=T/2\).

Inner Energy is Equipartition

\[ U=\left\langle E\right\rangle=\left\langle\frac{x^2}{2}\right\rangle=\frac{T}{2} \]

  • \(\left\langle x^2\right\rangle=T\) because \(p=\mathcal{N}(0,T)\)
  • One quadratic degree of freedom holds \(k_BT/2\)
  • Maxwell’s kinetic energy \(\tfrac32 k_BT\) is three copies of this

Free Energy and Entropy, Closed Form

\[ F=-T\log Z=-\frac{T}{2}\log(2\pi T),\qquad S=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi T) \]

  • \(Z=\sqrt{2\pi T}\), so \(F\) is immediate
  • \(S=(U-F)/T\) recovers the Gaussian differential entropy
  • Check: \(F+TS=T/2=U\)

The Well, and the Expectation

The energy is the same parabola at every temperature. Raising \(T\) spreads \(p=\mathcal{N}(0,T)\) and the occupation climbs the walls: \(\left\langle E\right\rangle=T/2\) rises linearly.

The Decomposition versus \(T\)

Closed form for \(E(x)=x^2/2\) with \(k_B=1\). Inner energy \(U=T/2\) is the straight line; \(TS\) takes a growing cut; \(F=U-TS\) is negative and decreases.

\(d\) Quadratic Degrees of Freedom

\[ E(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d} x_i^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad U=\frac{d\,T}{2} \]

  • \(Z=(2\pi T)^{d/2}\), so \(F=-(d T/2)\log(2\pi T)\)
  • Independent coordinates: the one-dimensional split, \(d\) times
  • Maxwell’s three velocity components were this with \(d=3\)

The Air Thermometer?

Gay-Lussac’s vanishing volume is not this well. It is the same \(T\).

  • Three velocity components: \(U_{\mathrm{kin}}=\tfrac32 T\) per particle — done
  • A box, not a well: positions contribute a factor \(V\) to \(Z\), not another Gaussian
  • Helmholtz: \(\displaystyle P=-\bigl(\partial F/\partial V\bigr)_T = NT/V\)
Do not derive Sackur–Tetrode or Gibbs \(G\). Kinetic Gaussians are done. Name \(V\) in \(Z\), name the Helmholtz derivative, sit down. Charles’s law is \(PV=NT\) at fixed \(P\).
