The Information Game

The Information Game

One player has chosen an object from a known finite list. The other may ask only yes/no questions.

  • Goal: name the object
  • Cost: number of questions (worst case, or on average)
  • Optimal play halves the remaining possibilities each time
Run this live if time allows: pick a small list (sixteen animals, eight Cambridge colleges). Students should feel that a balanced question buys one bit. Do not yet write \(-\sum p\log p\); the counting argument is enough.

Bits as Balanced Questions

A fair yes/no answer distinguishes two equally likely cases — one bit.

  • \(N\) equally likely objects \(\Rightarrow\) need \(\log_2 N\) bits
  • Twenty questions reach about a million hypotheses (\(2^{20}\))
  • Unequal priors: best questions still split probability as evenly as possible

The same idea, with a different instrument, comes next — MacKay’s balance.

MacKay’s Balance and Balls

Twelve Balls, One Odd

MacKay’s weighing problem (MacKay, 2003, p. Ex.~4.1): twelve balls; exactly one is odd; it may be heavier or lighter.

A two-pan balance and twelve identical balls.
  • Instrument: a two-pan balance
  • Task: identify the odd ball and whether it is heavy or light
State the puzzle. Do not list the readings of the balance, and do not walk through which balls go on which pan.

How Should We Proceed?

We have a finite list of hypotheses and a physical instrument. How should we think?

  • What, exactly, do we need to name?
  • What would count as a useful use of the balance?
  • When would we know we were done?
Leave this live. Do not say left, right, or level, and do not say three. After they have named the hypotheses, try the two-outcome story next — as if this were the information game again.

Information at the Start

Maths. Before any weighing, how many hypotheses?

  • 12 choices for which ball is odd
  • \(\times\) 2 directions (heavy or light)
  • \(= 24\) equally likely states of the world

Try Two Outcomes

Let’s Try Two Outcomes

Treat the balance as the information game: left heavy or right heavy only.

  • Two readings \(\Rightarrow\) at most \(\log_2 2\) bits per use
  • Mechanism next: what we actually put on the pans
  • Maths stays on its own slides — the two accounts can disagree
Say ``log two bits’’ — one bit if the two readings are equally likely. Do not yet mention a third reading. Board 6 vs 6 as the move people reach for from twenty questions.

Maths: Two Readings

Maths. Start from \(\log_2 24\). Each use returns at most \(\log_2 2\).

\[ \log_2 24 \approx 4.58,\qquad 2^4 = 16 < 24 \le 32 = 2^5 \]

  • Four uses cannot name 24 hypotheses
  • Five uses can, if each use really returns one bit
  • That ``if’’ is mechanism, not maths

Mechanism: 6 vs 6

Mechanism. Put six against six.

  • The pans will tip — the odd ball is on one side
  • Follow the down pan, as if the odd ball were heavier
  • Six balls remain under that story
Ask what we actually learned. Under ``it is heavier’’, 12 balls become 6, one bit toward which ball. We do not know the direction. The down pan is six maybe-heavies; the up pan is six maybe-lights. All twelve balls are still in play. Maths next.

Maths: What 6 vs 6 Bought

Maths. Score the same physical act on the 24 hypotheses.

  • 24 \(\to\) 12 remaining hypotheses: exactly \(\log_2 2\) bits
  • Zero balls eliminated — each ball is still possibly odd
  • The bit went into a coupling of group and direction

Mechanism: 3 vs 3

Mechanism. Keep going as a binary search of the balls: three against three.

  • Under the heavier-side story, three balls remain
  • Same move, smaller pile
  • We are still not asking about light versus heavy

Mechanism: Three Left

Mechanism. Three balls remain. We cannot put one and a half on a pan.

  • 2 vs 2 needs a fourth ball
  • Borrow one of known normal mass
  • Then 1 vs 1 on whatever the 2 vs 2 leaves
Make them say the known ball out loud. Ask: how many bits do we get from 2 vs 2 now? Do not calculate before they see that the split of three remaining balls cannot be even.

Maths: The Known Ball Taxes the Bit

Maths. Three remaining heavier-suspects; pans are 2 vs 1+known.

\[ \left(\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{1}{3}\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad H = h_2\!\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right) \approx 0.92 < 1 \text{ bit} \]

  • The even split was \(\log_2 2\); the pans cannot make it
  • Padding with a known ball is a mechanism patch
  • Information gain falls because the probabilities are no longer equal

Mechanism: 1 vs 1

Mechanism. If two suspects remain, weigh them against each other.

  • Now the split can be even
  • Under the heavier story, this names the ball
  • We still have not asked light versus heavy

Mechanism: Lighter or Heavier?

Mechanism. We have a candidate ball. Can we name the direction?

  • Weigh the candidate against a known normal
  • Two outcomes name heavy versus light — if it tips
  • If it balances, our heavier-side story was wrong
This is the crack in the two-outcome attempt. A known-normal comparison is the natural test for direction, and it keeps offering a third reading: level. The instrument was never a yes/no question. Pause, then the aha slide.

Two Accounts, One Experiment

Keep the columns separate.

  • Maths: \(\log_2 24\) to close; at most \(\log_2 2\) per two-outcome use
  • Mechanism: 6 vs 6, 3 vs 3, 2 vs 2 with a known, 1 vs 1, then direction
  • The known ball, and the unused ``level’’ reading, are mechanism — they change the gain

Three Readings

Aha: It Balances Three Ways

The pans also sit level. Left heavy, right heavy, or balance.

  • The instrument was never a yes/no question
  • 6 vs 6 made the third reading impossible
  • That was a mechanism choice that threw away a symbol
Let them say the three readings. Then go straight to the new budget. Do not start the 4 vs 4 tree.

Maths: Three Readings

Maths. Each use now returns at most \(\log_2 3\).

\[ \log_2 3 \approx 1.58,\qquad 3^2 = 9 < 24 \le 27 = 3^3 \]

  • Two uses cannot name 24 hypotheses
  • Three uses can, if each use really returns \(\log_2 3\)
  • Same ``if’’ as before: mechanism must realise the split

Mechanism Must Collect \(\log_2 3\)

Mechanism. To earn \(\log_2 3\), all three readings must be possible and near equal.

  • First weighing: 4 vs 4, not 6 vs 6 — so level can happen
  • A known ball, or emptying a reading, is again a tax
  • Optimal play keeps the three readings as near equiprobable as possible

Doable in Three — Strategy Deferred

The counting argument shows three weighings must be enough in principle.

  • We do not give the sequence of groupings today
  • Info gained and mechanism are separate ledgers — score both
  • Same moral as twenty questions: split the prior evenly, now three ways

Coarse-Graining a Die

A fair die has \(H(X)=\log_2 6\approx 2.58\) bits.

Stage the question: does every yes/no cost one bit?
  • First cut: odd or even?
  • Second cut: is it a six?
  • Same total either way — different prices for the first answer
Board both partitions. Odd/even is balanced so exactly 1 bit. ``Is it six?’’ is not one bit — students often assume every binary question buys a bit. That is the MacKay moral applied to grouping.

Odd or Even: Exactly One Bit

Groups \(\{1,3,5\}\) and \(\{2,4,6\}\) are equiprobable.

\[ H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})=1 \text{ bit}, \qquad H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}})=\log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits} \]

  • Group label: fair coin — one bit
  • Inside either group: three equally likely faces
  • Total: \(1+\log_2 3=\log_2 6\)

“Is It a Six?”: Not One Bit

A yes/no question is one bit only when the two answers are equiprobable.

\[ w=\Bigl(\tfrac16,\tfrac56\Bigr), \qquad H(G_{\mathrm{six}})=h_2\Bigl(\tfrac16\Bigr)\approx 0.65 \text{ bits} \]

  • Yes (\(X=6\)): no residual uncertainty
  • No: five faces left, \(H=\log_2 5\)
  • Remaining: \(\tfrac56\log_2 5\approx 1.93\) bits
  • Total again \(\approx 0.65+1.93=2.58=\log_2 6\)

Same Total, Different First Cut

However you slice the questions, you pay \(\log_2 6\) in total.

  • Odd/even first: pay \(1\), then \(\log_2 3\)
  • Six-or-not first: pay \(\approx 0.65\), then \(\approx 1.93\)
  • Coarse-graining keeps only the group term and throws away the rest

Grouping Composition

Uncertainty splits into stages: which group, then where inside the group.

\[ H(X)=H(w)+\sum_i w_i\, H(p_i) \]

  • \(w_i\): probability of group \(i\)
  • \(p_i\): distribution inside group \(i\)
  • First term: cost of the coarse label; second: average residual detail
This is the chain rule for a deterministic grouping \(G=g(X)\): \(H(X)=H(G)+H(X\mid G)\). Board that identification. Khinchin pointer only — consistency under staged questions characterises Shannon \(H\); no proof today.

Reverse Direction

Learning the face determines the group for free. What is left to find after the group?

  • \(H(G\mid X)=0\): the coarse label is redundant given \(X\)
  • Information extracted by the group: \(I(X;G)=H(G)\)
  • Information still to find: \(H(X\mid G)\)

Why the Total Is Invariant

Entropy is the uncertainty that is consistent no matter how you decompose a question into stages.

  • Any partition: \(H(w)+\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) returns the same \(H(X)\)
  • That consistency is special to the Shannon form (Khinchin; later Baez–Fritz–Leinster)
  • Pointer only today — week 3 derives \(H\) from axioms

Coarse-Graining as Abstraction

Bernoulli already coarse-grained. Intelligence does it constantly.

  • Kinetic theory: keep pressure; discard each molecule’s curve
  • Thermodynamics: keep \(T,V,P\); discard which microstate
  • Perception / ML: pixels \(\to\) features \(\to\) concepts
Callback to the early ensemble block and to Jaynes’ anthropomorphic entropy below. One slide only — the die gave the accounting; this is the moral.

Bridge Back to the Ensemble

Macrostate \(=\) group. Microstate \(=\) face inside the group.

  • Information lost by the macro description: \(\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\)
  • That is what thermodynamic entropy is counting
  • Same bookkeeping from atoms to gas, or pixels to concepts

Static Bookkeeping Versus a Physical Ensemble

The grouping identity needs no dynamics. Reading \(p\) as an ensemble does.

  • Die identity: true for any \(p\) and any partition, at one instant
  • Physical ensemble: stationarity (and usually ergodicity) so averages match long-run time
  • Equilibrium: each group has settled; only the weights \(w_i\) can still move

Before Boltzmann: Heat Engines

  • Carnot (1824): no real engine beats a reversible cycle between two baths
  • Clausius (1850s): heat cannot flow from cold to hot without work
  • Clausius (1865): names entropy — the state’s transformation content
Carnot is the efficiency question; Clausius is the second law and the word entropy. Perpetual motion fails because it tries to evade that toll.

Clausius and the No-Go

Clausius: the entropy of the universe tends to a maximum.

  • You cannot run a cyclic engine that converts heat entirely into work
  • Similar for perpetual motion — Clausius makes the prohibition explicit
  • Prescription follows separately: Boltzmann weights, then Shannon/Jaynes
  • Macroscopic: Carnot \(\to\) Clausius (second law, entropy named)
  • Microscopic: Maxwell, Boltzmann, Gibbs (same \(S\), counted states)
  • Information: Shannon, Jaynes (same \(H\), different job)

The Gibbs–Boltzmann Distribution

Gibbs–Boltzmann Occupation

Same formula, three names: Boltzmann weights, Gibbs distribution, canonical ensemble.

  • \(p_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z\), \(\quad Z(\beta)=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}\)
  • \(\beta=1/k_B T\): coldness; \(T\): bath temperature
  • \(\log Z\) is the cumulant generating function of the energy
Introduce and use, do not derive. Board the formula. MaxEnt with fixed mean energy recovers the same \(p_i\).
Occupation of a two-state system as coldness increases. At low \(\beta\) both states are populated; at high \(\beta\) the ground state dominates.

  • Boltzmann machine (1985): make those couplings learnable
  • Pairwise energy: \(E(\mathbf{s})=-\sum_i b_i s_i-\sum_{i<j}W_{ij}s_i s_j\)

From Loss Landscapes to Hamiltonians

Walking Down an Energy

Gradient descent: ‘walking downhill’

  • Contours of \(E(\mathbf{ w})\) = elevation lines on a hill
  • Steepest descent: step opposite \(\nabla E\)
  • Same \(E\) as least squares / negative log-likelihood
Contours of the least-squares energy \(E(m,c)=\sum_n(y_n-mx_n-c)^2\). Steepest descent walks toward the bowl.

Potential Energy

The loss is a potential energy \(V(\mathbf{q})\) on parameter space.

  • Configuration \(\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{ w}\): where the system sits
  • Force \(\propto -\nabla V\): direction of steepest descent
  • Common analogy: ball in a bowl; GD is the overdamped limit

In Practice: Stochastic

SGD estimates \(\nabla V\) from a random mini-batch

  • Full-batch \(\nabla V\): exact force, expensive
  • SGD: noisy force; cheap; enables online updates
  • Noise can help escape shallow traps (less relevant for convex bowls)

Where Is the Kinetic Energy?

Where is the kinetic energy?

  • GD / SGD: overdamped—friction, no momentum inventory
  • Momentum methods (heavy ball, Adam): heuristic inertia
  • Physics: kinetic energy \(T(\mathbf{p})\) lives on momentum \(\mathbf{p}\)

The Hamiltonian

\[ H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p}) = T(\mathbf{p}) + V(\mathbf{q}) \]

  • Total energy = kinetic + potential
  • Hamilton: \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\partial H/\partial\mathbf{p}\), \(\dot{\mathbf{p}}=-\partial H/\partial\mathbf{q}\)
  • \(H\) conserved along the flow (ideal, frictionless case)

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

HMC: invent momentum, simulate Hamiltonian dynamics, accept/reject.

  • Draw \(\mathbf{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,M)\); integrate Hamilton’s equations for a short fictitious time
  • Propose the endpoint \((\mathbf{q}',\mathbf{p}')\); Metropolis test on \(H\)
  • Marginal samples of \(\mathbf{q}\) target \(p(\mathbf{q})\propto e^{-V(\mathbf{q})}\)

Why Stay on a Contour of \(H\)?

Moving along a contour of \(H\) costs nothing.

  • Target on \((\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\): \(\pi\propto e^{-H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})}\)
  • Along a contour \(H\) is constant, so \(\pi\) is constant
  • Metropolis accepts with \(\min(1,e^{-\Delta H})\): \(\Delta H\approx 0\) means accept

Contours Are Not Contours of \(V\)

Staying on a level of \(H\) is not staying at a level of \(V\).

  • \(H=V(\mathbf{q})+T(\mathbf{p})\): kinetic energy converts to potential and back
  • Up the side of the bowl, \(\mathbf{p}\) shrinks; back down, it grows
  • Fresh \(\mathbf{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,M)\) jumps to a new level of \(H\)
Unit harmonic oscillator, \(H=(q^2+p^2)/2\). Circles are levels of \(H\). The solid arc rides one level (Hamiltonian flow). The dashed jump at fixed \(q\) is a momentum refresh onto a new level.
Solid = free trip around a contour. Dashed = Gibbs update of \(p\), which changes \(H\) and leaves \(q\) alone.

Why Not Just Descend?

Descent crosses contours of \(V\). Sampling must not.

  • Descent goes toward the mode: great for a point estimate, wrong for a distribution
  • Each descent step changes \(V\) a lot, so \(\pi\) changes a lot, so it gets rejected
  • High dimensions: typical samples sit away from the mode
Density of \(\|q\|^2/d\) for \(q\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d)\). Mean is \(1\) for every \(d\); the mass tightens around that value. Typical samples sit in a shell, not at the mode.
Point at \(d=50\): almost all the mass is near \(\|q\|^2=d\). The mode of the Gaussian is still the origin. Descent heads for a point that is not where samples live.

Why Leapfrog?

We now know we want to follow a contour of \(H\). The computer cannot follow a curve, it takes steps.

  • A descent-shaped step walks off the level: \(H\) grows, and Metropolis fights the drift
  • The discrete map must be reversible and volume-preserving, or the accept/reject is no longer a small correction
The contour argument is done. This slide is the discrete-step problem. Then Euler leaving the circle, then leapfrog.

Euler Leaves the Orbit

Forward Euler on a quadratic well: each step is a shear, the circle inflates.

  • \(q\leftarrow q+\varepsilon p\), \(p\leftarrow p-\varepsilon q\)
  • \(H\) grows at every step
  • Metropolis on \(\Delta H\) then rejects almost everything

Leapfrog Stays Near It

Leapfrog: half-step \(\mathbf{p}\), full-step \(\mathbf{q}\), half-step \(\mathbf{p}\).

  • Reversible: flip \(\mathbf{p}\), replay, flip \(\mathbf{p}\)
  • Each substep has \(\det J=1\) — a cloud of states keeps its area
  • \(H\) wobbles without a secular drift, so accept rates stay high
Do not derive the symplectic form. The figure is the argument: Euler walks off the circle, leapfrog stays. Name reversible + volume-preserving; stop.
Harmonic oscillator \(H=(q^2+p^2)/2\), forty steps of size \(0.3\) from \((1,0)\). The exact motion is the dashed circle. Euler inflates \(H\); leapfrog stays near the level set.

The Algorithm: Hop, Ride, Correct

Each iteration: hop to a new level, ride around it, correct.

  1. Hop: draw \(\mathbf{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,M)\) (changes \(H\), not \(\mathbf{q}\))
  2. Ride: \(L\) leapfrog steps from \((\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\) to \((\mathbf{q}',\mathbf{p}')\)
  3. Correct: accept with probability \(\min(1,e^{-\Delta H})\), else stay at \(\mathbf{q}\)
Say it as three verbs: hop, ride, correct. Then point at the trajectory figure: the curves are step 2, the markers are what survives step 3.
Hop, ride, correct on \(H=(q^2+p^2)/2\). Dashed verticals: momentum refresh (hop). Marked polylines: leapfrog (ride). Large markers: accepted endpoints (correct).

Trajectories Propose; Endpoints Sample

Curves are step 2; markers are what survives step 3.

  • The leapfrog path builds one proposal — waypoints are discarded
  • Only the accepted endpoint enters the chain
  • Next iteration hops again: fresh \(\mathbf{p}\), new level of \(H\)
Point at the quadratic figure: curves = step 2; markers = step 3. The three circles were the same loop in \((q,p)\).

Shannon asked: what number measures uncertainty in a discrete distribution \(p=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\)?

The Partition Function

\(Z\) as Generating Function

The canonical ensemble from the bath: fix \(\beta\) and let the small system fluctuate.

  • \(Z(\beta) = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}\)
  • \(U = -\partial_\beta \log Z\), \(F = -\beta^{-1}\log Z\), \(S = \beta(U-F)\)
  • Equilibrium = on the \(\beta\)-manifold; finite-time driving leaves it

Discrete Shannon Entropy Is Bounded

Week 3 derived Shannon entropy for discrete \(p=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\).

  • \(0 \le H(p) \le \log n\) on \(n\) outcomes
  • Maximum at uniform; zero on a delta
  • Code interpretation: average length cannot beat \(H\)

KL Divergence

Comparing two distributions needs a functional that is always sensible.

  • \(\text{KL}\left( p\,\|\,q \right) = \sum_i p_i \log(p_i/q_i)\) discrete; \(\int p\log(p/q)\,dx\) continuous
  • Always \(\text{KL}\left( p\,\|\,q \right) \ge 0\); zero iff \(p = q\) (same support)
  • Extra surprise when \(q\) stands in for \(p\) — not a metric, but a directed cost
LO8 setup. Use \(\text{KL}\left( p\,\|\,q \right)\) throughout. Physics synonym: relative entropy. Board: \(\text{KL}\left( p\,\|\,q \right)\neq\text{KL}\left( q\,\|\,p \right)\). Jaynes die week 5 minimised \(\text{KL}\left( p\,\|\,r \right)\) without naming it. Week 7: \(m\)-projection.

Maxwell–Boltzmann: Components Versus Speed

Each Cartesian velocity component is Gaussian. Speed \(v=|\mathbf{v}|\) is not.

  • Component: \(f(v_x)=\sqrt{m/(2\pi kT)}\,e^{-mv_x^2/2kT}\)
  • Speed: integrate the isotropic 3D Gaussian over angles
  • Jacobian \(d^3v = v^2\,dv\,d\Omega\) supplies the \(v^2\) factor
Both statements are Maxwell–Boltzmann. The histogram of one component is Gaussian; the histogram of speed peaks away from zero. Board the spherical shell volume briefly.

Same Physics, Different Variable

\[ f(v_x)=\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{2\pi kT}}\,e^{-mv_x^2/2kT} \qquad\text{versus}\qquad f(v)\propto v^2\,e^{-mv^2/2kT} \]

  • The \(v^2\) is not an energy correction — it is geometry
  • Continuous entropy will shift under this map (next)

How Differential Entropy Transforms

Continuous entropy is not invariant under reparametrisation.

\[ h(Y)=h(X)+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\ln|g'(X)|\bigr] \]

  • Linear rescaling: \(h(aX)=h(X)+\ln|a|\)
  • Changing units (m/s \(\to\) km/s) changes \(h\)
  • \(h\) can be negative (narrow Gaussian) — not Shannon uncertainty
Board the one-line derivation. Emphasise: \(\ln f\) takes the log of a density with units, so something is missing. Week 5: MaxEnt ``Gaussian maximises entropy for fixed variance’’ is relative to the chosen coordinate.

Discrete Entropy from Binning

Discrete \(H\) is well defined. Differential \(h\) is what remains after subtracting \(\ln\Delta\).

  • Bin \(X\) with width \(\Delta\): \(p_i\approx f(x_i)\Delta\)
  • \(H(X^\Delta)\approx h(X)-\ln\Delta\)
  • As \(\Delta\to 0\), discrete \(H\) diverges; \(h\) is the finite part
Board \(p_i\approx f\Delta\) for one bin. The \(-\ln\Delta\) is the price of resolving more finely. Relabelling with \(y=g(x)\) while keeping uniform bins in \(y\) changes the partition in \(x\).

Phase Space Needs a Cell Size

Classical statistical mechanics faces the same issue: \(S\) needs a cell volume in phase space.

  • Cell size \(h^{3N}\) makes dimensionless occupation numbers
  • Liouville: canonical maps have unit Jacobian — phase-space entropy is invariant under them
  • A generic change of variables is not canonical

KL Fixes the Coordinate Dependence

KL divergence is invariant under invertible reparametrisation. Differential entropy is not.

\[ \text{KL}\left( f_Y\,\|\,g_Y \right)=\text{KL}\left( f_X\,\|\,g_X \right) \]

  • Both densities transform by the same Jacobian — it cancels
  • Discrete KL converges; differential entropy does not
  • Mutual information is likewise reparametrisation-invariant

Differential Entropy Is KL Against \(m\)

Ordinary \(h\) hides a reference density \(m\) — usually ``Lebesgue in the coordinate we chose’’.

\[ h_m(f)=-\int f\ln\frac{f}{m}\,dx=-\text{KL}\left( f\,\|\,m \right) \]

  • Taking \(m\) uniform in \(x\) recovers ordinary \(h\)
  • Jaynes: limiting density of discrete points — the bin width promoted to a density
  • Change coordinates \(\Leftrightarrow\) change the undeclared \(m\)
This is the hinge for week 5 MaxEnt: maximising \(h\) is maximising \(-\text{KL}\left( f\,\|\,m \right)\) for a forgotten \(m\). Name Jaynes’ limiting density of discrete points; do not digress into measure theory.

Local Equilibrium as Grouping

Equilibrium is the special case where within-group dynamics is fast.

\[ S(t)=H\bigl(w(t)\bigr)+\sum_i w_i(t)\, H(\pi_i) \]

  • Groups \(=\) macrostates; \(\pi_i=\) conditional equilibrium inside group \(i\)
  • Timescale separation: \(p_i\) relaxes quickly to \(\pi_i\); \(w_i(t)\) evolves slowly
  • Thermodynamic entropy at fixed macrostate is the within-group term
Answers ``equilibrium versus non-equilibrium? (first cut)’’ from the define list. Non-eq: either \(p_i\neq\pi_i\) still relaxing, or \(w(t)\) driven. No proof of Markov mixing.

Chain Rule, Mutual Information, Capacity

Four results we will need later — stated, not proved today.

  • Chain rule: \(H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X)\)
  • Mutual information: \(I(X;Y) = H(X)-H(X|Y) = H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)\)
  • Capacity: no rate above \(C\); achieving \(C\) requires the capacity-achieving \(p(x)\)
  • Data processing: if \(X\to Y\to Z\) is Markov, \(I(X;Z)\le I(X;Y)\)
