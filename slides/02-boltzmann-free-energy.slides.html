From Daniel Bernoulli: Week 1 Carry-Over

Laplace’s Molecule: an Anachronism?

Last week Laplace spoke of ``a simple molecule of air.’’ That sounds odd once you meet Boltzmann.

  • Laplace, Essai (1814): the curve of a molecule is as determined as a planet’s
  • Boltzmann dies in 1906 still fighting atomists’ opponents
  • Atoms / molecules were not settled science in the nineteenth century
Callback to lecture 1 quote. Do not resolve yet — ask why Laplace can talk this way a century before Perrin. Answer is Bernoulli’s kinetic tradition, not modern chemistry.

``Molecule’’ Before Chemistry Won

Laplace is not citing Dalton. He is speaking Newton’s corpuscular language.

  • French molécule: a small particle of matter, not yet the chemist’s molecule
  • The kinetic idea is older than the chemical proof of atoms
  • The gremlin is ignorance of that particle’s path, not a claim that atoms were established

Hydrodynamica

Daniel Bernoulli’s Hydrodynamica published in 1738. It was one of the first works to use the idea of conservation of energy. It used Newton’s laws to predict the behaviour of gases.

Daniel Bernoulli’s chapter on the kinetic theory of gases, for a review on the context of this chapter see Mikhailov (n.d.). For 1738 this is extraordinary thinking. The notion of kinetic theory of gases wouldn’t become fully accepted in Physics until 1908 when a model of Einstein’s was verified by Jean Baptiste Perrin.

What Bernoulli Assumes

Hydrodynamica (1738) already treats a gas as many tiny bodies in motion.

  • Many corpuscles, small compared with the vessel
  • Motion is swift and disordered; collisions transfer momentum
  • Pressure on a wall is the rain of impacts, not a static push
  • Idealisation: elastic billiard-like encounters, no lingering forces
Board the assumptions as modelling choices. The billiards canvas makes them literal. Flag what is not assumed: chemical identity of atoms, quantum mechanics, or a finished thermodynamic entropy.

Assumptions Are Already Coarse

Bernoulli does not track Laplace’s curve for every particle — he summarises a swarm.

  • Individual trajectories are discarded
  • What remains is a mechanical story for pressure
  • Next: make the discarded detail a probability — the ensemble

From Paths to an Ensemble

Laplace’s gremlin says we cannot follow every curve. The ensemble is how we live with that.

  • Microstate: positions and velocities of all particles (Laplace’s curves)
  • Macrostate: a few numbers — \(N\), \(V\), \(E\) or \(T\), \(P\)
  • Ensemble: a probability over the microstates compatible with the macrostate
Name coarse-graining without measure theory. Link: billiard histogram bins are a toy coarse-graining; \(T,V,P\) are the physicist’s. Gibbs distribution later today is one ensemble (canonical). Do not derive MaxEnt.

Coarse-Graining Is a Choice of Description

Temperature and pressure are sufficient statistics of the swarm — not decorations on the truth.

  • Histogram bins: keep some velocity detail, discard the rest
  • Thermodynamics: keep \(T\), \(V\), \(P\); discard which molecule was which
  • The uncertainty we write as entropy lives at the chosen grain

Bridge to Maxwell, Boltzmann, Gibbs

They turn Bernoulli’s swarm and Laplace’s ignorance into a calculus.

  • Maxwell: velocity statistics of the ideal gas
  • Boltzmann: \(S\) linked to probability over microstates
  • Gibbs: ensembles as the systematic language

James Clerk Maxwell (1831-1879), Ludwig Boltzmann (1844-1906) Josiah Willard Gibbs (1839-1903)

Boltzmann’s paper Boltzmann (n.d.) which introduced the relationship between entropy and probability. A translation with notes is available in Sharp and Matschinsky (2015).

Entropy Review

The Information Game

The Information Game

One player has chosen an object from a known finite list. The other may ask only yes/no questions.

  • Goal: name the object
  • Cost: number of questions (worst case, or on average)
  • Optimal play halves the remaining possibilities each time
Run this live if time allows: pick a small list (sixteen animals, eight Cambridge colleges). Students should feel that a balanced question buys one bit. Do not yet write \(-\sum p\log p\); the counting argument is enough.

Bits as Balanced Questions

A fair yes/no answer distinguishes two equally likely cases — one bit.

  • \(N\) equally likely objects \(\Rightarrow\) need \(\log_2 N\) bits
  • Twenty questions reach about a million hypotheses (\(2^{20}\))
  • Unequal priors: best questions still split probability as evenly as possible

The same idea with a three-outcome instrument comes next — MacKay’s balance.

MacKay’s Balance and Balls

Twelve Balls, One Odd

MacKay’s weighing problem (MacKay, 2003, p. Ex.~4.1): twelve balls; exactly one is odd; it may be heavier or lighter.

A two-pan balance and twelve identical balls. Each use of the balance returns one of three readings: left heavy, balance, or right heavy.
  • Instrument: a two-pan balance
  • Each use: left heavy, right heavy, or balance — three outcomes
  • Task: identify the odd ball and whether it is heavy or light
State the puzzle. Do not walk through which balls go on which pan. The lecture point is the information budget, not the adaptive strategy tree.

Information at the Start

Before any weighing, how many hypotheses?

  • 12 choices for which ball is odd
  • \(\times\) 2 directions (heavy or light)
  • \(= 24\) equally likely states of the world

Information from the Balance

Each weighing has three possible readings.

  • Ideal (equiprobable) outcomes: \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\) bits per use
  • Two weighings: at most \(3^2 = 9\) leaves — too few for \(24\) hypotheses
  • Three weighings: at most \(3^3 = 27\) leaves — enough, since \(27 \ge 24\)

Doable in Three — Strategy Deferred

The counting argument shows three weighings must be enough in principle.

  • We do not give the sequence of groupings today
  • Optimal play keeps the three readings as near equiprobable as possible
  • Same moral as twenty questions: ask questions that split the prior evenly
Stop here. Invite them to try Exercise 4.1 before reading MacKay’s solution figure. Optional aside: a first weighing of 1–6 vs 7–12 can never balance under the problem statement, so it yields only one bit and is already suboptimal.

Computing Expectations Example

  • Consider the following distribution.
\(y\) 1 2 3 4
\(P\left(y\right)\) 0.3 0.2 0.1 0.4
  • What is the mean of the distribution?
  • What is the standard deviation of the distribution?
  • Are the mean and standard deviation representative of the distribution form?
  • What is the expected value of \(-\log P(y)\)?

Expectations Example: Answer

  • We are given:
\(y\) 1 2 3 4
\(P\left(y\right)\) 0.3 0.2 0.1 0.4
\(y^2\) 1 4 9 16
\(-\log(P(y))\) 1.204 1.609 2.302 0.916
  • Mean: \(1\times 0.3 + 2\times 0.2 + 3 \times 0.1 + 4 \times 0.4 = 2.6\)
  • Second moment: \(1 \times 0.3 + 4 \times 0.2 + 9 \times 0.1 + 16 \times 0.4 = 8.4\)
  • Variance: \(8.4 - 2.6\times 2.6 = 1.64\)
  • Standard deviation: \(\sqrt{1.64} = 1.2806\)

Expectations Example: Answer II

  • We are given that:
\(y\) 1 2 3 4
\(P\left(y\right)\) 0.3 0.2 0.1 0.4
\(y^2\) 1 4 9 16
\(-\log(P(y))\) 1.204 1.609 2.302 0.916
  • Expectation \(-\log(P(y))\): \(0.3\times 1.204 + 0.2\times 1.609 + 0.1\times 2.302 +0.4\times 0.916 = 1.280\)

Uncertainty as a Number

Shannon entropy turns a distribution into a scalar measure of uncertainty — the average number of fair yes/no questions under optimal play.

  • Discrete: \(H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i\)
  • Base 2: bits; natural log: nats
  • Fair coin: \(H=1\) bit; certain outcome: \(H=0\)

Bernoulli and Binomial

  • Fair coin: \(H=1\) bit

Bernoulli Entropy

Bernoulli Entropy

Bernoulli(\(p=0.3\)). Left: the distribution. Middle: surprisal \(-\log_2 P(x)\). Right: the summands \(-P(x)\log_2 P(x)\); their total is the entropy \(H\).

Bernoulli Entropy

  • Fair coin \(p=\tfrac12\): \(H=1\) bit — maximal for a binary variable
  • As \(p\to 0\) or \(1\): \(H\to 0\) — the outcome is nearly certain
  • Closed form: \(H=-p\log_2 p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p)\)

Binomial Entropy

Binomial Entropy

Binomial(\(n=12\), \(p=0.4\)). The right-hand panel sums to \(H\); tails contribute little because \(P(k)\) is small even when surprisal is large.

Binomial Entropy

  • Same visual grammar as Bernoulli: \(P\), \(-\log_2 P\), \(-P\log_2 P\)
  • Surprisal is largest in the tails; weight \(P(k)\) suppresses them in \(H\)
  • Large-\(n\) approximation: \(H\approx\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\, np(1-p))\) bits

Poisson and Multinomial

  • Poisson(\(\lambda\)): numerical sum over \(k\); \(\approx\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\lambda)\) for large \(\lambda\)
  • Categorical / one multinomial trial: \(H=-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k\)
  • Uniform on \(K\) symbols: \(H=\log_2 K\)

Poisson Entropy

Poisson Entropy

Poisson(\(\lambda=4\)). Mid: surprisal rises in the tails. Right: the probability weight cuts those tails back, so \(H\) is dominated by the bulk.

Poisson Entropy

  • Count distributions: same three-panel reading as Bernoulli / binomial
  • Large-\(\lambda\) approximation: \(H\approx\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\lambda)\) bits
  • Entropy grows only logarithmically with the rate

Multinomial / Categorical Entropy

Categorical Entropy

The four-outcome distribution from the expectation example, now in bits. Right-hand summands total \(H\); category \(y=3\) is rarest (highest surprisal) but contributes modestly because \(P(3)\) is small.

Categorical Entropy

  • One multinomial trial \(=\) categorical: \(H=-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k\)
  • Uniform on \(K\) symbols: \(H=\log_2 K\) — maximal
  • \(n\) i.i.d. trials: entropies add; the count vector is more subtle

Gaussian

  • Differential entropy \(h=-\int p\log_2 p\,\mathrm{d}y\)
  • Univariate Gaussian: \(h=\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2)\) bits
  • Can be negative; not the same object as discrete \(H\)

Gaussian Differential Entropy

Gaussian Differential Entropy

Univariate Gaussian (height example). Middle: \(-\log_2 p(y)\); dips below zero where the density exceeds \(1\). Right: the integrand whose integral is differential entropy \(h\).

Gaussian Differential Entropy

  • Closed form: \(h=\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2)\) bits
  • Grows with \(\sigma\); independent of \(\mu\)
  • Differential entropy \(\neq\) discrete Shannon \(H\) — can be negative
Flag the continuous caveat once. Do not dwell; the three-panel analogy is the point. Discrete \(H\ge 0\); differential \(h\) is a relative measure.

Entropy: physical and anthropomorphic

  • Thermodynamic \(S\) and Shannon \(H\): same form; in equilibrium \(S = k_B H\)

Joint, Conditional, Chain Rule (Names Only)

  • \(H(X,Y)\) joint uncertainty
  • \(H(X\mid Y)\) residual uncertainty after observing \(Y\)
  • Chain rule: \(H(X,Y)=H(X\mid Y) + H(Y)\)

ML to Thermodynamics

Carnot and Clausius

Before Boltzmann: Heat Engines

  • Carnot (1824): no real engine beats a reversible cycle between two baths
  • Clausius (1850s): heat cannot flow from cold to hot without work
  • Clausius (1865): names entropy — the state’s transformation content
Carnot is the efficiency question; Clausius is the second law and the word entropy. Perpetual motion fails because it tries to evade that toll.

Clausius and the No-Go

Clausius: the entropy of the universe tends to a maximum.

  • You cannot run a cyclic engine that converts heat entirely into work
  • Similar for perpetual motion — Clausius makes the prohibition explicit
  • Prescription follows separately: Boltzmann weights, then Shannon/Jaynes
  • Macroscopic: Carnot \(\to\) Clausius (second law, entropy named)
  • Microscopic: Maxwell, Boltzmann, Gibbs (same \(S\), counted states)
  • Information: Shannon, Jaynes (same \(H\), different job)

The Gibbs–Boltzmann Distribution

Gibbs–Boltzmann Occupation

Same formula, three names: Boltzmann weights, Gibbs distribution, canonical ensemble.

  • \(p_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z\), \(\quad Z(\beta)=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}\)
  • \(\beta=1/k_B T\): coldness; \(T\): bath temperature
  • \(\log Z\) is the cumulant generating function of the energy
Introduce and use, do not derive. Board the formula. MaxEnt with fixed mean energy recovers the same \(p_i\).
Occupation of a two-state system as coldness increases. At low \(\beta\) both states are populated; at high \(\beta\) the ground state dominates.

Aside: Hopfield, Boltzmann Machines, Nobel 2024

The same energy-and-occupation grammar reappears in associative memory and early deep learning.

  • Hopfield (1982): fixed-weight energy; recall as settling
  • Boltzmann machine (1985): make those couplings learnable
  • Pairwise energy: \(E(\mathbf{s})=-\sum_i b_i s_i-\sum_{i<j}W_{ij}s_i s_j\)
Colour only. Nobel 2024 (Hopfield and Hinton) names the line. Do not divert into training algorithms or Landauer here.

Coldness and Temperature

  • \(T\): the bath, the thermometer
  • \(\beta = 1/k_B T\): coldness
  • Same occupation; entropy sits in a different place

Two Representations

  • \(T\)-first: \(F = U - TS\)
  • \(\beta\)-first: \(Z(\beta)\), then \(U = -\partial_\beta\log Z\)

Free Energy Decomposition

Once \(p_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z\) is fixed, thermodynamics is bookkeeping.

  • Mean energy: \(U = \langle E\rangle = \sum_i p_i E_i\)
  • Entropy: \(S = -k_B\sum_i p_i \log p_i\)
  • Helmholtz free energy: \(F = U - TS = -kT\log Z\)
LO1. Read \(F=U-TS\) as available energy. Name the subtraction; Legendre transform waits for week 5.

  • Prescription: \(p_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z\)
  • Accounting: \(F = U - TS\)

From Loss Landscapes to Hamiltonians

Walking Down an Energy

Gradient descent: ‘walking downhill’

  • Contours of \(E(\mathbf{ w})\) = elevation lines on a hill
  • Steepest descent: step opposite \(\nabla E\)
  • Same \(E\) as least squares / negative log-likelihood
Contours of the least-squares energy \(E(m,c)=\sum_n(y_n-mx_n-c)^2\). Steepest descent walks toward the bowl.

Potential Energy

The loss is a potential energy \(V(\mathbf{q})\) on parameter space.

  • Configuration \(\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{ w}\): where the system sits
  • Force \(\propto -\nabla V\): direction of steepest descent
  • Common analogy: ball in a bowl; GD is the overdamped limit

In Practice: Stochastic

SGD estimates \(\nabla V\) from a random mini-batch

  • Full-batch \(\nabla V\): exact force, expensive
  • SGD: noisy force; cheap; enables online updates
  • Noise can help escape shallow traps (less relevant for convex bowls)

Where Is the Kinetic Energy?

Where is the kinetic energy?

  • GD / SGD: overdamped—friction, no momentum inventory
  • Momentum methods (heavy ball, Adam): heuristic inertia
  • Physics: kinetic energy \(T(\mathbf{p})\) lives on momentum \(\mathbf{p}\)

The Hamiltonian

\[ H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p}) = T(\mathbf{p}) + V(\mathbf{q}) \]

  • Total energy = kinetic + potential
  • Hamilton: \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\partial H/\partial\mathbf{p}\), \(\dot{\mathbf{p}}=-\partial H/\partial\mathbf{q}\)
  • \(H\) conserved along the flow (ideal, frictionless case)

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

HMC: invent momentum, simulate Hamiltonian dynamics, accept/reject.

  • Draw \(\mathbf{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,M)\); integrate Hamilton’s equations for a short fictitious time
  • Propose the endpoint \((\mathbf{q}',\mathbf{p}')\); Metropolis test on \(H\)
  • Marginal samples of \(\mathbf{q}\) target \(p(\mathbf{q})\propto e^{-V(\mathbf{q})}\)

Why Leapfrog?

Hamilton’s equations are continuous. A computer must take discrete steps.

  • Naive Euler breaks time-reversibility and volume preservation — Metropolis rejects almost everything
  • Leapfrog (velocity Verlet): staggered updates of \(\mathbf{p}\) and \(\mathbf{q}\)
  • Symplectic and reversible; \(H\) drifts only slowly, so accept rates stay high
One blackboard sketch of leapfrog staggering is enough. Do not derive the symplectic condition; name reversibility + volume preservation as the reason Euler fails.

Trajectories Propose; Endpoints Sample

The leapfrog path is not a sequence of posterior samples.

  • \(L\) leapfrog steps build one proposal from \((\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\) to \((\mathbf{q}',\mathbf{p}')\)
  • Intermediate points on the path are integrator waypoints — discard them
  • Only the accepted endpoint (after Metropolis) enters the Markov chain
Point at the quadratic figure: curves = proposals; markers = accepted samples only. Say it twice if needed.

Thermodynamic Bath and Schottky’s Anomaly

A thermodynamic bath is the large system that justifies the canonical ensemble: it fixes \(T\) and exchanges energy with a small system.

  • Two-state system: \(E\in\{0,\varepsilon\}\)
  • Heat capacity: \(C = \partial U/\partial T\) peaks at intermediate \(T\)
  • Schottky’s anomaly: both states populated; maximal thermal response
