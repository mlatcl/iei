The Information Game

The Information Game

I’m thinking of an animal …

  • Goal: name the object
  • Cost: number of questions (worst case, or on average)
  • Optimal play halves the remaining possibilities each time
Run this live if time allows: owl, fox, snake, Gruffalo. Two fair questions name one of four. Students should feel that a balanced question buys one bit. Do not yet write \(-\sum p\log p\); the counting argument is enough.

Four Animals, Two Bits

Owl, fox, snake, Gruffalo. Two fair yes/nos name one of four.

A balanced yes/no tree for four animals. Each question halves the remaining list, so two answers name one of \(2^2=4\) hypotheses.

Bits as Balanced Questions

A fair yes/no answer distinguishes two equally likely cases i.e. one bit.

  • \(N\) equally likely objects \(\Rightarrow\) need \(\log_2 N\) bits
  • Twenty questions covers megabit or \(\approx\) million hypotheses: \(2^{20}=2^{10}\times 2^{10}\approx 10^3\times 10^3\)
  • Unequal priors: best questions still split probability as evenly as possible

Twelve Balls, One Odd

MacKay’s weighing problem (MacKay, 2003, p. Ex.~4.1): twelve balls; exactly one is odd; it may be heavier or lighter.

A two-pan balance and twelve identical balls.
  • Instrument: a two-pan balance
  • Task: identify the odd ball and whether it is heavy or light
State the puzzle. Do not list the readings of the balance, and do not walk through which balls go on which pan.

How Should We Proceed?

We have a finite list of hypotheses and a physical instrument. How should we think?

  • How many outcomes are there?
  • How much information do we get per measurement?
  • How many measurements do we require?

Information at the Start

Maths. Before any weighing, how many hypotheses?

  • 12 choices for which ball is odd
  • \(\times\) 2 directions (heavy or light)
  • \(= 24\) equally likely states of the world

Try Two Outcomes

What should we do?

  • Two readings \(\Rightarrow\) at most \(\log_2 2\) bits per use
  • Mechanism next: what we actually put on the pans
Say ``log two bits’’ — one bit if the two readings are equally likely. Do not yet mention a third reading. Board 6 vs 6 as the move people reach for from twenty questions.

Maths: Two Readings

Maths. Start from \(\log_2 24\). Each use returns at most \(\log_2 2\).

\[ \log_2 24 \approx 4.58,\qquad 2^4 = 16 < 24 \le 32 = 2^5 \]

  • Four uses cannot name 24 hypotheses
  • Five uses can, if each use really returns one bit
  • That “if” is the mechanism … answered separately

Mechanism: 6 vs 6

Mechanism. Put six against six.

  • The pans will tip — the odd ball is on one side
  • Follow the down pan, as if the odd ball were heavier
  • Six balls remain under that story
Ask what we actually learned. Under ``it is heavier’’, 12 balls become 6, one bit toward which ball. We do not know the direction. The down pan is six maybe-heavies; the up pan is six maybe-lights. All twelve balls are still in play. Maths next.

Maths: What 6 vs 6 Bought

Maths. Score the same physical act on the 24 hypotheses.

  • 24 \(\to\) 12 remaining hypotheses: exactly \(\log_2 2\) bits
  • Zero balls eliminated — each ball is still possibly odd
  • The bit went into a coupling of group and direction

Coarse-Graining a Die

The game let us invent any yes/no. A cube, like the pans, will not.

Same moral as MacKay, new instrument. Twenty questions was the idealised world. The balance already spoiled it. The die spoils it again: six faces, not \(2^k\). \(\{1,2,3\}\) versus \(\{4,5,6\}\) is one grouping the cube affords. Odd versus even is another — not the next question. After the first grouping three faces remain, as three balls remained on the pans.
  • Fair die: \(H(X)=\log_2 6\approx 2.58\) bits — not an integer
  • One grouping the cube affords: \(\{1,2,3\}\) versus \(\{4,5,6\}\)
  • A different grouping, not a nested question: odd versus even

Low or High: One Question, Three Faces

Is the face in \(\{1,2,3\}\) or \(\{4,5,6\}\)? One yes/no combines three faces.

\[ H(G_{\text{low}})=1 \text{ bit}, \qquad H(X\mid G_{\text{low}})=\log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits} \]

  • Group label: fair coin — one bit
  • Inside either group: three equally likely faces
  • Total: \(1+\log_2 3=\log_2 6\)
Board \(\{1,2,3\}\) versus \(\{4,5,6\}\). This is one grouping the cube affords, not a three-sided die. We keep the coarse label and refuse the face. The residual is \(\log_2 3\) — mechanism will not sell that remainder as a bit.

Mechanism: Three Faces Left

Mechanism. Three faces remain. We cannot ask a nested fair yes/no.

\[ h_2\Bigl(\tfrac13\Bigr)\approx 0.92 < 1 \text{ bit} \]

  • After low/high: \(\{1,2,3\}\) or \(\{4,5,6\}\) — three faces
  • Odd versus even inside either group is \(2\) versus \(1\) — the known-ball tax again
  • An eight-sided die would still be the game: \(4\)–\(4\), then \(2\)–\(2\), then \(1\)–\(1\)
Echo the pans: three balls left, cannot put one and a half on a pan. If a student offers odd/even as the follow-up, board it inside \(\{1,2,3\}\): odd \(\{1,3\}\), even \(\{2\}\). That is \(h_2(1/3)\), not \(1\). The twenty-questions tree needs \(2^k\) leaves. Six is the mechanism refusing. Eight would still live in the idealised world.

Odd or Even: A Different Grouping the Cube Affords

Go back to all six faces. Odd versus even is another physical cut, not the next question.

\[ H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})=1 \text{ bit}, \qquad H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}})=\log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits} \]

  • Same accounting as low/high — a different grouping the cube affords
  • Not the second question after \(\{1,2,3\}\) versus \(\{4,5,6\}\)
  • Mechanism offers cuts; it does not offer the game’s next bit

“Is It a Six?”: Not One Bit

A yes/no question is one bit only when the two answers are equiprobable.

\[ w=\Bigl(\tfrac16,\tfrac56\Bigr), \qquad H(G_{\mathrm{six}})=h_2\Bigl(\tfrac16\Bigr)\approx 0.65 \text{ bits} \]

  • Yes (\(X=6\)): no residual uncertainty
  • No: five faces left, \(H=\log_2 5\)
  • Remaining: \(\tfrac56\log_2 5\approx 1.93\) bits
  • Total again \(\approx 0.65+1.93=2.58=\log_2 6\)
MacKay callback: a yes/no is one bit only when the mechanism realises an even split. The pans taught that; ``is it a six?’’ says it on the cube.

Same Total, Mechanism Chooses the Cut

The budget still adds to \(\log_2 6\). The cube decides which splits exist.

  • Low/high, or odd/even: each pays \(1\), then \(\log_2 3\) — separately
  • Six-or-not: pay \(\approx 0.65\), then \(\approx 1.93\)
  • If you stop at low/high, the die is a coin — composition next
Do not board the grouping identity here. The next include is the tree: stop at the squares and \(H(G)\) is all you pay. Low/high is the 50/50 use of a die.

Grouping Composition

You rarely need the bottom number. Ask a coarser question and stop.

  • “Is it five or above?” — two outcomes, odds \(1:2\)
  • “Low or high?” — two outcomes, odds \(1:1\): the die is a fair coin
  • The coarse-grained state has its own entropy, \(H(G)\)
Start from the practical move: I don’t care which face, only whether it is five or above. Or I want a 50/50 decision and I only have a die — low/high is a coin. In both cases I have stopped part way down the tree. The point of the section is what happens to the entropy when I stop, and when I carry on.
Two coarse-grainings of a fair die. The top level is the coarse question, with branch widths set by the group probabilities \(w_i\); the bottom level is the residual detail. Stopping at the squares costs \(H(G)\). Going all the way down costs \(H(G)+H(X\mid G)\), which is \(\log_2 6\) for either cut.

Coarse-Grained Entropies Compose

Stopping costs \(H(G)\). Carrying on adds the residual. The total never changes.

\[ H(X)=\underbrace{H(w)}_{\text{coarse state}}+\underbrace{\sum_i w_i\, H(p_i)}_{\text{residual detail}} \]

  • \(w_i\): probability of group \(i\); \(p_i\): distribution inside group \(i\)
  • Five or above: \(0.918 + \left(\tfrac{2}{3}\cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 1\right) = 0.918 + 1.667 = \log_2 6\)
  • Coin: \(1 + \log_2 3 = \log_2 6\)
This is the chain rule for a deterministic grouping \(G=g(X)\): \(H(X)=H(G)+H(X\mid G)\). Board that identification.

Coarse-Graining a Coarse-Graining

Coarse-grainings nest, and their entropies add stage by stage.

The entropy budget of a die under different coarse-grainings. Each bar is a sequence of questions, each segment the entropy paid at that stage. The third bar refines “five or above” into pairs \(\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\{5,6\}\) and then into faces. You may stop after any segment; the cost so far is the entropy of the coarse-grained state you have reached.

Reverse Direction

Learning the face determines the group for free. What is left to find after the group?

  • \(H(G\mid X)=0\): the coarse label is redundant given \(X\)
  • Information extracted by the group: \(I(X;G)=H(G)\)
  • Information still to find: \(H(X\mid G)\)

Why the Total Is Invariant

Entropy is the uncertainty that is consistent no matter how you decompose a question into stages.

  • Any partition, any nesting: the stages sum to the same \(H(X)\)
  • That consistency is special to the Shannon form (Baez et al., 2011; Khinchin, 1957)
  • Pointer only today
Khinchin pointer only — consistency under staged questions characterises Shannon \(H\); no proof today. Cite Baez–Fritz–Leinster as the later categorical form of the same demand.

Grouping Is a Choice of Abstraction

Keeping \(\{1,2,3\}\) versus \(\{4,5,6\}\) and refusing the face is the same move as a macrostate.

  • The group label is what we keep; \(\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) is what we refuse
  • Macroscopic variables: extensive \(N,V,E,S\); intensive \(T,P\)
  • We cannot follow Laplace’s curves, so we keep a few numbers

Gaussian, Maxwell–Boltzmann, and the Missing Measure

Composition wrapper only. Children carry the figures. The die has already paid grouping; these figures are the continuous remainder and the undeclared \(m\). Stop before MaxEnt / Lagrange.

Entropy of a Gaussian Velocity Histogram

Equal-width bins of one Cartesian component. Discrete \(H\) is a sum over masses.

  • \(p_i = n_i/N\) from equal-\(\Delta v\) bins of \(v_x\sim\mathscr{N}\left(0,\sigma^2\right)\)
  • \(H=-\sum_i p_i \log p_i\) depends only on those masses
  • Analytic \(h=\tfrac12\log(2\pi e \sigma^2)\); the histogram tracks \(h-\log\Delta\)
Callback to the week-2 velocity histogram. Board \(p_i\approx f\Delta\) once. The two numbers on the figure should agree; \(h\) itself is smaller by \(\log\Delta\) (and is negative if \(\Delta\) is large). Nats throughout.

Histograms as Riemann Sums

A histogram replaces \(f(x)\) by a piecewise-constant function: height \(f(x_i)\) on each bin of width \(\Delta\).

  • Bin mass \(p_i = \int_{\text{bin } i} f(x)\,\text{d}x \approx f(x_i)\Delta\) (midpoint rule)
  • \(\sum_i g(x_i)\Delta \to \int g(x)\,\text{d}x\) as \(\Delta\to 0\)
  • Used twice: \(\sum_i f_i\Delta \approx 1\) and \(-\sum_i f_i\log f_i\,\Delta \approx h\)
  • So \(H \approx h - \log\Delta\): the \(-\log\Delta\) comes from the \(\Delta\) inside the log
Draw the Gaussian, overlay rectangles of width \(\Delta\) and height \(f(x_i)\), shade one rectangle and label its area \(p_i\). Then split \(\log(f_i\Delta)\) on the board. Point out that the second sum is not a Riemann sum of anything finite as \(\Delta\to 0\), which is why \(H\) diverges while \(h\) doesn’t.
Equal-width histogram of one Cartesian velocity component. Discrete \(H\) matches \(h-\log\Delta\); \(h\) itself is the remainder after the bin-width term is subtracted.

Pushed Bins Keep the Same Entropy

The same cells, written on the speed axis: masses unchanged, widths change.

  • Equal-volume shells in 3D velocity: equal width in \(u=s^3\)
  • Push the edges to \(s=u^{1/3}\); \(\Delta s_i\) now grows then shrinks
  • \(H\) is a functional of the masses — it does not see the widths
Left panel: probability bars, equal visual width, height \(p_i\). Right: density \(p_i/\Delta s_i\) with the true speed widths. Same \(H\) on both. This is a 1–1 relabelling of a partition of speed (volume coordinate \(\leftrightarrow\) speed). Folding \(v\mapsto|v|\), or grouping Cartesian cubes into shells, merges cells and drops \(H\). That is not this figure.
Equal-volume shells in 3D velocity, written two ways. Left: probability masses. Right: the same masses as a density on the speed axis. The bins change shape; \(H\) does not.

The Continuous Limit Drops \(\log\Delta\)

As the bins shrink, discrete \(H\) tracks \(-\log\Delta\) to infinity. Differential \(h\) is what remains.

  • \(H(X^\Delta)\approx h-\log\Delta\)
  • \(-\log\Delta\to+\infty\) as \(\Delta\to 0\)
  • Passing to a density throws that term away
The scatter should sit on the line \(h-\log\Delta\). The horizontal line at \(h\) is the finite remainder. The move to a continuous entropy is exactly ignoring a term that is driving to \(+\infty\).
Discrete entropy of an equal-width Gaussian histogram against \(-\log\Delta\). The points track \(h-\log\Delta\); \(h\) is the finite part. The continuous limit discards a term that diverges.

Entropy of the Maxwell Speed Law

The speed \(s=|\mathbf{v}|\) is a different random variable, with a different \(h\).

  • \(f(s)=4\pi s^2(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-3/2}e^{-s^2/2\sigma^2}\)
  • \(h(s)=\log(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})+\gamma-\tfrac12\), not \(\tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)\)
  • Why is this number not the Gaussian’s \(h\)?
Overlay the Maxwell curve on an equal-\(\Delta s\) histogram of the same 3D sample. Read both numbers. The Gaussian \(h\) was for \(v_x\); this \(h\) is for \(s\). Different variable and different Lebesgue measure. Leave the Jacobian for the next snippet.
Equal-width histogram of speed from the same 3D Gaussian sample. Discrete \(H\) tracks \(h(s)-\log\Delta\). The Maxwell \(h(s)\) is not the Gaussian \(h(v_x)\).

Two Discretisations of the Same Speeds

Equal \(\Delta s\) is a different partition from the pushed equal-volume cells. Discrete \(H\) changes.

  • Same samples \(s=|\mathbf{v}|\), two partitions
  • Pushed: equal 3D volume (\(u=s^3\)); uniform: equal \(\Delta s\)
  • The discretisation is the measure
Side by side: same \(N\), same \(s_{\max}\), same number of bins. Only the edge placement changes. Two values of \(H\). Uniform-in-\(s\) is Lebesgue on the speed axis; equal-volume is Lebesgue in 3D, written in \(s\).
The same speed samples, two partitions, two values of discrete \(H\). Left: equal 3D volume, pushed to the speed axis. Right: equal \(\Delta s\). The discretisation is the measure.

Bin Widths Are a Radon-Nikodym Derivative

The ratio of widths is the discrete Jacobian. It converts one \(h\) into the other.

  • Same cells: \(H\) is shared; \(h\approx H+\sum_i p_i\log\Delta_i\)
  • \(\Delta s_i/\Delta u_i \to |{\mathrm{d}s}/{\mathrm{d}u}|=1/(3s^2)\)
  • Continuum name: Radon-Nikodym derivative between the two measures
Board \(h\approx H+\sum p_i\log\Delta_i\) for unequal bins. The difference of the two remainders is \(\sum p_i\log(\Delta s_i/\Delta u_i)\), which tracks \(\mathbb{E}\log|J|\). Do not do \(\sigma\)-algebras. The width ratio is the discrete RN derivative.
Width ratio of the pushed equal-volume cells, against the Jacobian \(1/(3s^2)\). The average log-ratio converts \(h(u)\) into \(h(s)\). That ratio is the discrete Radon-Nikodym derivative.

Jaynes: Promote the Bins to a Measure

The well-defined continuum object is relative entropy against a declared \(m\).

\[ h_m(f)=-\int f\log\frac{f}{m}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=-\text{KL}\left( f\,\|\,m \right) \]

  • \(m\) is Jaynes’ limiting density of discrete points — the binning, written as a density
  • Uniform \(m\) in \(v_x\) recovers ordinary \(h(v_x)\)
  • Uniform \(m\) in 3D velocity is \(m(s)\propto s^2\) in speed
Name \(m\). Do not do Lagrange, the die, or MaxEnt — those snippets already exist. The Gaussian versus Maxwell disagreement was an undeclared change of \(m\). KL against a common \(m\) is invariant; see relative-entropy-reparametrisation.

Compartments Around the Membrane

Draw extra partitions. Local equilibrium is allowed only where collisions can restore it.

  • Distant left, distant right: far from the gate — Maxwellian at \(T_L\), \(T_R\)
  • Kinetic layers against the membrane: selected arrivals, not remixed
  • The membrane itself is the JS rule: hot left \(\to\) right, cold right \(\to\) left

Bulks Are Groups; the Gate Is Not

The extra partitions are a coarse graining. They are legal only where mixing is fast.

  • Each bulk: the die’s move — keep \(T\), \(N\), \(E\); refuse which molecule
  • Kinetic layer: the faces have just been selected; \(\pi_i\) has not returned
  • We may write a Maxwellian in the distant compartments. Not at the gate

Two Ledgers at the Membrane

What Does the Membrane Do?

The JavaScript demon is a selective membrane: one speed threshold \(v_\star\), opposite rules on each side.

  • Left \(\to\) right: pass only if \(v > v_\star\) (hot)
  • Right \(\to\) left: pass only if \(v \le v_\star\) (cold)
  • Elastic collisions remix velocities in the bulks; the membrane only gates crossings
Match the canvas: red = hot, blue = cold. Threshold in the sim is \(v_\star=3\) with initial speed \(5\). Right bulk heats. The compartments include already drew the extra partitions.

Steady State Is Two Ledgers

A closed box in steady state needs both currents through the gate to vanish. That is not detailed balance.

  • Number current: balls left \(\to\) right equal balls right \(\to\) left, or density keeps drifting
  • Energy current: the two streams carry different energy per ball
  • Detailed balance would match every velocity class with its reverse — the gate forbids that
Name the distinction once. Flux balance of a conserved quantity is necessary for a NESS of that quantity. Detailed balance is stronger: each process equals its reverse. The membrane is non-reciprocal, so velocity-resolved detailed balance fails by construction. Board number versus energy next.
Two ledgers at the same gate. For a closed box, a steady bulk number requires equal counts both ways. The hot stream carries more energy per ball than the cold stream, so those equal counts are not an energy balance. A steady bulk energy would need the lower pair of arrows to match as well.

Maxwellians Cannot Close Both

Assume Maxwellian bulks at the gate. Then number balance leaves a heat current.

  • Hot crossings carry more energy per ball than cold crossings
  • Equal number fluxes still leave a net energy flux
  • Closing energy as well would break number — unless \(T_L=T_R\), which the filter drives away from
  • So the distributions at the gate are not the bulk Maxwellians
This is the compartments paying rent. The distant left and right may be Maxwellian. The kinetic layers cannot be, because that is the only way a closed box can stop evolving. Do not dive into the \(T_L\), \(T_R\) numbers until they ask; those numbers force the illegal assumption.

A First Cut: Close Only the Number Ledger

If we force Maxwellian bulks and balance counts only, the temperatures split and gas entropy falls.

  • Assume each bulk thermalises to a 2D Maxwellian at its own \(T_L\), \(T_R\)
  • Particle flux \(L\to R\): only the hot tail, \(v>v_\star\)
  • Particle flux \(R\to L\): only the cold body, \(v\le v_\star\)
  • Number ledger: \(\Phi_L^{\mathrm{hot}}(n_L,T_L)=\Phi_R^{\mathrm{cold}}(n_R,T_R)\)
These numbers are what you get if you extend local equilibrium up to the gate and ignore the energy ledger. Live \(T_L\approx 1.9\), \(T_R\approx 23\) from the plot. They are a first cut on direction, not a true NESS.
Left: temperatures from balancing number flux at equal populations, forcing Maxwellian bulks all the way to the gate. Right: entropy of that two-Maxwellian state minus the single-temperature equilibrium. The red point is the simulation threshold \(v_\star=3\). These numbers close only one ledger.

The Split Is the Illegal Extension

For the simulation budget, number-flux balance at equal \(N\) yields \(T_L\approx 1.9\), \(T_R\approx 23\) (\(k_B=1\)).

  • Unconstrained equilibrium: one \(T_0=\langle\mathrm{KE}\rangle\), entropy \(S_0\)
  • This first cut: \(S_{\mathrm{ss}}<S_0\) (here \(\Delta S\approx -24\) nats)
  • The gas under this assumption looks like a second-law violation
  • The assumption was the Maxwellian gate — which the energy ledger already forbade
Canvas entropy is a coarse velocity-bin proxy; this \(S\) is the ideal-gas Shannon entropy of two Maxwellians. Do not harvest the \(\Delta S\). The fork is next: where the entropy is allowed to sit once the gate is not Maxwellian.

Where Can the Entropy Sit?

The two ledgers do not close under a Maxwellian gate. That is the fork, not the resolution.

  • Illegal coarse graining: Maxwellian all the way to \(v_\star\) — both ledgers cannot close
  • Physical trapdoor: the door thermalises (Smoluchowski, 1914)
  • Memoryful policy: the record fills; erasure is on the ledger (Landauer, 1961; Szilard, 1929)
  • One ensemble for the whole box: sorting is a fluctuation, not a cycle (Maxwell, 1871)
Pointer only. Do not open Szilard, Landauer, or Ellis here. Name the families so the next worksheet has somewhere to go. Maxwell’s own point is already on the quote slide: the second law is about bodies we deal with in large masses.

The Demon Sorts Groups

Hot versus cold is a coarse label — week 2’s grouping, now with a policy.

  • Group \(G\): pass / no-pass under the membrane rule (tail vs body)
  • Within group: collisions try to restore a Maxwellian \(\pi_G\)
  • Local-equilibrium assumption: mixing inside each side is fast vs crossings
Callback to the die and to the extra partitions. Maxwellians are licensed in the distant bulks, not at the gate. Hot versus cold is a second grouping, now in velocity, on top of the spatial one. The number-flux \(T_L\), \(T_R\) split already used local equilibrium as a hypothesis — and the energy ledger forbade extending it to the membrane.

Lumpability (Pointer)

When do the groups themselves form a Markov process?

  • If exit rates agree for every microstate in a group \(\Rightarrow\) coarse chain is Markov (lumpable)
  • Otherwise coarse-graining leaves memory — projection operators (Zwanzig/Mori), not this course’s core
  • Demon membrane: approximately lumpable only while local equilibrium holds
