The Information Game

[edit]

Before writing a formula for entropy, play a game. One player thinks of an object drawn from a known finite list. The other may ask only yes/no questions. How many questions do you need in the worst case? That count is already a measure of information: it is how much uncertainty you must remove to name the object.

With a list of size \(N\) and equal prior probability on each item, an ideal strategy halves the search space at every step. After \(k\) answers you can distinguish at most \(2^k\) hypotheses, so you need \[ k \ge \log_2 N \] questions in the worst case. Twenty questions reach a million possibilities: \(2^{20} = 1\,048\,576\). A fair yes/no answer therefore carries one bit of information. That is Shannon’s unit before Shannon’s formula: information as the number of balanced binary distinctions required to resolve uncertainty.

If the prior is not uniform, the best question is no longer a literal half-split of the count of remaining objects. It is a half-split of their probability. The average number of questions under optimal play becomes the Shannon entropy \(H = -\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i\). We will meet that formula next; the game already shows what the number is counting.

MacKay’s Balance and Balls

[edit]

David MacKay opens the compression half of Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms with a weighing puzzle (MacKay, 2003, p. Exercise~4.1). State the puzzle first. Do not yet say that the instrument is ternary. The information-game habit is to ask how we should proceed — what we must name — and then to try the instrument as if it had two readings, like a yes/no question. Keep the mechanism (what we put on the pans) on a different slide from the maths (how many bits that use can return). Only after that sequence do we notice that the balance has a third reading.

Figure: A two-pan balance and twelve identical balls.

You are given twelve balls, identical in appearance. Exactly one differs in weight from the others; you do not know whether that odd ball is heavier or lighter. A two-pan balance compares two equal-sized groups. Design a strategy that finds the odd ball and the direction of the defect.

Resist jumping to a particular grouping. Count first: how many states of the world. Then try the instrument as a two-outcome device, because that is the game we already know. Keep what we put on the pans separate from the information budget those pans can return.

Under a uniform prior over those twenty-four possibilities, the Shannon uncertainty we must remove is \[ H_{\mathrm{start}} = \log_2 24 \approx 4.58 \text{ bits}. \] At the end we need a single named hypothesis: \(H_{\mathrm{end}} = 0\). The deficit to close is therefore about \(4.58\) bits. That difference — start minus end — is the information the experiment must supply. We have not yet said what the balance returns.

Try Two Outcomes

A two-outcome instrument, used optimally, returns at most \(\log_2 2 = 1\) bit per measurement. The information budget then says \[ 2^4 = 16 < 24 \le 32 = 2^5, \] so five uses can suffice on paper and four cannot. Whether a physical balance achieves that one bit is a different question: it depends on what we put on the pans.

\[ \log_2 24 \approx 4.58,\qquad 2^4 = 16 < 24 \le 32 = 2^5 \]

Write two columns if you can: left, the information budget; right, the pans. The left column does not know about equal group sizes, leftover balls, or borrowing a ball of known mass. Those constraints live on the right. The rest of this block is the right-hand column, scored afterwards by the left.

The move 6 vs 6 is the binary-search instinct. If we already knew the odd ball was heavier, it would be a perfect split of twelve balls. We do not know that. A down-left reading means ``one of the left six is heavy, or one of the right six is light’’: twelve of the original twenty-four hypotheses remain. One bit toward the 24, and no ball has been eliminated. Mechanism and maths have already come apart: the pans moved, the pile of balls did not shrink.

The information gain can be perfect for the two-outcome model — an even split of 24 — while the mechanism fails to name a set of six suspect balls. What we carry forward is six maybe-heavy and six maybe-light. If we now pretend it was heavier and keep only the down pan, we have thrown away half of the remaining hypotheses by assumption, not by measurement.

This is the sequence people sketch on the board: 6 vs 6, then 3 vs 3. It is the right mechanism for a different puzzle — twelve balls, one heavier. Hold that sequence, because the next move is where the pans refuse to cooperate.

With three suspects we have two mechanical choices. Weigh 1 vs 1 and leave one off: the left-off ball is the odd one if the pans balance. That reading is not in the two-outcome story we are trying to tell. Or weigh 2 vs 2 by padding with a ball already known to be normal: A,B against C plus a normal. The pans are now full, so in the heavier-only story they must tip, and we have not used a third symbol. We have changed the split.

\[ \left(\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{1}{3}\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad H = h_2\!\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right) \approx 0.92 < 1 \text{ bit} \]

If A,B,C are the three remaining heavier-suspects and N is known normal, the reading A,B down leaves two hypotheses and has probability \(2/3\); C,N down names C and has probability \(1/3\). Binary entropy \(h_2(2/3)\approx 0.92\) bits. The deficit \(1-0.92\) is exactly the price of the mechanical constraint ``equal numbers on the pans, and no third reading’’. We did not lose those bits because the mathematics of two outcomes changed. We lost them because the mechanism could not realise an equiprobable two-way split of three objects.

1 vs 1 of two remaining heavier-suspects returns the missing bit: the down pan is the odd ball, if the heavier-side story is true. The sequence of acts was 6 vs 6, 3 vs 3, 2 vs 2 with a known, then 1 vs 1. The sequence of gains was 1, 1, \(0.92\), 1 — already not four bits. And this named a ball only inside the heavier-side story.

To finish the original task we must name the direction as well as the ball. The obvious mechanism is candidate versus known normal. If the two-outcome story were true, this use would return the last bit: heavy or light. But if we followed the down pan all the way and the odd ball was actually light, the candidate may be normal — and then the pans balance. The third reading has been sitting in the mechanism whenever a known ball appears. We cannot keep it out without lying about what the device does. That is the cue to abandon the two-outcome model, not to patch it again.

The moral is not that the binary budget is wrong. It is that an information budget is silent about pans. Equal group sizes, leftover balls, and borrowed normals are constraints on the channel we actually have. Score every weighing twice: what the device did, then how many bits that split was worth.

Three Readings

A two-pan balance has a ternary alphabet. The reading ``level’’ is not an annoyance to be designed out; it is a symbol that can carry information. The first move of the two-outcome story, 6 vs 6, sets the probability of that symbol to zero: with exactly one odd ball among the twelve, the pans cannot balance. We spent a mechanism choice to turn a three-outcome device into a two-outcome device, and then paid further mechanism taxes (the known ball) to keep the third symbol from coming back.

\[ \log_2 3 \approx 1.58,\qquad 3^2 = 9 < 24 \le 27 = 3^3 \]

A binary question yields at most one bit. If the three readings are equiprobable, one weighing contributes \[ \log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits}. \] After \(k\) weighings the strategy tree has at most \(3^k\) leaves, so it can name at most \(3^k\) hypotheses. Compare budgets: \[ \begin{align*} 3^2 &= 9 < 24,\\ 3^3 &= 27 \ge 24. \end{align*} \] Two weighings cannot suffice no matter how clever the groupings. Three weighings can: there is spare capacity of three unused leaves (\(27-24=3\)). In bits, \[ 3\log_2 3 \approx 4.75 > \log_2 24 \approx 4.58. \] The gap is small. That is why the puzzle feels tight. It is the same comparison we already made with \(\log_2 2\), with a larger per-use budget.

The first weighing that realises the ternary budget puts four against four and leaves four off. Level then means the odd ball is in the four left off; a tip couples a group of four with a direction, as 6 vs 6 did, but now the third symbol is alive. We do not give the rest of the tree. The design principle is the one already present in the information game: choose each measurement so that its outcomes are as nearly equiprobable as possible under the current posterior. That maximises expected information gain per weighing and is what makes the \(3^3 \ge 24\) budget achievable in practice. Adding a known-normal ball, or arranging a weighing that can never balance, is the same kind of mechanism tax we already computed for \(h_2(2/3)\).

We stop at the existence argument. Constructing the adaptive tree — which balls on which pan at each step — is MacKay’s exercise, and solving it is the best way to feel what ``informative experiment’’ means. What the lecture owns is the split between ledgers: \(\log_2 24\) versus \(\log_2 2\) or \(\log_2 3\) is the information account; 6 vs 6, 4 vs 4, and padding with a known ball are the mechanical account. The two are not the same, and the known-ball tax is the exhibit.

Coarse-Graining a Die

[edit]

Take a fair six-sided die, \(X\in\{1,\ldots,6\}\) with \(p(x)=1/6\), so \[\begin{align} H(X)=\log_2 6 = 1 + \log_2 3 \approx 2.585 \text{ bits}. \end{align}\] Ask questions in two stages: first learn which group the face falls in, then resolve the face inside the group.

\[ H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})=1 \text{ bit}, \qquad H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}})=\log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits} \]

Let \(G_{\mathrm{parity}}=0\) on even faces and \(1\) on odd. Then \(w=(1/2,1/2)\), so \(H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})=1\). Conditional on the group, three faces remain, each with probability \(1/3\), so \(H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}})=\log_2 3\). The stages add: \[\begin{align} H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})+H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}}) = 1+\log_2 3 = \log_2 6 = H(X). \end{align}\]

\[ w=\Bigl(\tfrac16,\tfrac56\Bigr), \qquad H(G_{\mathrm{six}})=h_2\Bigl(\tfrac16\Bigr)\approx 0.65 \text{ bits} \]

Let \(G_{\mathrm{six}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{X=6\}}\). The group weights are unbalanced, \(w=(1/6,5/6)\), so the binary entropy of the first answer is \[\begin{align} H(G_{\mathrm{six}}) = h_2\Bigl(\tfrac16\Bigr) = -\tfrac16\log_2\tfrac16-\tfrac56\log_2\tfrac56 \approx 0.650 \text{ bits}, \end{align}\] one bit. After a yes'' the face is known ($H=0$). After ano’’ five faces remain (\(H=\log_2 5\)). Averaging, \[\begin{align} H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{six}}) = \tfrac16\cdot 0 + \tfrac56\log_2 5 \approx 1.935 \text{ bits}. \end{align}\] Again \(H(G_{\mathrm{six}})+H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{six}})=H(X)\). Note that ``\(\ge 4\)?’’ on a fair die is also a balanced cut (\(\{1,2,3\}\) vs \(\{4,5,6\}\)), so it is one bit — the unbalanced example has to be chosen carefully.

Grouping Composition

[edit]

\[ H(X)=H(w)+\sum_i w_i\, H(p_i) \]

Partition the outcomes of \(X\) into groups labelled by \(i\), with group probabilities \(w_i\) and within-group distributions \(p_i\). Then \[\begin{align} H(X)=H(w)+\sum_i w_i\, H(p_i). \end{align}\] The die examples are the case check: \(H(w)\) is the entropy of the coarse question; \(\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) is what remains once the group is known. If \(G=g(X)\) is the group label, this identity is exactly the chain rule \[\begin{align} H(X)=H(G)+H(X\mid G), \end{align}\] because \(G\) is a function of \(X\) (so \(H(G\mid X)=0\)) and \(H(X\mid G)=\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\).

In the reverse direction, observing \(X\) fixes \(G\) with no leftover uncertainty. The mutual information \(I(X;G)=H(G)-H(G\mid X)=H(G)\) is exactly the price of the coarse question. After you know the group, the remaining work is \(H(X\mid G)\) — which of the fine outcomes inside that group. Coarse-graining keeps \(H(G)\) as your description and discards \(H(X\mid G)\): \[\begin{align} \text{information lost by coarse-graining} = H(X\mid G) = \sum_i w_i\, H(p_i). \end{align}\]

The die paid \(\log_2 6\) whichever cut came first. That invariance under regrouping is not an accident of base-two logarithms; it is part of what characterises Shannon entropy among candidate uncertainty measures (Khinchin-information57?). Categorical accounts make the same demand precise (Baez-entropy11?). For this lecture it is enough that staged questions add, so coarse-graining has a well-defined price.

Coarse-Graining as Abstraction

[edit]

Daniel Bernoulli did not integrate Laplace’s curve for every corpuscle. He replaced the swarm with a coarse mechanical story that still predicts pressure — exactly keeping a group-level description and discarding within-group path detail. Thermodynamic macrostates are the same move with better bookkeeping: the entropy \(S\) is the uncertainty still unresolved inside the macrostate. In intelligent systems the hierarchy is familiar: raw observations are partitioned into features, features into concepts. At each level you retain \(H(w)\) for the labels you use and lose \(\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) for the distinctions you refuse to make. Which partition you choose is Jaynes’ and Wigner’s anthropomorphic choice of degrees of freedom — coming next in this review.

Static Bookkeeping Versus a Physical Ensemble

[edit]

The decomposition \(H=H(w)+\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) is pure bookkeeping on a single distribution. The die roll has no temporal dynamics because we used the identity only as accounting. Interpreting \(p\) as ``the distribution of the system’s state’’ adds a dynamical hypothesis: something like stationarity plus ergodicity, so that an ensemble average equals what one trajectory shows over long time. Equilibrium thermodynamics makes the same move. The equilibrium density is stationary under the microscopic dynamics, and thermodynamic entropy is then a time-independent number of the form \[\begin{align} S = H(w) + \sum_i w_i H(\pi_i) \end{align}\] when each group has relaxed to its own conditional stationary law \(\pi_i\) and only the coarse weights remain to carry slow change. Dynamics and groups — local equilibrium, lumpability, broken ergodicity, and the decay of KL — return later today and in week 8.

Heat, and the Box

The heat thread from 27 October got us to Helmholtz: inner energy splits as \(U=F+TS\), and entropy is the cut you cannot get back as work. That algebra assumed an ensemble. The ensemble lives in a container. Black’s quantity of heat was measured by mixing, and later by a calorimeter that is literally a box of ice. Rumford surrounded a cannon with a wooden box of water and boiled it. Carnot’s mill has an upper reservoir and a lower one. After Maxwell the kinetic half of an ideal-gas particle is three copies of \(E=v_i^2/2\); the configuration half is a factor \(V\) in \(Z\) — a box, not another Gaussian well.

Bernoulli already made the same move without the word entropy: he drew a box around a subsection of the flow and kept a mechanical story that still predicts pressure. That box is a group in the sense we have just written. The coarse label is the macrostate of the vessel. The faces inside the group are the molecules we refuse to track. Thermodynamic entropy is counting the residual, \[ \sum_i w_i H(p_i). \] Clausius’s second law is a statement about that box, written for bodies we deal with only in large masses. The demon is the being who declines the discard.

Local Equilibrium as Grouping

[edit]

\[ S(t)=H\bigl(w(t)\bigr)+\sum_i w_i(t)\, H(\pi_i) \]

Week 2’s grouping identity holds at every instant. Local equilibrium is the dynamical regime in which each within-group distribution has already settled to its conditional stationary law \(\pi_i\), while the coarse weights \(w_i(t)\) may still change on a slower clock. Then \[\begin{align} S(t)=H\bigl(w(t)\bigr)+\sum_i w_i(t)\, H(\pi_i). \end{align}\] Macroscopic thermodynamics lives in this picture: the within-group sum is the entropy attributed to a fixed macrostate, and the slow story is redistribution of probability among macrostates. Non-equilibrium, at this first cut, means either the groups have not finished relaxing (\(p_i\neq\pi_i\)) or an external protocol is driving \(w(t)\) (finite-time thermodynamics; Crooks length in weeks 6–7).

Clausius’s Second Law

Maxwell wrote that his demon would act ``in contradiction to the second law of thermodynamics’’ — the law Clausius had formulated for heat engines and irreversible processes. Szilard and Landauer do not replace Clausius; they extend the same no-go to stored outcomes and erased bits. The demon’s sorting policy remains a prescription; it cannot repeal the law Clausius stated. What is new today is the grouping language: the being is trying to move \(H(w)\) while treating the within-group term as a bath that has already mixed.

Maxwell’s Demon

Maxwell’s Demon

[edit]

Maxwell’s demon is a thought experiment described by James Clerk Maxwell in his book, Theory of Heat (Maxwell, 1871) on page 308.

But if we conceive a being whose faculties are so sharpened that he can follow every molecule in its course, such a being, whose attributes are still as essentially finite as our own, would be able to do what is at present impossible to us. For we have seen that the molecules in a vessel full of air at uniform temperature are moving with velocities by no means uniform, though the mean velocity of any great number of them, arbitrarily selected, is almost exactly uniform. Now let us suppose that such a vessel is divided into two portions, A and B, by a division in which there is a small hole, and that a being, who can see the individual molecules, opens and closes this hole, so as to allow only the swifter molecules to pass from A to B, and the only the slower ones to pass from B to A. He will thus, without expenditure of work, raise the temperature of B and lower that of A, in contradiction to the second law of thermodynamics.

James Clerk Maxwell in Theory of Heat (Maxwell, 1871) page 308

He goes onto say:

This is only one of the instances in which conclusions which we have draw from our experience of bodies consisting of an immense number of molecules may be found not to be applicable to the more delicate observations and experiments which we may suppose made by one who can perceive and handle the individual molecules which we deal with only in large masses

Detailed Balance Across the Membrane

[edit]

In the simulation the membrane does not move. A ball on the left is allowed through only when its speed exceeds \(v_\star\); a ball on the right is allowed through only when its speed is at most \(v_\star\). Between crossings, elastic collisions redistribute energy, so a ball that is cold now can become hot later and then cross. The question is not whether sorting begins — it does — but where the net particle and energy flows settle.

Take equal chamber areas and a 2D ideal gas (the billiard world). On each side the velocity density is Maxwellian, \[ f(\mathbf{v}\mid\beta)=\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta v^2\right),\qquad \beta=\frac{1}{k_B T}, \] with mean kinetic energy \(k_B T\) per ball (two quadratic degrees of freedom). The one-way effusion flux through a unit aperture is \(\Phi_{\mathrm{tot}}=n/\sqrt{2\pi\beta}\). Restricting to the hot or cold set gives \[\begin{align} \Phi^{\mathrm{hot}}(n,\beta) &= \frac{n}{\pi}\left[v_\star e^{-\beta v_\star^2/2}+\int_{v_\star}^\infty e^{-\beta v^2/2}\,\mathrm{d}v\right],\\ \Phi^{\mathrm{cold}}(n,\beta) &= \Phi_{\mathrm{tot}}(n,\beta)-\Phi^{\mathrm{hot}}(n,\beta). \end{align}\] Particle-number detailed balance across the membrane is \[ \Phi^{\mathrm{hot}}(n_L,\beta_L)=\Phi^{\mathrm{cold}}(n_R,\beta_R). \] Total ball number \(N=N_L+N_R\) and total energy \(E=N_L k_B T_L+N_R k_B T_R\) are conserved. For the simulation parameters (\(v_\star=3\), mean kinetic energy \(12.5\) in code units, equal populations \(N_L=N_R\)) this fixes the temperatures uniquely.

Figure: Left: temperatures on each side of the selective membrane from particle-flux detailed balance at equal populations (simulation energy budget). Right: entropy of the two-Maxwellian state minus the single-temperature equilibrium. The red point is the simulation threshold \(v_\star=3\).

With \(k_B=1\), equal populations, \(v_\star=3\) and total energy matching the simulation’s mean kinetic energy \(12.5\), particle-flux detailed balance gives \(T_L\approx 1.94\) and \(T_R\approx 23.1\). The ideal-gas Shannon entropy of a 2D Maxwellian chamber (position Stirling term plus velocity differential entropy) \[ S(N,\beta)=N\left[2+\log\frac{A}{N}-\log\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\right] \] falls by about \(24\) nats relative to the single-temperature state at the same \(N\) and \(E\). That drop is the thermodynamic content of the sorting you see on the canvas: hot accumulates on the right, cold on the left, and the joint distribution over \((\mathrm{side},\mathbf{v})\) is more concentrated than the unconstrained Maxwellian.

A Maxwellian is symmetric in a way the membrane is not. Conditioning on \(v>v_\star\) selects a high-energy set; conditioning on \(v\le v_\star\) selects a low-energy set. When the two number fluxes match, the energy fluxes generally do not. The true non-equilibrium steady state therefore cannot be a pair of perfect Maxwellians: the left hot tail is depleted by leakage, the right cold body is depleted by leakage the other way, and collisions continually refill those sets. The local-temperature calculation remains the right first cut — it predicts a cold left, a hot right, and a lower gas entropy — but the precise \((T_L,T_R)\) pair is an approximation to a non-Maxwellian steady state.

The selective membrane is Maxwell’s demon written as a dynamical rule. The detailed-balance calculation shows that the rule really does drive the gas toward a lower-entropy steady state. Restoring Clausius requires putting the membrane — or the memory that controls an equivalent trap door — on the thermodynamic ledger. Szilard’s engine isolates one bit of that ledger; Landauer’s principle prices erasing it. The simulation’s ``velocity-bin entropy’’ is a coarse proxy for the same story: as sorting proceeds, the displayed histogram entropy typically drops relative to the unsorted gas.

The Demon Sorts Groups

[edit]

Maxwell’s sorting rule partitions velocity space into coarse sets — hot enough to cross'' versuscold enough to cross’’ — and applies different gates to each set. That is exactly week 2’s grouping: a deterministic label \(G=g(\mathbf{v})\) plus residual uncertainty inside each label. The membrane calculation assumed each chamber stays close to a Maxwellian at its own \(T\) — local equilibrium, timescale separation between collisions and crossings. When that fails, the true steady state is no longer a pair of perfect Maxwellians (tails deplete). Information engines and demons play on the tension between moving probability among groups (\(H(w)\)) and the within-group bath term \(\sum w_i H(p_i)\) that thermodynamics prices.

Kemeny–Snell lumpability is the precise condition under which a partition of a Markov chain induces another Markov chain on the groups. When it fails, the macro description needs memory of how the system entered the group. Name the issue; do not develop projection-operator formalism here.

Szilard’s Engine

Figure: Szilard’s Engine

John Norton refers to Szilard’s engine as the “worst thought experiment” Norton (2018) in science.

Szilard (1929): partition, measure which side, expand from \(V_0/2\) to \(V_0\) against a thermal bath. For one molecule the work is \(k_BT\ln 2\). If the outcome is equiprobable, \(H(M)=\ln 2\) nats and \(W_{\mathrm{ext}} = k_B T H(M)\). This is the first link in the chain from Shannon bits (weeks 2–3) to Landauer’s joules (next section).

In Feynman Lectures on Computation (Hey ed., Ch. 5) Feynman uses the Szilard box with a piston on the occupied side: information about position is operational fuel. A chain of such strokes is only sustainable if you keep supplying low-entropy records or pay to erase them. In The Feynman Lectures on Physics (Feynman et al., 1963), Ch. 46, he shows that a one-bath ratchet fails because thermal kicks on the pawl destroy rectification — the same fluctuation physics Smoluchowski and Parrondo et al. cite for autonomous demons. The historical chain is Maxwell (sort by velocity), Szilard (sort by position, one bit), Landauer (pay on erase).

Parrondo, Horowitz and Sagawa (Parrondo et al., 2015) frame the thermodynamics of information as non-equilibrium thermodynamics for states updated by measurement. Shannon entropy of the microstate, multiplied by \(k_B\), is the operative entropy for isothermal processes far from equilibrium. A measurement that correlates system \(X\) with outcome \(M\) raises non-equilibrium free energy by \(k_B T I(X;M)\), so feedback can extract work bounded by that mutual information. In a cyclic Szilard engine with error-free measurement, \(I(X;M)=H(M)=\ln 2\) and \(W_{\mathrm{ext}}=k_BT\ln 2\) saturates the bound. The memory where \(M\) is stored must be physical (Landauer: metastable wells, broken ergodicity) — the equal-depth double well drawn in the next block is that claim made concrete, and one cell of an information reservoir. Over measure–feedback–reset, the mutual-information work is paid either during measurement or during erasure — the cost cannot disappear from the ledger. Stochastic thermodynamics and fluctuation theorems now reproduce Szilard and Landauer in the lab; we cite the review rather than reproducing the full formalism here.

Information Engines

This block condenses _information-game/includes/intelligence-thermodynamics-connection.md from the information-engines talk. The full talk also develops Markov blankets, generalised Jarzynski with feedback, and Ashby requisite variety; we name those in week 8. Here the point is operational: intelligence as feedback control is an information engine only if the memory and measurement bandwidth can support the mutual information the second law demands.

At human scale the energy in random thermal motion swamps any ledger we could maintain in bits. A feedback controller that tried to match \(I(X;M)\) to the work flow of a car engine would need memory and measurement bandwidth beyond physical possibility. Clausius and Carnot already state the macroscopic no-go; information thermodynamics explains why importing Szilard into a gearbox does not help.

ATP synthase synthesises ATP from ADP and phosphate using the proton-motive force. The gradient is both energy reservoir and signal about cellular state. Each proton transit is a discrete event at room temperature; the \(\gamma\) subunit rotates in steps as protons bind and release — a molecular ratchet of the kind Feynman analysed, but coupled to a chemical fuel (gradient) not a single bath. Rough accounting: \(\sim 10^4\) ATP per synaptic event, \(\sim 4\times 10^4\) protons; \(\sim 10^{14}\) synapses with sparse firing gives \(\sim 10^{18}\) protons/s brain-wide, each with several thermal degrees of freedom — petabit-per-second physical throughput distributed across vast numbers of mitochondria and synthase copies, not a single memory register. That is how life improves free-energy conversion where a car engine cannot: nanoscale composition of many information engines, not one demon on a macroscopic shaft. See the information-engines talk and Parrondo et al. (2015) for laboratory Szilárd engines; Roh et al. for cryo-EM structure of rotary proton pumps.

Landauer’s Principle

Boltzmann Memory: Equal Double Well

[edit]

Parrondo, Horowitz and Sagawa insist that memory is physical: outcomes live in metastable states with broken ergodicity (Parrondo et al., 2015). Draw that claim. A thermal particle in a symmetric double well — equal depth, barrier high compared with \(k_BT\) — is one bit of Gibbs memory. The bit is which well, not an energy bias between the wells.

Equal depth is the point. If the wells differed in depth, part of the story would be energetic preference for one label. With \(E_L=E_R\) the equilibrium Gibbs occupation is uniform, \(p=\tfrac12\), and the thermodynamic cost of resetting an unknown bit is purely informational. Retention is kinetic: rare barrier crossings. That is Landauer’s and Bennett’s metastable-memory geometry in one picture.

Figure: Boltzmann memory: equal-depth double well. Store and read use two metastable basins; erase merges them. Equal depth keeps \(E_0=E_1\), so Landauer’s cost is informational rather than an energy bias between labels.

Landauer’s bound applies to the logically irreversible map that sends both labels to a standard state. If the controller already knows the bit and it sits in the target well, there is nothing to compress. If the bit is unknown and equiprobable, phase-space volume halves and the bath must take at least \(k_BT\ln 2\). Equal depth makes that statement sharp: there is no free-energy difference between \(0\) and \(1\) to confuse with the informational cost (Bennett, 1982; Landauer, 1961).

In the thermodynamics of information, an information reservoir is a subsystem that can change the entropy balance while exchanging negligible energy — ideally a sequence of energetically degenerate bits (Barato and Seifert, 2014; Parrondo et al., 2015). The equal-depth double well is one physical cell of that idealisation: flipping or randomising the bit changes Shannon entropy without an energy bias between values. A tape of such wells is the reservoir. Resetting the tape is Landauer accounting on the reservoir’s entropy. A Boltzmann machine is not this one-bit picture; it is a joint Gibbs distribution \(p(s)\propto e^{-E(s)/T}\) over many binary units with couplings. Week 2 named that lineage; week 5 builds the two-spin case. Here the point is the drawable Landauer bit that makes ``memory is physical’’ concrete.

Broken Ergodicity as Frozen Weights

[edit]

Week 2’s grouping identity returns with a dynamical reading. When barriers trap the system in disconnected basins, ergodicity breaks: the long-run behaviour depends on which basin you started in. The invariant measures decompose as \[\begin{align} \pi = \sum_i w_i\, \pi_i \end{align}\] over ergodic components \(\pi_i\) (here: left well and right well). The coarse weights \(w_i\) are fixed by the initial condition and do not equilibrate on the timescale of interest, so \(H(w)\) is a frozen contribution that equilibrium bookkeeping often conditions away by declaring ``the system is in well \(0\).’’ Landauer erasure merges the wells: it destroys that frozen distinction and must pay at least \(k_BT\ln 2\) when the bit was unknown — exactly purchasing the discarded \(H(w)\) from the bath. Glasses are the many-basin version of the same story.

Is Landauer’s Limit Related to Shannon’s Gaussian Channel Capacity?

[edit]

Digital memory can be viewed as a communication channel through time - storing a bit is equivalent to transmitting information to a future moment. This perspective immediately suggests that we look for a connection between Landauer’s erasure principle and Shannon’s channel capacity. The connection might arise because both these systems are about maintaining reliable information against thermal noise.

The Landauer limit (Landauer, 1961) is the minimum amount of heat energy that is dissapated when a bit of information is erased. Conceptually it’s the potential energy associated with holding a bit to an identifiable single value that is differentiable from the background thermal noise (representated by temperature).

The Gaussian channel capacity (Shannon, 1948) represents how identifiable a signal \(S\), is relative to the background noise, \(N\). Here we trigger a small exploration of potential relationship between these two values.

When we store a bit in memory, we maintain a signal that can be reliably distinguished from thermal noise, just as in a communication channel. This suggests that Landauer’s limit for erasure of one bit of information, \(E_{min} = k_BT\), and Shannon’s Gaussian channel capacity, \[ C = \frac{1}{2}\log_2\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right), \] might be different views of the same limit.

Landauer’s limit states that erasing one bit of information requires a minimum energy of \(E_{\text{min}} = k_BT\). For a communication channel operating over time \(1/B\), the signal power \(S = EB\) and noise power \(N = k_BTB\). This gives us: \[ C = \frac{1}{2}\log_2\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\log_2\left(1 + \frac{E}{k_BT}\right) \] where the bandwidth B cancels out in the ratio.

When we operate at Landauer’s limit, setting \(E = k_BT\), we get a signal-to-noise ratio of exactly 1: \[ \frac{S}{N} = \frac{E}{k_BT} = 1 \] This yields a channel capacity of exactly half a bit per second, \[ C = \frac{1}{2}\log_2(2) = \frac{1}{2} \text{ bit/s} \]

The factor of 1/2 appears in Shannon’s formula because of Nyquist’s theorem - we need two samples per cycle at bandwidth B to represent a signal. The bandwidth \(B\) appears in both signal and noise power but cancels in their ratio, showing how Landauer’s energy-per-bit limit connects to Shannon’s bits-per-second capacity.

This connection suggests that Landauer’s limit may correspond to the energy needed to establish a signal-to-noise ratio sufficient to transmit one bit of information per second. The temperature \(T\) may set both the minimum energy scale for information erasure and the noise floor for information transmission.

Implications for Information Engines

This connection suggests that the fundamental limits on information processing may arise from the need to maintain signals above the thermal noise floor. Whether we’re erasing information (Landauer) or transmitting it (Shannon), we need to overcome the same fundamental noise threshold set by temperature.

This perspective suggests that both memory operations (erasure) and communication operations (transmission) are limited by the same physical principles. The temperature \(T\) emerges as a fundamental parameter that sets the scale for both energy requirements and information capacity.

The connection between Landauer’s limit and Shannon’s channel capacity is intriguing but still remains speculative. For Landauer’s original work see Landauer (1961), Bennett’s review and developments see Bennet (1982), and for a more recent overview and connection to developments in non-equilibrium thermodynamics Parrondo et al. (2015).

Landauer (1961): erasing one bit in a bath at temperature \(T\) dissipates at least \(k_B T\ln 2\). Erasure of stored outcomes restores the second law. The demon’s measurement policy is the prescription; it does not repeal the bound. Ellis agrees the demon fails but locates the cost at measurement and gating; Bennett (1982) completes the information ledger: erasure is logically irreversible, so phase-space compression costs at least \(k_B T\ln 2\) per bit — derived, not assumed to save Clausius. Today’s statement is the quasi-static bound (equality in the reversible limit). Finite-time erasure pays a dissipative penalty above \(k_B T\ln 2\); the exact distributional tool is Crooks’ fluctuation theorem, which we meet in week 6 as the bridge to thermodynamic length.

GAIST on Maxwell and Landauer

[edit]

Section 3.3 of (Welling et al., 2026), pages 49–52, is the book’s account of today’s pair. Heat is energy stored in fluctuations we cannot couple to; work is energy stored in a few accessible macroscopic degrees of freedom. The split is subjective: it depends on the devices we can build. Maxwell’s demon is the being who can couple to the molecules, and so appears to extract work from a single bath.

The resolution is Landauer and Bennett (Bennett, 1982; Landauer, 1961): the demon must acquire, store, and erase information, and that process increases entropy by at least as much as the system lost. The modern review is Parrondo et al. (2015). GAIST does not develop Szilard’s engine, and does not yet state the \(k_B T\ln 2\) bound as a calculation. Read Landauer’s two pages for that. The finite-time correction to Landauer — you cannot saturate \(k_B T\ln 2\) in finite time — is Section 22.3, and waits until week 8.

Information and Intelligence: First Cut

Landauer: you cannot erase a bit for less than \(k_B T\ln 2\). Embodiment: you cannot communicate at machine bandwidth. The Atomic Human (Lawrence, 2024) takes the second as the defining constraint on human intelligence — we are locked in relative to the machine, and we overcome it by modelling other minds, not by opening a wider channel. Bauby is the extreme of that fence: Shannon lets us count how locked in he is. Probability’s job, on the human side, is to say how that narrow budget is spent.
