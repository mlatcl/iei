The Information Game

[edit]

Before writing a formula for entropy, play a game. One player thinks of an object drawn from a known finite list. The other may ask only yes/no questions. How many questions do you need in the worst case? That count is already a measure of information: it is how much uncertainty you must remove to name the object.

With a list of size \(N\) and equal prior probability on each item, an ideal strategy halves the search space at every step. After \(k\) answers you can distinguish at most \(2^k\) hypotheses, so you need \[ k \ge \log_2 N \] questions in the worst case. Twenty questions reach a million possibilities: \(2^{20} = 1\,048\,576\). A fair yes/no answer therefore carries one bit of information. That is Shannon’s unit before Shannon’s formula: information as the number of balanced binary distinctions required to resolve uncertainty.

If the prior is not uniform, the best question is no longer a literal half-split of the count of remaining objects. It is a half-split of their probability. The average number of questions under optimal play becomes the Shannon entropy \(H = -\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i\). We will meet that formula next; the game already shows what the number is counting.

MacKay’s Balance and Balls

[edit]

David MacKay opens the compression half of Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms with a weighing puzzle (MacKay, 2003, p. Exercise~4.1). State the puzzle first. Do not yet say that the instrument is ternary. The information-game habit is to ask how we should proceed — what we must name — and then to try the instrument as if it had two readings, like a yes/no question. Keep the mechanism (what we put on the pans) on a different slide from the maths (how many bits that use can return). Only after that sequence do we notice that the balance has a third reading.

Figure: A two-pan balance and twelve identical balls.

You are given twelve balls, identical in appearance. Exactly one differs in weight from the others; you do not know whether that odd ball is heavier or lighter. A two-pan balance compares two equal-sized groups. Design a strategy that finds the odd ball and the direction of the defect.

Resist jumping to a particular grouping. Count first: how many states of the world. Then try the instrument as a two-outcome device, because that is the game we already know. Keep what we put on the pans separate from the information budget those pans can return.

Under a uniform prior over those twenty-four possibilities, the Shannon uncertainty we must remove is \[ H_{\mathrm{start}} = \log_2 24 \approx 4.58 \text{ bits}. \] At the end we need a single named hypothesis: \(H_{\mathrm{end}} = 0\). The deficit to close is therefore about \(4.58\) bits. That difference — start minus end — is the information the experiment must supply. We have not yet said what the balance returns.

Try Two Outcomes

A two-outcome instrument, used optimally, returns at most \(\log_2 2 = 1\) bit per measurement. The information budget then says \[ 2^4 = 16 < 24 \le 32 = 2^5, \] so five uses can suffice on paper and four cannot. Whether a physical balance achieves that one bit is a different question: it depends on what we put on the pans.

\[ \log_2 24 \approx 4.58,\qquad 2^4 = 16 < 24 \le 32 = 2^5 \]

Write two columns if you can: left, the information budget; right, the pans. The left column does not know about equal group sizes, leftover balls, or borrowing a ball of known mass. Those constraints live on the right. The rest of this block is the right-hand column, scored afterwards by the left.

The move 6 vs 6 is the binary-search instinct. If we already knew the odd ball was heavier, it would be a perfect split of twelve balls. We do not know that. A down-left reading means ``one of the left six is heavy, or one of the right six is light’’: twelve of the original twenty-four hypotheses remain. One bit toward the 24, and no ball has been eliminated. Mechanism and maths have already come apart: the pans moved, the pile of balls did not shrink.

The information gain can be perfect for the two-outcome model — an even split of 24 — while the mechanism fails to name a set of six suspect balls. What we carry forward is six maybe-heavy and six maybe-light. If we now pretend it was heavier and keep only the down pan, we have thrown away half of the remaining hypotheses by assumption, not by measurement.

This is the sequence people sketch on the board: 6 vs 6, then 3 vs 3. It is the right mechanism for a different puzzle — twelve balls, one heavier. Hold that sequence, because the next move is where the pans refuse to cooperate.

With three suspects we have two mechanical choices. Weigh 1 vs 1 and leave one off: the left-off ball is the odd one if the pans balance. That reading is not in the two-outcome story we are trying to tell. Or weigh 2 vs 2 by padding with a ball already known to be normal: A,B against C plus a normal. The pans are now full, so in the heavier-only story they must tip, and we have not used a third symbol. We have changed the split.

\[ \left(\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{1}{3}\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad H = h_2\!\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right) \approx 0.92 < 1 \text{ bit} \]

If A,B,C are the three remaining heavier-suspects and N is known normal, the reading A,B down leaves two hypotheses and has probability \(2/3\); C,N down names C and has probability \(1/3\). Binary entropy \(h_2(2/3)\approx 0.92\) bits. The deficit \(1-0.92\) is exactly the price of the mechanical constraint ``equal numbers on the pans, and no third reading’’. We did not lose those bits because the mathematics of two outcomes changed. We lost them because the mechanism could not realise an equiprobable two-way split of three objects.

1 vs 1 of two remaining heavier-suspects returns the missing bit: the down pan is the odd ball, if the heavier-side story is true. The sequence of acts was 6 vs 6, 3 vs 3, 2 vs 2 with a known, then 1 vs 1. The sequence of gains was 1, 1, \(0.92\), 1 — already not four bits. And this named a ball only inside the heavier-side story.

To finish the original task we must name the direction as well as the ball. The obvious mechanism is candidate versus known normal. If the two-outcome story were true, this use would return the last bit: heavy or light. But if we followed the down pan all the way and the odd ball was actually light, the candidate may be normal — and then the pans balance. The third reading has been sitting in the mechanism whenever a known ball appears. We cannot keep it out without lying about what the device does. That is the cue to abandon the two-outcome model, not to patch it again.

The moral is not that the binary budget is wrong. It is that an information budget is silent about pans. Equal group sizes, leftover balls, and borrowed normals are constraints on the channel we actually have. Score every weighing twice: what the device did, then how many bits that split was worth.

Three Readings

A two-pan balance has a ternary alphabet. The reading ``level’’ is not an annoyance to be designed out; it is a symbol that can carry information. The first move of the two-outcome story, 6 vs 6, sets the probability of that symbol to zero: with exactly one odd ball among the twelve, the pans cannot balance. We spent a mechanism choice to turn a three-outcome device into a two-outcome device, and then paid further mechanism taxes (the known ball) to keep the third symbol from coming back.

\[ \log_2 3 \approx 1.58,\qquad 3^2 = 9 < 24 \le 27 = 3^3 \]

A binary question yields at most one bit. If the three readings are equiprobable, one weighing contributes \[ \log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits}. \] After \(k\) weighings the strategy tree has at most \(3^k\) leaves, so it can name at most \(3^k\) hypotheses. Compare budgets: \[ \begin{align*} 3^2 &= 9 < 24,\\ 3^3 &= 27 \ge 24. \end{align*} \] Two weighings cannot suffice no matter how clever the groupings. Three weighings can: there is spare capacity of three unused leaves (\(27-24=3\)). In bits, \[ 3\log_2 3 \approx 4.75 > \log_2 24 \approx 4.58. \] The gap is small. That is why the puzzle feels tight. It is the same comparison we already made with \(\log_2 2\), with a larger per-use budget.

The first weighing that realises the ternary budget puts four against four and leaves four off. Level then means the odd ball is in the four left off; a tip couples a group of four with a direction, as 6 vs 6 did, but now the third symbol is alive. We do not give the rest of the tree. The design principle is the one already present in the information game: choose each measurement so that its outcomes are as nearly equiprobable as possible under the current posterior. That maximises expected information gain per weighing and is what makes the \(3^3 \ge 24\) budget achievable in practice. Adding a known-normal ball, or arranging a weighing that can never balance, is the same kind of mechanism tax we already computed for \(h_2(2/3)\).

We stop at the existence argument. Constructing the adaptive tree — which balls on which pan at each step — is MacKay’s exercise, and solving it is the best way to feel what ``informative experiment’’ means. What the lecture owns is the split between ledgers: \(\log_2 24\) versus \(\log_2 2\) or \(\log_2 3\) is the information account; 6 vs 6, 4 vs 4, and padding with a known ball are the mechanical account. The two are not the same, and the known-ball tax is the exhibit.

Coarse-Graining a Die

[edit]

Take a fair six-sided die, \(X\in\{1,\ldots,6\}\) with \(p(x)=1/6\), so \[\begin{align} H(X)=\log_2 6 = 1 + \log_2 3 \approx 2.585 \text{ bits}. \end{align}\] Ask questions in two stages: first learn which group the face falls in, then resolve the face inside the group.

\[ H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})=1 \text{ bit}, \qquad H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}})=\log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits} \]

Let \(G_{\mathrm{parity}}=0\) on even faces and \(1\) on odd. Then \(w=(1/2,1/2)\), so \(H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})=1\). Conditional on the group, three faces remain, each with probability \(1/3\), so \(H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}})=\log_2 3\). The stages add: \[\begin{align} H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})+H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}}) = 1+\log_2 3 = \log_2 6 = H(X). \end{align}\]

\[ w=\Bigl(\tfrac16,\tfrac56\Bigr), \qquad H(G_{\mathrm{six}})=h_2\Bigl(\tfrac16\Bigr)\approx 0.65 \text{ bits} \]

Let \(G_{\mathrm{six}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{X=6\}}\). The group weights are unbalanced, \(w=(1/6,5/6)\), so the binary entropy of the first answer is \[\begin{align} H(G_{\mathrm{six}}) = h_2\Bigl(\tfrac16\Bigr) = -\tfrac16\log_2\tfrac16-\tfrac56\log_2\tfrac56 \approx 0.650 \text{ bits}, \end{align}\] one bit. After a yes'' the face is known ($H=0$). After ano’’ five faces remain (\(H=\log_2 5\)). Averaging, \[\begin{align} H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{six}}) = \tfrac16\cdot 0 + \tfrac56\log_2 5 \approx 1.935 \text{ bits}. \end{align}\] Again \(H(G_{\mathrm{six}})+H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{six}})=H(X)\). Note that ``\(\ge 4\)?’’ on a fair die is also a balanced cut (\(\{1,2,3\}\) vs \(\{4,5,6\}\)), so it is one bit — the unbalanced example has to be chosen carefully.

Grouping Composition

[edit]

\[ H(X)=H(w)+\sum_i w_i\, H(p_i) \]

Partition the outcomes of \(X\) into groups labelled by \(i\), with group probabilities \(w_i\) and within-group distributions \(p_i\). Then \[\begin{align} H(X)=H(w)+\sum_i w_i\, H(p_i). \end{align}\] The die examples are the case check: \(H(w)\) is the entropy of the coarse question; \(\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) is what remains once the group is known. If \(G=g(X)\) is the group label, this identity is exactly the chain rule \[\begin{align} H(X)=H(G)+H(X\mid G), \end{align}\] because \(G\) is a function of \(X\) (so \(H(G\mid X)=0\)) and \(H(X\mid G)=\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\).

In the reverse direction, observing \(X\) fixes \(G\) with no leftover uncertainty. The mutual information \(I(X;G)=H(G)-H(G\mid X)=H(G)\) is exactly the price of the coarse question. After you know the group, the remaining work is \(H(X\mid G)\) — which of the fine outcomes inside that group. Coarse-graining keeps \(H(G)\) as your description and discards \(H(X\mid G)\): \[\begin{align} \text{information lost by coarse-graining} = H(X\mid G) = \sum_i w_i\, H(p_i). \end{align}\]

The die paid \(\log_2 6\) whichever cut came first. That invariance under regrouping is not an accident of base-two logarithms; it is part of what characterises Shannon entropy among candidate uncertainty measures (Khinchin-information57?). Categorical accounts make the same demand precise (Baez-entropy11?). For this lecture it is enough that staged questions add, so coarse-graining has a well-defined price.

Coarse-Graining as Abstraction

[edit]

Daniel Bernoulli did not integrate Laplace’s curve for every corpuscle. He replaced the swarm with a coarse mechanical story that still predicts pressure — exactly keeping a group-level description and discarding within-group path detail. Thermodynamic macrostates are the same move with better bookkeeping: the entropy \(S\) is the uncertainty still unresolved inside the macrostate. In intelligent systems the hierarchy is familiar: raw observations are partitioned into features, features into concepts. At each level you retain \(H(w)\) for the labels you use and lose \(\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) for the distinctions you refuse to make. Which partition you choose is Jaynes’ and Wigner’s anthropomorphic choice of degrees of freedom — coming next in this review.

Static Bookkeeping Versus a Physical Ensemble

[edit]

The decomposition \(H=H(w)+\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) is pure bookkeeping on a single distribution. The die roll has no temporal dynamics because we used the identity only as accounting. Interpreting \(p\) as ``the distribution of the system’s state’’ adds a dynamical hypothesis: something like stationarity plus ergodicity, so that an ensemble average equals what one trajectory shows over long time. Equilibrium thermodynamics makes the same move. The equilibrium density is stationary under the microscopic dynamics, and thermodynamic entropy is then a time-independent number of the form \[\begin{align} S = H(w) + \sum_i w_i H(\pi_i) \end{align}\] when each group has relaxed to its own conditional stationary law \(\pi_i\) and only the coarse weights remain to carry slow change. Dynamics and groups — local equilibrium, lumpability, broken ergodicity, and the decay of KL — return in weeks 3–4 and 8.

ML to Thermodynamics

Week 1 seeded the occupation \(p_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}\); Week 2 stopped at embodiment. We pick up Gibbs and free energy here before Shannon. From a stationary distribution with given energy we write the Gibbs distribution, \[ p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z} \quad Z = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}. \] Boltzmann came at energy and entropy from the perspective of thermodynamics, Clausius and Carnot, but today we will show the relationship between this stationary (and equilibrium) distribution and the macroscopic formulae of Mayer, Kelvin, Joule, Helmholtz, Clausius and Carnot. We go deeper into Boltzmann’s (and Maxwell, Gibbs and Einstein) microscopic or ensemble interpretation of this equation, and a derivation that that underpins the famous equation \[ U = W + TS \] where \(U=\left\langle E\right\rangle\) is total energy (or inner energy), \(F\) is the free engergy, \(T\) is temperature and \(S\) is physical entropy. Here what we mean by physical entropy, is the translation of dimensionless energy, \(H\), into physical units of Joules per degree through Boltzmann’s constant, \(k_B\), i.e. \(k_B H = -k_B \sum_i p_i \log p_i\).

Carnot and Clausius

[edit]

Sadi Carnot (1796–1832) asked, in 1824, what limits the efficiency of a heat engine. Rudolf Clausius (1822–1888) built on Carnot and Kelvin to state the second law of thermodynamics in several equivalent forms, and in 1865 he coined the name entropy for the state function that tracks irreversibility. Boltzmann and Gibbs, later in the same century, gave the microscopic count behind Clausius’s macroscopic \(S\). Shannon and Jaynes, in the twentieth century, reuse the same functional form with different operational readings. The historical chain is: engines first, then entropy as a named quantity, then statistics, then information.

Clausius did not give the Boltzmann distribution. He gave the macroscopic balance that any prescription must respect. When we write \(p_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}/Z\) and later derive it from MaxEnt, read it as the statistical answer to a constraint Clausius already framed: fixed mean energy, maximum entropy, no perpetual motion.

The Gibbs–Boltzmann Distribution

[edit]

Name the object and fix the formula. The MaxEnt derivation that earns these weights is a separate step: deriving the occupation from Lagrange multipliers before Shannon \(H\) and Jaynes would put the cart before the horse.

Write the equilibrium occupation of a discrete system with energies \(\{E_i\}\) as \[ p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z(\beta)}, \qquad Z(\beta)=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}. \] Physicists call \(p_i\) the Boltzmann distribution (or Boltzmann weights) and, for a system exchanging energy with a bath at fixed \(T\), the Gibbs distribution or canonical ensemble. The three names point at the same formula. The normalisation \(Z(\beta)\) is the partition function. Its logarithm \(\log Z(\beta)\) is the cumulant generating function for the energy under this exponential family: derivatives of \(\log Z\) recover the mean energy, the variance (heat capacity, up to factors of \(\beta\)), and higher cumulants. That generating-function reading is why \(Z\) is more than a normalisation constant.

Figure: Occupation of a two-state system as coldness increases. At low \(\beta\) both states are populated; at high \(\beta\) the ground state dominates.

The Gibbs–Boltzmann occupation is not only a statement about gases and magnets. Hopfield networks (Hopfield, 1982) assign an energy to every binary configuration of a recurrent net and treat recall as a descent toward low-energy states — equilibrium statistics are again Gibbs. Ackley, Hinton and Sejnowski (Ackley et al., 1985) made the weights of that energy learnable: a Boltzmann machine is an undirected model whose distribution over configurations is exactly \(p(s)\propto e^{-E(s)/T}\). The 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics, awarded to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton, recognised that physical-systems reading of computation and learning. Name the lineage so the formula does not feel confined to nineteenth-century heat baths; do not divert into training algorithms.

Coldness and Temperature

[edit]

The Boltzmann occupation can be written in two ways, \[ p_i = \frac{e^{-E_i/k_B T}}{Z} = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z}, \qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}. \] The mathematics is the same, but the emphasis is different.

\(T\) is the variable of the bath. It is what a thermometer reports, and it is the intensive parameter in the Helmholtz accounting \(F = U - TS\). In that representation energy comes first and entropy is the correction: \(TS\) is the cut the second law takes from \(U\).

\(\beta\) is coldness. It is the variable conjugate to energy. In that representation entropy comes first: you maximise \(S\) (or write a generating function in \(\beta\)) and energy is the constraint. Temperature is then a derived reading, \(T = 1/k_B\beta = (\partial S/\partial U)^{-1}\).

We will use both. Weeks 1–2 keep \(T\) in view so the bath is familiar, and they already compute in \(\beta\) because that is the natural argument of \(Z\). Week 5 makes the \(\beta\)-first order honest: the Lagrange multiplier on a mean-energy constraint is coldness, and it is the natural parameter of the exponential family. Weeks 6–7 then treat \(\beta\) as a coordinate on that family. A student who still hears \(\beta\) as ``one over the thermometer’’ will miss why the geometry is written in those coordinates.

From Loss Landscapes to Hamiltonians

[edit]

Seeing standard energies from machine learning as simply the logarithm of the probability leads to a form of static energy. It’s only the “potential energy” of a system. The aim is to find the minimum potential energy, and that is what we call learning. So what of “kinetic energy”.

We can think of the movement of the system as being the learning dynamics. The training is the movement of the parameters towards the minimum. That movement could have mass, and therefore momentum and kinetic energy.

Sometimes this analogy is proposed, like in momentum variables for stochastic gradient descent, but the analogy seems loose. One place where it’s tight is Hamiltonian Monte-Carlo.

Imagine a mass on a hill. It is sensitive to the pull of gravity as transmitted to it through the local slope. This is the potential energy, \(E(\mathbf{ w})\), acting as a force. The force is taken by the gradient of the potential energy which gives the slope, \(\nabla E(\mathbf{ w})\).

In gradient descent we take repeated steps opposite the gradient to reach a local minimum. We consider a little example below where the loss surface, the energy, is taken from a small regression problem where the true line has a gradient of \(m=1.4\) and an offset of \(c=-3.1\). We sample some data from that line by adding noise with a standard deviation of \(0.15\). Then we show the energy landscape for this system.

# Toy line: the error surface is the quadratic energy of the residual.
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 25)
m_true, c_true = 1.4, -3.1
y = m_true * x + c_true + 0.15 * rng.standard_normal(x.shape)

m_vals = np.linspace(m_true - 3, m_true + 3, 80)
c_vals = np.linspace(c_true - 3, c_true + 3, 80)
m_grid, c_grid = np.meshgrid(m_vals, c_vals)
E_grid = np.zeros_like(m_grid)
for i in range(m_grid.shape[0]):
    for j in range(m_grid.shape[1]):
        E_grid[i, j] = ((y - m_grid[i, j] * x - c_grid[i, j]) ** 2).sum()

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5, 5))
plot.regression_contour(fig, ax, m_vals, c_vals, E_grid)
mlai.write_figure('loss-energy-contour.svg', directory='./ml')

In physics the parameters would be the position, \(q\), instead of the weights, \(w\). And typically (for a Hamiltonian) we denote the potential energy in physics by \(V(\cdot)\), so instead of \(E(\mathbf{ w})\) we have \(V(\mathbf{q})\).

So in mechanics, potential energy \(V(\mathbf{q})\) depends on position, \(q\). Gravity on a landscape is the everyday example mentioned above. But there are other forms of potential energy, for example the electrostatic energy of a charge configuration. Or the displacement of a spring. Gradient descent treats model parameters as a position \(\mathbf{q}\) and the training objective as \(V(\mathbf{q})\). The update \(\mathbf{q}\leftarrow\mathbf{q}-\eta\nabla V\) is a discrete, -dominated limit of a particle sliding on a potential. There is no inertia, there is only force from \(V(\cdot)\).

Modern neural network models train when the number of data points is very large. This makes computing the exact gradients very computationally expensive. The energy is typically decomposed as a sum over data points, \[ E(\mathbf{ w}, \mathbf{X}) = \sum_i E(\mathbf{ w}, \mathbf{x}_i) \] where the full data \(\mathbf{X} = \{ \mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^n\).

So when \(n\) is huge, the sum becomes prophibitive. Stochastic gradient descent replaces \(\nabla V\) by an unbiased estimate from one example or a mini-batch. The walk is no longer smooth: the particle feels a jittering force. That is still dynamics on a potential; only the force estimate is random.

The closely related stochastic gradient with Langevin dynamics (SGLD, Welling and Teh (2011)) makes this connection very explicit. There the descent is taken to be a descent in a high friction regime which is subject to thermal noise. Thermal noise is the response of the descending particle to being bumped by particles of a surrounding fluid, like in Brownian motion. So SGLD is like Brownian motion in a potential well.

A classical particle has both potential and kinetic energy. Pure gradient descent never tracks a momentum conjugate to \(\mathbf{q}\); each step forgets velocity. Momentum SGD and Adam reintroduce inertia heuristically. Hamiltonian mechanics does it properly: introduce momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) and a kinetic energy \(T(\mathbf{p})\), usually \(\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{p}^\top M^{-1}\mathbf{p}\).

The Hamiltonian \(H\) is the sum of kinetic and potential energies. Hamilton’s equations are a mainstay of mechanics. They generate a flow on the joint \((\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\) space that conserves \(H\) when the system is isolated. For sampling, one chooses \(V(\mathbf{q})=-\log p(\mathbf{q})\) (up to a constant) so that the marginal on \(\mathbf{q}\) under the Boltzmann weight \(e^{-H}\) recovers the target density \(p(\mathbf{q})\).

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (originally hybrid Monte Carlo) uses fictitious momentum variables so proposals move far in parameter space while staying near level sets of \(H\). The continuous flow cannot be integrated in closed form for a general \(V\), so we discretise. Discretisation error is corrected by a Metropolis accept/reject on the Hamiltonian. The method turns gradient information about \(V\) into efficient exploration of high-dimensional densities. The method was originally developed for Bayesian sampling of neural network weights.

In these systems time is introduced as a “fictitious variable” representing the evolution of the algorithm and the momentum variables are known as “auxiliary variables”. The idea is to show that as time increases the system converges to the stationary distribution associated with the energy function, \(E(\mathbf{ w})\).

We want samples from \(p(\mathbf{q})\propto e^{-V(\mathbf{q})}\). Hamiltonian Monte Carlo works on an enlarged space, adding a momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) so that the joint density is \(\pi(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\propto e^{-H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})}=e^{-V(\mathbf{q})}e^{-T(\mathbf{p})}\). The \(\mathbf{q}\) marginal is the target we care about, and \(\mathbf{p}\) is a nuisance variable we discard.

Now ask what makes a good proposal. A Metropolis step accepts with probability \(\min(1,e^{-\Delta H})\) (given the map is reversible and volume preserving, which is the next section’s business). A proposal is cheap to accept when it lands where \(\pi\) is about as large as where it started, which means \(\Delta H\approx 0\). Along a contour of \(H\) the joint density \(\pi\) does not change at all, so a move that stays on the contour is one the target is indifferent to.

Compare random-walk Metropolis. A big step in a random direction usually goes uphill in \(V\), the density drops, and the step is rejected. So the step size has to stay small, and the walk diffuses. A contour of \(H\) is different: it can be long, and you can travel all the way round it at no cost in acceptance.

It would be a mistake to read this as keeping \(\mathbf{q}\) at constant \(V\). A particle on a level set of \(H\) rolls up the side of the well, slowing as it goes, and rolls back down. Kinetic energy \(T\) and potential energy \(V\) trade off while their sum stays fixed. So \(\mathbf{q}\) travels a long way and visits many values of \(V\), and that is exactly the exploration we want.

What the flow cannot do is change the level of \(H\). That job goes to the momentum refresh. Drawing \(\mathbf{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,M)\) afresh is an exact Gibbs update of \(\pi(\mathbf{p}\mid\mathbf{q})\), and it changes \(T\), hence \(H\), while leaving \(\mathbf{q}\) alone. Each iteration therefore has two parts: a random hop between levels, and a deterministic, free-of-charge trip around the level we landed on. Together they cover the joint space.

This ties back to the energy story. When \(V\) was a loss, crossing its contours was the whole point: that is how we reach the bottom of the bowl. When \(e^{-V}\) is a distribution the bottom is just the single most probable point, and in high dimensions most of the probability mass lies in a shell around it rather than at it. Samples have to be spread over that shell, so a move that keeps dropping to lower \(V\) is working against us. The Hamiltonian flow gives us a way to travel without changing the density, which is the one thing descent cannot do.

The shell is not a metaphor. For \(q\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d)\) we have \(\|q\|^2\sim\chi^2_d\), with mean \(d\) and variance \(2d\). The relative width of that law is \(\sqrt{2/d}\), so as \(d\) grows the mass concentrates near \(\|q\|^2=d\), a spherical shell of radius \(\sqrt{d}\), not at the mode \(q=0\). Descent toward the mode is walking out of the typical set.

The computer cannot follow a contour as a curve. It takes steps. We already know why those steps have to stay near a level of \(H\): that move is free, and Metropolis accepts it. The remaining question is which discrete map keeps us there.

A careless step has the same shape as gradient descent: evaluate the field, move. That was the right move when \(V\) was a loss. Here it walks off the contour, \(H\) grows, and Metropolis is asked to undo a structural failure.

Forward Euler is the descent-shaped integrator: evaluate the field, take a step. On the harmonic well \(V(q)=q^2/2\) the true motion in \((q,p)\) is a circle. Euler does \[ q\leftarrow q+\varepsilon p,\qquad p\leftarrow p-\varepsilon q \] using the old \(q\) in the momentum update. Each step is a shear. The radius grows. \(H\) at the end is larger than \(H\) at the start. Run Metropolis on that \(\Delta H\) and you are fighting a systematic pump of energy.

The continuous flow was reversible and volume-preserving: a small cloud of possible \((q,p)\) — an ensemble of microstates — kept its area as it rode around the circle (Liouville). Euler inflates the cloud and cannot be run backwards by the same rule. The Metropolis correction that was supposed to mop up a small discretisation error is then asked to undo a structural failure, and it cannot.

Leapfrog (velocity Verlet) splits the momentum update around the position update: a half-step of \(\mathbf{p}\), a full-step of \(\mathbf{q}\), a half-step of \(\mathbf{p}\). Reverse the tape by flipping the sign of \(\mathbf{p}\), taking the same steps, flipping \(\mathbf{p}\) again. Each substep is a shear whose Jacobian is triangular with ones on the diagonal, so a blob of possible \((\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\) keeps its area. \(H\) still wobbles — the map is not the true flow — but the wobble does not accumulate a drift. Metropolis then sees a small \(\Delta H\) and the accept rate stays usable.

That is the reason for leapfrog. Not that it is a more accurate ODE solver in the usual sense. A high-order Runge–Kutta can track the circle more closely and still fail reversibility or volume, and then the Metropolis correction is no longer a small mopping-up. The discretisation has to respect the geometry that made the continuous flow a valid proposal in the first place.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import mlai

# Harmonic oscillator H = (q^2 + p^2)/2. Exact motion is the unit circle
# from (1, 0). Euler inflates it; leapfrog stays nearby.
eps = 0.3
n_steps = 40
q0, p0 = 1.0, 0.0

def euler_path(q, p, eps, n):
    qs, ps = [q], [p]
    for _ in range(n):
        q_new = q + eps * p
        p_new = p - eps * q
        q, p = q_new, p_new
        qs.append(q)
        ps.append(p)
    return np.array(qs), np.array(ps)

def leapfrog_path(q, p, eps, n):
    qs, ps = [q], [p]
    for _ in range(n):
        p = p - 0.5 * eps * q
        q = q + eps * p
        p = p - 0.5 * eps * q
        qs.append(q)
        ps.append(p)
    return np.array(qs), np.array(ps)

Each move alone fails. The momentum refresh moves between levels of \(H\) but leaves \(\mathbf{q}\) where it was. The leapfrog ride moves \(\mathbf{q}\) a long way but stays on one level, so alone it could never visit the others. Both leave \(e^{-H}\) invariant, and alternating them covers the joint space: a random hop between levels, then a nearly free trip around the level we landed on.

The Metropolis step is the safety net. Leapfrog does not follow the level exactly, so \(\Delta H\) is small but not zero, and the accept/reject removes the bias that would otherwise leave us sampling the wrong density.

Two knobs. The step size \(\varepsilon\) sets how well leapfrog stays on the level, and so the accept rate. The number of steps \(L\) sets how far round the level we travel. Too few and the chain is a random walk. Too many and the orbit curves back toward where it started, which wastes computation.

# One HMC iteration. V, grad_V: potential and its gradient.
# Leapfrog matches mlai.hmc.leapfrog with unit mass.
def hmc_step(q, V, grad_V, eps, L, rng):
    p = rng.standard_normal(q.shape)           # 1. hop: new level of H
    H0 = V(q) + 0.5 * p @ p

    q_new, p_new = q.copy(), p.copy()          # 2. ride: leapfrog
    p_new -= 0.5 * eps * grad_V(q_new)
    for _ in range(L - 1):
        q_new += eps * p_new
        p_new -= eps * grad_V(q_new)
    q_new += eps * p_new
    p_new -= 0.5 * eps * grad_V(q_new)
    p_new = -p_new                             # flip: makes the map reversible

    H1 = V(q_new) + 0.5 * p_new @ p_new        # 3. correct: Metropolis on dH
    if np.log(rng.uniform()) < H0 - H1:
        return q_new, True
    return q, False

import numpy as np
def shannon_entropy(probs, base=2):
    p = np.asarray(probs, dtype=float)
    p = p[p > 0]
    H = -np.sum(p * np.log(p))
    return H / np.log(base) if base == 2 else H

Arithmetic Coding and Dasher

Arithmetic Coding

[edit]

In Chapter 6 of Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms (MacKay, 2003), MacKay introduces arithmetic coding with a clear philosophy: compression of data from a source entails probabilistic modelling of that source. The encoding operation is then almost an afterthought — once you can predict, you can code.

The guessing game

Section 6.1 motivates the idea with a guessing game. An English speaker tries to predict the next character of a text; after each correct guess we record how many attempts it took. The sequence of guess-counts is highly skewed toward 1 and 2, so it compresses easily. Decoding needs an identical twin who makes the same sequence of guesses: stop them after the recorded number of attempts and they land on the right letter.

The game demonstrates two design principles we will keep: (i) a time-varying predictive mapping is allowed — the predictor may use as much context as it likes; (ii) encoder and decoder must share an identical model.

From guesses to intervals

Section 6.2 replaces the human by a program that, given the string so far, returns a predictive distribution \(\{p_i\}\) over the next symbol. Arithmetic coding turns that distribution into nested intervals on \([0,1)\) (Figures 6.1–6.2). A binary transmission itself defines an interval — the string 01 is the dyadic interval \([0.25, 0.50)\) — so encoding means: find a bit-string whose interval sits inside the interval that the model assigned to the message.

MacKay’s Algorithm 6.3 is the iterative form. Write \(Q_n\) and \(R_n\) for the lower and upper cumulative predictive probabilities. Starting from \(u=0\), \(v=1\), \(p=v-u\), each symbol updates \[ v \leftarrow u + p\,R_n(x_n\mid x_{<n}),\qquad u \leftarrow u + p\,Q_n(x_n\mid x_{<n}),\qquad p \leftarrow v-u. \] The Shannon information content of the string is \(h(x\mid H)=\log[1/P(x\mid H)]\); Exercise 6.1 shows the coded length stays within two bits of that ideal.

Dasher is a writing interface invented by David MacKay (MacKay-dasher98?) that visualises the connection between arithmetic coding and character prediction. The Partition Function

[edit]

The partition function \(Z(\beta)=\sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}\) is a generating function: \(U=-\partial_\beta\log Z\), \(F=-\beta^{-1}\log Z\), \(S=\beta(U-F)\). The canonical ensemble is an equilibrium construction; driving in finite time leaves that manifold.

Figure: Thermodynamic quantities from \(Z(\beta)\) for the two-state system used as a running example.

import numpy as np

def partition(energies, beta):
    return np.sum(np.exp(-beta * np.asarray(energies)))

def thermo_from_Z(beta, energies):
    e = np.asarray(energies, dtype=float)
    Z = partition(e, beta)
    p = np.exp(-beta * e) / Z
    U = np.sum(p * e)
    F = -np.log(Z) / beta
    S = beta * (U - F)
    return U, S, F

GAIST on Entropy and the Partition Function

[edit]

Section 1.2.3 of (Welling et al., 2026) defines Shannon entropy as expected surprisal, \(\mathrm{S}[p] = -\int p\log p\), in nats. It computes the Gaussian entropy and shows that it depends only on \(\sigma\), not on \(\mu\). Section 1.2.4 introduces KL as relative entropy, the information lost when \(q\) stands in for \(p\), and records the chain-rule decomposition of KL. That is useful scaffolding for week 6; it is not Shannon’s chain rule for \(H\).

Section 3.2.4 is the generating-function half of today’s lecture: from \(Z\) to mean energy, entropy, and free energy. Read those two sections. Do not look here for the Shannon axioms, for \(S = kH\), or for channel capacity. Those are MacKay and Shannon (1948). GAIST treats entropy as a property of a distribution, not as a no-go on a code.

GAIST §1.2.3 uses \(-\int p\log p\) for Gaussians — that is differential entropy. It can be negative and is not bounded like discrete Shannon \(H\in[0,\log n]\). Below we introduce \(\text{KL}\left( p\,\|\,q \right)\), which is always \(\ge 0\) discrete and continuous. That is why MaxEnt projections minimise \(\text{KL}\left( \cdot\,\|\,r \right)\), not raw \(H\).

Discrete Shannon Entropy Is Bounded

[edit]

KL divergence \(\text{KL}\left( \cdot\,\|\,\cdot \right)\) measures information lost when \(q\) is used instead of \(p\). It is non-negative in both discrete and continuous settings (with common support). Unlike Shannon entropy, it is defined relative to a reference. MaxEnt and \(m\)-projections minimise \(\text{KL}\left( \cdot\,\|\,r \right)\); that is why week 4’s die optimisation used \(\sum_i p_i\log(p_i/r_i)\).

For \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\), differential entropy is \(\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)\) in nats. As \(\sigma\to 0\) it diverges negatively. Discrete Shannon entropy stays in \([0,\log n]\). Thermodynamic \(S=kH\) in week 1 used the discrete sum form on Boltzmann probabilities. Continuous MaxEnt still works because constraints fix scale; comparing to a reference uses KL, which remains non-negative.

Week 2 left a forward pointer: Maxwell–Boltzmann components are Gaussian; speed is not. That Jacobian is the worked example for why differential entropy depends on coordinates and why \(\text{KL}\left( \cdot\,\|\,\cdot \right)\) does not.

Maxwell–Boltzmann: Components Versus Speed

[edit]

Write the joint density on Cartesian velocities as isotropic, \[\begin{align} f(\mathbf{v}) = \left(\frac{m}{2\pi kT}\right)^{3/2} \exp\Bigl(-\frac{m|\mathbf{v}|^2}{2kT}\Bigr). \end{align}\] Each marginal \(f(v_x)\) is Gaussian. Change to spherical coordinates with \(v=|\mathbf{v}|\) and solid angle \(\Omega\). Probability conservation uses the volume element \(d^3v = v^2\,dv\,d\Omega\). Integrating over angles (\(\int d\Omega = 4\pi\)) gives the Maxwell speed distribution \[\begin{align} f(v) = 4\pi\left(\frac{m}{2\pi kT}\right)^{3/2} v^2\,e^{-mv^2/2kT}, \qquad v\ge 0. \end{align}\] The factor \(v^2\) is the Jacobian of the change of variables — the density of states of spherical shells in velocity space. Calling ``Maxwell–Boltzmann’’ Gaussian is correct for a component and wrong for speed.

\[ f(v_x)=\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{2\pi kT}}\,e^{-mv_x^2/2kT} \qquad\text{versus}\qquad f(v)\propto v^2\,e^{-mv^2/2kT} \]

How Differential Entropy Transforms

[edit]

Define differential entropy \(h(X)=-\int f_X(x)\ln f_X(x)\,dx\). For a smooth invertible map \(y=g(x)\), probability conservation requires \(f_Y(y)\,|dy|=f_X(x)\,|dx|\), so \[\begin{align} f_Y(y)=\frac{f_X(x)}{|g'(x)|}. \end{align}\] Substitute into \(h(Y)\) and change the integration variable back to \(x\): \[\begin{align} h(Y) &= -\int f_Y(y)\ln f_Y(y)\,dy = -\int f_X(x)\ln\frac{f_X(x)}{|g'(x)|}\,dx = h(X)+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\ln|g'(X)|\bigr]. \end{align}\] The shift is the average log-Jacobian.

\[ h(Y)=h(X)+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\ln|g'(X)|\bigr] \]

For a linear map \(y=ax+b\) with \(a\neq 0\), the formula collapses to \(h(aX)=h(X)+\ln|a|\). Because a density \(f\) has units of inverse length (or inverse speed), \(\ln f\) is the log of a dimensionful quantity. That is a symptom that differential entropy secretly depends on a reference scale. Even the claim that a Gaussian maximises entropy at fixed variance is relative to the variable you chose: fixing \(\mathrm{Var}(v_x)\) and fixing \(\mathrm{Var}(v_x^3)\) yield different maximisers.

With scale \(a=\sqrt{kT/m}\), a single Gaussian component has \(h=\ln(a\sqrt{2\pi})+\tfrac12\). The Maxwell speed distribution has a different value (the expectation of \(\ln(v^2)\) against \(f(v)\) shifts the integral). Neither number is ``the’’ entropy of the gas; each is entropy in a chosen coordinate.

Discrete Entropy from Binning

[edit]

Partition the real line into bins of width \(\Delta\). Then \(p_i\approx f(x_i)\Delta\) and \[\begin{align} H(X^\Delta) = -\sum_i p_i\ln p_i \approx h(X)-\ln\Delta. \end{align}\] Shannon entropy of the binned variable is non-negative and diverges as the bins shrink. Differential entropy is the \(\Delta\)-independent remainder.

Now relabel \(y=g(x)\). Uniform bins of width \(\Delta\) in \(y\) correspond to bins of width \(\Delta/|g'|\) in \(x\) — a non-uniform partition of the original line. A fixed discrete partition is unchanged by renaming outcomes, but ``uniform bins of fixed width’’ is a different partition in each coordinate. That is exactly where \(\mathbb{E}\ln|g'|\) appears. Continuous entropy therefore depends on a choice of reference measure (here, bin width in the chosen coordinate).

Boltzmann counting needs a phase-space cell of volume \(h^{3N}\) (Planck’s constant to the power of degrees of freedom) before \(S=k\ln W\) is meaningful. Liouville’s theorem helps for Hamiltonian flow: canonical transformations preserve phase-space volume, so the reference measure is unchanged. An arbitrary reparametrisation of coordinates does not.

KL Fixes the Coordinate Dependence

[edit]

Take two densities \(f\) and \(g\) on the same space and transform both by the same invertible map \(y=\phi(x)\). Each density picks up the factor \(1/|\phi'|\), which cancels inside the logarithm: \[\begin{align} \text{KL}\left( f_Y\,\|\,g_Y \right) &= \int f_Y\ln\frac{f_Y}{g_Y}\,dy = \int f_X\ln\frac{f_X/|\phi'|}{g_X/|\phi'|}\,dx = \text{KL}\left( f_X\,\|\,g_X \right). \end{align}\] KL divergence (and mutual information) is therefore a property of the pair of distributions, not of the coordinate chart. It also has a clean discrete limit: with a common binning, \(\text{KL}\left( p^\Delta\,\|\,q^\Delta \right)\to \text{KL}\left( f\,\|\,g \right)\) as \(\Delta\to 0\), with no divergent \(-\ln\Delta\) term. Physics often calls KL relative entropy; we keep \(\text{KL}\left( \cdot\,\|\,\cdot \right)\).

\[ \text{KL}\left( f_Y\,\|\,g_Y \right)=\text{KL}\left( f_X\,\|\,g_X \right) \]

\[ h_m(f)=-\int f\ln\frac{f}{m}\,dx=-\text{KL}\left( f\,\|\,m \right) \]

Differential entropy is KL divergence against a reference density \(m\) that is usually left unwritten. Taking \(m\) uniform (Lebesgue) in the coordinate \(x\) recovers the textbook \(h\). Jaynes’ limiting density of discrete points is the same idea: the binning measure of the previous section, written as a density. Once \(m\) is explicit, the Maxwell–Boltzmann story becomes: different choices of ``equally likely a priori’’ yield different maximum-entropy distributions under the same energy constraint.

Local Equilibrium as Grouping

[edit]

\[ S(t)=H\bigl(w(t)\bigr)+\sum_i w_i(t)\, H(\pi_i) \]

Week 2’s grouping identity holds at every instant. Local equilibrium is the dynamical regime in which each within-group distribution has already settled to its conditional stationary law \(\pi_i\), while the coarse weights \(w_i(t)\) may still change on a slower clock. Then \[\begin{align} S(t)=H\bigl(w(t)\bigr)+\sum_i w_i(t)\, H(\pi_i). \end{align}\] Macroscopic thermodynamics lives in this picture: the within-group sum is the entropy attributed to a fixed macrostate, and the slow story is redistribution of probability among macrostates. Non-equilibrium, at this first cut, means either the groups have not finished relaxing (\(p_i\neq\pi_i\)) or an external protocol is driving \(w(t)\) (finite-time thermodynamics; Crooks length in weeks 6–7).
