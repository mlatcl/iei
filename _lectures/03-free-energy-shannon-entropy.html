From Heat to Helmholtz

Week 1 seeded the occupation \(p_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}\). Worksheet 1 told us Clausius did not make entropy a measure of uncertainty. The lecture is the heat thread that gets us to Helmholtz: Black, Carnot, Rumford, the air thermometer, Thomson’s 1848 mill-scale, Joule, Clausius, Thomson’s 1851 return, Maxwell–Boltzmann, Gibbs, then \(F=U-TS\) with \(U=\left\langle E\right\rangle\). The entropy game and MacKay’s weighing problem come after that story. Shannon axioms are parked in Week 4.

From a stationary distribution with given energy we will write the Gibbs distribution, \[ p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z} \quad Z = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}. \] The destination of the thread is \[ U = F + TS \] where \(U=\left\langle E\right\rangle\) is inner energy, \(F\) is Helmholtz free energy, \(T\) is temperature and \(S\) is physical entropy. Physical entropy is the dimensionless functional \(H=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i\) converted into joules per degree by Boltzmann’s constant, \(S=k_B H\). Helmholtz wrote \(A=U-TS\) (\(A\) for Arbeit); we use \(F\) for the same quantity because week 5 needs \(A(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) for the log-partition.

Joseph Black and Heat as a Quantity

[edit]

Before heat was understood as a property, it was understood as a quantity. For Clausius, entropy was not yet a measure of uncertainty, but to try and understand what he was measuring we need to go back to heat. For our purposes the intellectual thread starts with Joseph Black (1728–1799) who is responsible for making heat a quantity you can measure, mix and even hide. This notion of heat as a measurable is different from the notion of temperature as a measurable.

Figure: Joseph Black (1728–1799). Black was a physician and chemist at Glasgow, then Edinburgh. Stipple engraving by James Heath after Henry Raeburn, 1800 (Wellcome Collection / Wikimedia Commons, public domain).

Black lectured in medicine and chemistry, in the mid eighteenth century he was based in Glasgow, where he was a contemporary of James Watt. His work on heat was done while he was still a physician. It was published posthumously by John Robison as Lectures on the Elements of Chemistry (Edinburgh, 1803) (Black, 1803).

Thermometers had existed since about the 17th century. This leads to a mystery, ice melts, but the temperature doesn’t change. The same thing happens at the other end: water boils, steam is produced, and the thermometer sits at the boiling point. If you heat an equal mass of water equally as a mass of mercury, they do not change temperture equally. The important step that Joseph Black made was to realise that temperature and heat are separate quantities. Temperature is a reading, but heat is a ‘quantity’ that can enter that body without changing the reading.

Black’s mixing calorimetry is the mechanism. You account for heat as you would account for a fluid: what leaves one body arrives in the other. The maths is an additive ledger, not a kinetic theory. Mercury, he says, has less capacity for the matter of heat than water: the same heat produces a larger change of temperature. That is specific heat, named as a capacity. The heat that melts ice, or that turns water into steam, does not raise the temperature at all. Black calls it latent: combined with the particles, hidden from the thermometer, recovered when the change of state is reversed.

Figure: The calorimeter of Lavoisier and Laplace. Heat delivered is read as the mass of ice melted; the conversion factor is Black’s latent heat of fusion. Plate XI from Encyclopaedia Londinensis (1801–02), after H. Lascelles (Wikimedia Commons / Science History Institute, public domain).

Black did not build this box. He measured by mixing, and by timing how long a steady fire takes to melt ice or to boil a pan dry. The ice calorimeter is Lavoisier and Laplace in the winter of 1782–83: a sample in the inner chamber, a jacket of ice, an outer ice shield against the room. Heat that leaves the sample melts ice in the middle jacket; you weigh the meltwater. The conversion factor is Black’s latent heat of fusion, about \(143\)–\(144^\circ\mathrm{F}\) of heat per unit mass of ice. This is close to the modern figure of \(80^\circ\mathrm{C}\), or \(334\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg}^{-1}\). The instrument makes his accounting automatic: the thermometer is no longer the meter. The meltwater is.

Black’s unit is the heat that raises an equal mass of water by one degree Fahrenheit. On that scale the interval from melting ice to boiling water is \(180\) units. The extra heat to turn the boiling water into steam he estimates at about \(810\) units: the pan sits on the fire four and a half times longer boiling dry than it did coming up to the boil. He knows the timing method is rough because the the fire is not a closed system. His contemporary, James Watt, condensing steam and measuring the water it warms, gets nearer \(950\). The modern figure is about \(967^\circ\mathrm{F}\) on the same scale, \(540\,\mathrm{cal\,g}^{-1}\), or \(2256\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg}^{-1}\): a little more than five times the heat that takes water from \(0\) to \(100^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The number is already important industrially. James Watt is famous for improving the efficiency of the steam engine. Until his time, a Newcomen engine would condense steam by spraying cold water onto the engine cylinder. That condensation occuring on the cylinder becomes latent heat that the engine has to re-pay on every stroke. Watt’s invention of the separate condenser can be motivated by Black’s latent heat.

Sadi Carnot and the Fall of Heat

[edit]

Black made heat a quantity you can measure, mix and hide. Once it is a quantity, it looks like a fluid you can account for. From our perspective, the next step comes from Sadi Carnot (1796–1832). Carnot has a practical question, he’s not focussed on what heat is made of, he wants to understand the limits of a steam engine.

Figure: Sadi Carnot (1796–1832), aged 17, in the uniform of the École Polytechnique. Portrait by Louis-Léopold Boilly, 1813 (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).

Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot was a military engineer, son of Lazare Carnot. Lazare who was a mathematician and the “Organizer of Victory” of the Revolutionary armies. After Waterloo Lazare he was driven into exile, and Sadi’s career in army under the Bourbon restoration stalled. The portrait is a decade before the book. By 1824 he is on reduced pay in Paris, attending lectures at the Conservatoire, and paying himself to print six hundred copies of a short essay: Réflexions sur la puissance motrice du feu (Carnot, 1824).

After his time in Glasgow, James Watt had moved to Birmingham and together with Matthew Boulton, commercialised a more efficient steam engine. By the 1820s the steam engine was widely used in mines, mills, looms and forges. Carnot beleives that one day it will serve as a universal motor, but he notes that it doesn’t have a theory of mechanics. France is watching Britain do steam better. The so-called “practical men” are improving the capabilities of the machine, but Carnot wants to know whether any of that can be unbounded, or whether there is a ceiling independent of the machine and of the substance that runs through it.

Figure: Title page of R.~H. Thurston’s 1890 English translation of Carnot’s 1824 Réflexions sur la puissance motrice du feu. The original was printed in Paris by Bachelier, at Carnot’s expense, in six hundred copies.

The 1824 original is a short book, 118 pages, almost unread in his lifetime. Thurston’s translation is how most English readers met it. The argument does not depend on calculus. It depends on a picture of heat that Black had made plausible: caloric, a conserved fluid, falling from hot to cold.

Carnot’s own comparison: the motive power of heat can be compared to that of a waterfall. A waterfall has a maximum you cannot exceed, whatever machine you put in the stream. The power depends on the quantity of water and the height of the fall. Heat, likewise, depends on the quantity of caloric and on what he calls the height of its fall — the difference of temperature between the bodies exchanging it. In a steam engine the boiler is the upper reservoir, the condenser the lower. The caloric developed in the firebox is taken up by the steam, does something in the cylinder, and is given up to the cold water. In that picture heat is conserved because that is what a mill requires: the same working fluid in, the same working fluid out, work from the drop. He is not claiming to have weighed heat against work. He is taking Black’s ledger and using it as the mechanics of an engine.

This is the distinction to keep. Carnot’s focus is cyclic conversion of a temperature difference into work. He needs two reservoirs, a working substance, and a closed loop. On that loop, Black’s accounting — heat hidden in steam, recovered in the condenser — looks like conservation. Joule, twenty years later, will ask whether mechanical work can create heat at a definite rate. That is a different experiment, and a different question. We do not yet know that energy is conserved. We do not yet know that heat is energy. Carnot’s conservation of heat will turn out to be a special case, not the general law. Manuscripts found after his death show him beginning to doubt caloric. The book he published does not.

In the summer of 1832 Carnot falls ill; he dies of cholera on 24 August, at Ivry, aged 36. The Réflexions have made no impression. Two years later Émile Clapeyron, another polytechnicien, writes the argument in the language of indicator diagrams, and the book becomes findable. Kelvin will take the mill and make a temperature scale from it. Clausius will have to keep what was right in Carnot — there is a limit, and it depends only on the two temperatures — once heat is no longer a conserved fluid. We are not there yet.

Count Rumford’s Cannon

[edit]

Carnot treated heat as a conserved fluid because that is what a mill requires. The difficulty with that picture was already in print. In 1798, twenty-six years before the Réflexions, Benjamin Thompson, who styled himself Count Rumford, published a paper in the Philosophical Transactions on heat excited by friction (Thompson, 1798).

Figure: Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford (1753–1814). American-born, then in the service of the Elector of Bavaria. Portrait by Moritz Kellerhoven (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).

Rumford was the superintendent for the boring of brass cannons in the military arsenal at Munich. He is struck by how hot the gun becomes, and by how much hotter the metallic chips are (he checks and they are hotter than boiling water). His paper to the royal society asks where does the heat come from?

He asks whether the chips have changed their capacity for heat, as Black’s latent-heat doctrine would require if caloric were being squeezed out of the metal, but he finds they haven’t. He builds an experiment with a short hollow cylinder is turned against a blunt steel borer by machinery carried round by horses. A wooden box filled with water surrounds the cylinder. At one hour the water is at \(107^\circ\mathrm{F}\); at two hours \(178^\circ\mathrm{F}\); at two hours thirty minutes it boils.

Figure: Rumford’s 1798 apparatus, as reprinted in The Complete Works of Count Rumford (1870). Fig.~2: the cannon in the boring machine, turned by the bar \(w\). Fig.~3: the same cylinder in the water box that is brought to the boil.

Rumford remarks that the the source of the heat generated by friction appeared evidently to be inexhaustible. But anything which an insulated body can continue to furnish without limitation cannot possibly be a material substance. That is 1798. It is a qualitative blow to caloric, not a mechanical equivalent. He does not weigh a known quantity of work against a known quantity of heat. He has shown generation. He has shown that the thermodynamic ledger doesn’t close.

Mercury, Ice, Steam, and Air

[edit]

Black already treats temperature as a reading and heat as a quantity. The reading, in the 1840s, comes from one of three expansion thermometers. Fahrenheit and Celsius use mercury. Accurate laboratory work uses air. All three pin two fixed points — ice and steam, or a close cousin — and then divide the interval so that equal expansions of the chosen substance are equal degrees. Thomson will inherit that practice, and then refuse it as a definition of a degree.

Figure: Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit (1686–1736) with a mercury thermometer. Anonymous engraving, 18th century (Wikimedia Commons, public domain). The instrument is mercury in glass; the scale is equal expansion of that mercury between two pins.

Fahrenheit’s mercury thermometer is the household instrument of the eighteenth century. He marks a mixture of ice, water and sal-ammoniac as \(0\), and (originally) blood heat as \(96\). The scale that survives is a recalibration of the same tube: ice at \(32\), steam at \(212\), so there are \(180\) equal expansions of mercury between the two pins. Celsius, in 1742, uses the same pins on a centesimal division. His own numbering ran the other way — \(100\) at ice, \(0\) at steam — and was inverted within a few years. By Thomson’s time ``Celsius’’ means ice \(0\), steam \(100\). The physics is the same as Fahrenheit: you have chosen a substance, and you have called equal expansions of it equal degrees. Black’s whole point is that this reading is not the quantity of heat. The same rise of the same mercury takes different heat in water, in oil, in ice that is melting.

Figure: (a) Constant pressure: a sliding mercury index, open to the room; the volume of the trapped air is the reading. (b) Constant volume: a mercury manometer; the height \(h\) is the reading. Scheme by P. Peterlin (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0). Thomson’s 1848 comparison is with (a).

The air thermometer is the 1840s laboratory standard, the scale to which mercury and alcohol are reduced (Regnault). Take a glass bulb of dry air and confine it with mercury. The mercury is open to the room: either a sliding thread in a capillary, as in (a), or a cistern you raise and lower. You move the mercury until the two surfaces sit at the same height, so the trapped air is at atmospheric pressure. The volume of that air is then the reading. Ice water and steam still fix two points; the interval is divided so that equal increases of volume are equal degrees. That is Gay-Lussac made into an instrument: far from condensation, the volume of air rises almost linearly with this temperature. Push the same line far enough and the volume would vanish at about \(-273^\circ\) of the air scale. That number lives in the instrument, not in Carnot. Panel (b) is the other common form, a constant-volume gas thermometer, where you read pressure from the manometer height. Thomson in 1848 is arguing with (a). Air is a better expansion-substance than mercury — different gases agree closely unless they are about to liquefy — but it is still a substance. A degree that means ``equal expansion of air’’ is not yet independent of the thermometer.

Thomson’s Scale from the Mill

[edit]

William Thomson, who later became Lord Kelvin, takes Carnot seriously as theory. In 1848 he is a twenty-four year old professor of natural philosophy at Glasgow, and he has read Clapeyron’s recasting of the Réflexions. Fahrenheit, Celsius and the air thermometer already exist: ice and steam already mark 0 and 100 on the scale he will compare against. What does not exist is a meaning of degree that is independent of mercury, or of air. If the motive power of heat depends only on the quantity of caloric and the height of its fall, then that height should be usable as a thermometer (Thomson, 1848). That is a real question: what is a degree, if it is not mercury and cannot be equal heat? The 1848 answer still assumes that heat is conserved, still pins \(0\) at ice, and does not locate a bottom of the fall. It is not yet the scale that later bears his name. The question survives Joule. The formula does not.

Figure: William Thomson (1824–1907), later Lord Kelvin. Photograph from The World’s Work, 1902 (Wikimedia Commons, public domain) — half a century after the 1848 scale.

In 1848 Thomson is looking for a thermometric scale that is not an accident of mercury or air. He believes that Carnot’s theory provides such a scale. Quantities of heat he already has from Joseph Black — mixing, ice, steam — without any engine, and without treating heat as a form of work. What he still lacks is a meaning for an interval of temperature. Carnot’s mill supplies it: the motive power of a given quantity of heat falling through one degree. That motive power is mill-work from a fall, as water on a wheel. It is not heat obtained from work, and it is not heat destroyed to make work.

The air thermometer is the scale Thomson is arguing with. Household thermometers use mercury because it is convenient; in accurate work you compare the mercury against air and quote the air reading (Regnault). Air is a better expansion-substance than mercury, but it is still a substance. Thomson will not call that absolute.

Thomson opens the 1848 paper by refusing the calorimetric scale. A perfect thermometer, he says, might seem to mark equal additions of heat as equal elevations of temperature. Variations in the specific heats of bodies have already shown that thermometry under that condition is impossible. That is Black: mercury and water, given the same heat, do not rise equally; ice takes heat and does not rise at all. So Thomson is not cataloguing capacities across substances. Capacity is \(Q\) per degree of an already-given thermometer. He uses Black’s unit of heat — the heat that raises a kilogramme of water by one air-thermometer degree — and then asks a different question: what should a degree be, if it is not an equal expansion of mercury, and cannot be an equal dose of heat? Carnot’s answer: whatever interval makes a perfect mill, run on that unit of heat, always yield the same motive power. Independence of substance here means independence of the thermometer’s fluid and of the engine’s working fluid, not a table of specific heats.

Thomson’s own statement of the characteristic property: all degrees have the same value; a unit of heat descending from a body \(A\) at temperature \(T^\circ\) to a body \(B\) at \((T-1)^\circ\) gives out the same mechanical effect, whatever the number \(T\). That is the mill, turned into a ruler. The working fluid is still caloric. The fall is still a fall, not a conversion. He then compares the new ruler with the air thermometer, using Clapeyron’s formulae and Regnault’s steam data. The two scales are forced to agree at ice and at steam. Everything else is a table of how unequal an air-thermometer degree is, in mill-value, as you go up the scale. Work is how we make the degrees equal. It is not how we make the heat, and it is not a new zero.

The \(-273^\circ\) that looks like absolute zero is already sitting in the air thermometer — the same bulb of air, volume as the reading. If that volume is linear in temperature, pushing the graduation far enough reduces the air to nothing at \(-100/0.366\), which Thomson writes as \(-273^\circ\). He uses that fact only as a reason why Clapeyron’s table was to be expected: an air-thermometer degree is worth more when it is cold, because a finite number of those degrees is trying to reach infinite cold. It is not the zero of his scale, and he does not compute a table down to it. On the mill scale a unit of heat can always be let down one more equal-value degree, and still arrive. Infinite cold is at \(-\infty\). The genuine bottom — a temperature at which a reversible engine would reject no heat — appears only after Joule, when heat is no longer conserved and the two heats stand in a ratio \(Q_h/Q_c = T_h/T_c\). That is 1851, and that is the scale later called Kelvin. We are not there yet. 1848 has done something narrower: it has made temperature a Carnot object. Once Joule breaks conservation of heat, the same object has to be rebuilt from the ratio of the two heats rather than from equal mill-rungs of a conserved fluid.

This is the hinge. Thomson writes that in the present state of science no operation is known by which heat can be absorbed without either raising a temperature or becoming latent. Conversion of heat, or caloric, into mechanical effect is probably impossible, certainly undiscovered. Actual engines must therefore look for their power not in absorption and conversion, but in the transmission of heat. That is Carnot, stated as a fact of 1848. Then the footnote: a contrary opinion has been advocated by Mr Joule of Manchester; remarkable discoveries on the generation of heat by the friction of fluids, and known experiments with magneto-electric machines, seem to indicate an actual conversion of mechanical effect into caloric. No experiment, Thomson adds, is adduced in which the converse is exhibited. Much remains involved in mystery.

Joule and the Mechanical Equivalent

[edit]

James Prescott Joule (1818–1889) gives the answer to Rumford’s riddle. He theorises that a measured quantity of mechanical work could create a measured quantity of heat. If it can then heat is not a conserved fluid. This would imply that Carnot’s mill becomes a special case, not the general law. Joule is a Manchester brewer whoe tries this experiment with a paddle wheel (Joule, 1850).

Figure: James Prescott Joule (1818–1889). Seated portrait, later in life (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).

The family brewery is in Manchester where Joule has a private laboratory. From 1840 he is working to relate heat to electricity and to chemical affinity. In 1843 he argues that the heat from magneto-electricity is proportional to the work absorbed. Mayer, in 1842, has already claimed that agitating water warms it, without a measured force. Joule wants the equivalent.

Figure: Joule’s apparatus for the mechanical equivalent of heat. A falling weight turns a paddle in water; the work is known, the rise in temperature is measured. Engraving from Harper’s New Monthly Magazine, August 1869 (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).

In Joules’ apparatus weights descend and turn a paddle in a can of water. The paddle moves against the water’s resistance. When the weights have run down they are wound up and the motion is repeated. The thermometer is read after multiple descents. The work is easily calculated as the weight (\(\text{mg}) multiplied by the height (\))$. The heat is the rise in temperature times the capacity of the water. Mechanical power is being spent, and heat appears.

At the 1845 British Association meeting Joule exhibits the brass paddle. That run gives about \(890\) foot-pounds per degree per pound of water. He also uses other methods such as magneto-electricity, rarefaction of air, passing water through narrow tubes to give neighbouring figures. His mean estimate is near \(817\). By 1850 the number is settled as \(772.692\) foot-pounds. The scientific world, he notes, preoccupied with the hypothesis that heat is a substance, had almost unanimously denied that fluid friction could generate heat.

The contradiction is the cue, not the resolution. Clausius in 1850, and Thomson in 1851, will keep Carnot’s limit and drop conservation of heat. We take Clausius first: two laws, and \(\mathrm{d}Q/T\). Thomson then returns to rewrite the scale so that it has a bottom.

Clausius and \(\mathrm{d}Q/T\)

[edit]

Heat is not conserved. Carnot’s limit remains. That is the problem Clausius inherits from Joule and Thomson. If the caloric mill is gone, what still closes when a reversible engine runs a cycle? The answer is not a quantity of heat. It is heat divided by temperature. A reversible cycle produces no entropy.

Figure: Rudolf Clausius (1822–1888). Photograph from the AIP Emilio Segrè Visual Archives, Lande Collection (Wikimedia Commons, CC0).

Clausius is a mathematical physicist, first in Berlin, later Zurich, Würzburg and Bonn. In 1850, independently of Thomson’s 1851 dynamical theory, he writes the two laws that keep Joule and Carnot in the same ledger (Clausius, 1850). The first law is Joule’s equivalent made universal: heat is a form of work. The second is what remains of the mill: heat does not pass from cold to hot by itself, and no cyclic engine converts the heat of a single bath entirely into work.

The first law is why \(\oint \mathrm{d}Q\) is not zero for an engine. Some of the heat taken from the hot bath does not arrive at the cold bath; it has become work. That is Joule, inside Carnot’s cycle. The second law is why you still cannot take all of it. There is a remainder that must be rejected to the cold bath, and the size of that remainder, for a reversible engine, depends only on the two temperatures — not on whether the working substance is steam, air, or anything else. That was Carnot’s other claim, and it survives.

Thomson’s 1851 scale is this statement: the absolute temperatures stand in the same ratio as the two heats. Write it the other way up and the books close. The heat that leaves the hot bath, divided by \(T_h\), equals the heat that arrives at the cold bath, divided by \(T_c\). The difference of the heats is the work. The quotient of each heat by its temperature is the same number. That number is the transformation. Clausius’s 1854 paper calls it the equivalence-value of the transformation (Clausius, 1854). We are looking at a two-bath cycle; the same accounting will apply to a loop assembled from many small exchanges.

If every reversible cycle gives zero, the integral of \(\mathrm{d}Q/T\) between two states cannot depend on the reversible path you take. That is the definition of a state function. Clausius writes \[ S - S_0 = \int \frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}. \] Heat \(\mathrm{d}Q\) is not a state function: the heat you put in depends on the path, which is why \(\oint \mathrm{d}Q\) is the work of the engine. Dividing by \(T\) along a reversible path removes that path-dependence. Reversibility is the condition. It means the engine can be run backwards and the baths restored: no finite drops of temperature, no friction, no paddle wheel hidden in the mechanism.

This is the mill, rewritten after Joule. The working fluid comes home. Its entropy comes home with it. The hot bath has lost \(Q_h/T_h\); the cold bath has gained \(Q_c/T_c\); those two are equal. No transformation has been left over. Reversible does not mean ``nothing happened.’’ Work was done. It means the transformation can be undone, and when you close the loop there is no residue.

An irreversible cycle is still a cycle: the working substance returns, so its \(S\) is unchanged. What has changed is the baths. You took \(Q_h\) from the hot bath and, because some of the fall was wasted, you delivered more than the reversible \(Q_c\) to the cold bath. The integral \(\oint \mathrm{d}Q/T\), with \(\mathrm{d}Q\) the heat absorbed by the working substance, is then strictly negative (Clausius, 1854). Equivalently, the entropy lost by the hot bath is less than the entropy gained by the cold bath. The difference was produced. That is the second law as an accounting inequality, not as a prohibition in words.

Figure: Clausius names entropy, 1865. English translation of the memoir: \(\int \mathrm{d}Q/T = S-S_0\), from Greek τροπή, transformation. He forms the word to sit beside energy.

In 1865 he gives the state function a name (Clausius, 1865). Verwandlungsinhalt, the transformation content, would have done; he prefers a Greek root, τροπή, so that the word can pass unchanged between languages, and so that it sits next to energy. The two quantities, he says, are so nearly allied in their physical meanings that a similarity in designation appears desirable. Energy is the first-law account. Entropy is the second-law account. His summary of the two laws: the energy of the universe is constant; the entropy of the universe tends to a maximum.

Clausius did not give the Boltzmann distribution, and he did not make entropy a measure of uncertainty. He gave the macroscopic balance that any later prescription must respect. When we write \(p_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}/Z\) and later derive it from MaxEnt, read it as a statistical answer to a constraint he already framed: fixed mean energy, maximum entropy, no perpetual motion. The historical chain is engines, then the equivalent, then this integral, then statistics, then information.

Thomson Returns: The Scale After Joule

[edit]

Clausius has kept Carnot’s limit and dropped conserved heat. Thomson does the same, independently, a year later. In 1851 he writes the dynamical theory of heat (Thomson, 1851). The mill is still there. The ruler is not. Heat taken in and heat rejected now stand in a ratio of the two temperatures, and for the first time the fall has a bottom.

The 1848 paper had already heard of Joule, in a footnote, and had refused him: conversion of heat into work was probably impossible, certainly undiscovered. The paddle wheel is the experiment Thomson said had not been shown. By 1851 he accepts it. Heat can be generated by work, and some of the heat taken from the hot bath does not arrive at the cold bath — it has become work. Conservation of caloric cannot be the general law. Clausius had already said so. Thomson’s contribution, for us, is what happens to the scale.

In 1848 a degree was a height of fall of a conserved unit of heat: the same mill-work for every rung, whatever \(T\) you started from. After Joule the unit does not arrive. What is equal, for a reversible engine, is \(Q_h/T_h\) and \(Q_c/T_c\). That is Clausius’s entropy leaving the hot bath and arriving at the cold bath. Thomson takes it as the definition of the scale. Ice is no longer the zero. The numbers \(T_h\) and \(T_c\) are the absolute temperatures of the two baths; their ratio is the ratio of the heat taken in to the heat rejected.

This is the genuine floor that 1848 did not have. If \(Q_c/Q_h = T_c/T_h\), then \(T_c=0\) is the temperature at which a reversible engine, taking \(Q_h\) from the hot bath, rejects nothing. The \(-273^\circ\) of Gay-Lussac’s expansion was an accident of air. The zero of the 1851 scale is an accident of engines: it is the temperature you cannot fall below, because there is no heat left to reject. We still use this scale. We name it after the title Thomson later took.

Figure: James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879), Ludwig Boltzmann (1844–1906), Josiah Willard Gibbs (1839–1903). After Clausius the account becomes statistical: components of velocity, then occupation of energy, then free energy.

Clausius gave a macroscopic balance. Maxwell, Boltzmann and Gibbs give a microscopic count of the same \(S\). Maxwell’s first move is the velocity distribution: each Cartesian component is Gaussian, because the kinetic energy is quadratic. That quadratic is the energy whose expectation we will split as \(U=F+TS\).

Maxwell–Boltzmann: Components Versus Speed

[edit]

Write the joint density on Cartesian velocities as isotropic, \[\begin{align} f(\mathbf{v}) = \left(\frac{m}{2\pi kT}\right)^{3/2} \exp\Bigl(-\frac{m|\mathbf{v}|^2}{2kT}\Bigr). \end{align}\] Each marginal \(f(v_x)\) is Gaussian. Change to spherical coordinates with \(v=|\mathbf{v}|\) and solid angle \(\Omega\). Probability conservation uses the volume element \(d^3v = v^2\,dv\,d\Omega\). Integrating over angles (\(\int d\Omega = 4\pi\)) gives the Maxwell speed distribution \[\begin{align} f(v) = 4\pi\left(\frac{m}{2\pi kT}\right)^{3/2} v^2\,e^{-mv^2/2kT}, \qquad v\ge 0. \end{align}\] The factor \(v^2\) is the Jacobian of the change of variables — the density of states of spherical shells in velocity space. Calling ``Maxwell–Boltzmann’’ Gaussian is correct for a component and wrong for speed.

The Gibbs–Boltzmann Distribution

[edit]

Name the object and fix the formula. The MaxEnt derivation that earns these weights is a separate step: deriving the occupation from Lagrange multipliers before Shannon \(H\) and Jaynes would put the cart before the horse.

Write the equilibrium occupation of a discrete system with energies \(\{E_i\}\) as \[ p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z(\beta)}, \qquad Z(\beta)=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}. \] Physicists call \(p_i\) the Boltzmann distribution (or Boltzmann weights) and, for a system exchanging energy with a bath at fixed \(T\), the Gibbs distribution or canonical ensemble. The three names point at the same formula. The normalisation \(Z(\beta)\) is the partition function. Its logarithm \(\log Z(\beta)\) is the cumulant generating function for the energy under this exponential family: derivatives of \(\log Z\) recover the mean energy, the variance (heat capacity, up to factors of \(\beta\)), and higher cumulants. That generating-function reading is why \(Z\) is more than a normalisation constant.

Figure: Occupation of a two-state system as coldness increases. At low \(\beta\) both states are populated; at high \(\beta\) the ground state dominates.

The Gibbs–Boltzmann occupation is not only a statement about gases and magnets. Hopfield networks (Hopfield, 1982) assign an energy to every binary configuration of a recurrent net and treat recall as a descent toward low-energy states — equilibrium statistics are again Gibbs. Ackley, Hinton and Sejnowski (Ackley et al., 1985) made the weights of that energy learnable: a Boltzmann machine is an undirected model whose distribution over configurations is exactly \(p(s)\propto e^{-E(s)/T}\). The 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics, awarded to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton, recognised that physical-systems reading of computation and learning. Name the lineage so the formula does not feel confined to nineteenth-century heat baths; do not divert into training algorithms.

Coldness and Temperature

[edit]

The Boltzmann occupation can be written in two ways, \[ p_i = \frac{e^{-E_i/k_B T}}{Z} = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z}, \qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}. \] The mathematics is the same, but the emphasis is different.

\(T\) is the variable of the bath. It is what a thermometer reports, and it is the intensive parameter in the Helmholtz accounting \(F = U - TS\). In that representation energy comes first and entropy is the correction: \(TS\) is the cut the second law takes from \(U\).

\(\beta\) is coldness. It is the variable conjugate to energy. In that representation entropy comes first: you maximise \(S\) (or write a generating function in \(\beta\)) and energy is the constraint. Temperature is then a derived reading, \(T = 1/k_B\beta = (\partial S/\partial U)^{-1}\).

We will use both. Weeks 1–2 keep \(T\) in view so the bath is familiar, and they already compute in \(\beta\) because that is the natural argument of \(Z\). Week 5 makes the \(\beta\)-first order honest: the Lagrange multiplier on a mean-energy constraint is coldness, and it is the natural parameter of the exponential family. Weeks 6–7 then treat \(\beta\) as a coordinate on that family. A student who still hears \(\beta\) as ``one over the thermometer’’ will miss why the geometry is written in those coordinates.

Free Energy Decomposition

[edit]

Once the Gibbs occupation \[ p(x)=\frac{e^{-\beta E(x)}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_x e^{-\beta E(x)} \] is given, we can derive the macroscopic formulae that characterise the gas. They come to us from Helmholtz. We can compute them through the expectation of that occupation of the given engergies, taken at equilibrium. The relationship between energy and probability is \(p_i\propto e^{-\beta E_i}\). Here we will identify the “inner energy” or “total energy” with \(\left\langle E\right\rangle\) and read off \(F=U-TS\).

Start from the occupation and take the logarithm, \[ \log p(x)=-\beta E(x)-\log Z. \] Note that nothing statistical has happened here, this is an identity for every probability that is a member of the exponential family, which in physics we think of as the Gibbs distribution. It’s valid for every configuration \(x\) that the Gibbs distribution supports. If we solve for the energy of that configuration we can restore temperature through recognising that\(\beta=1/(k_BT)\), \[ E(x)=-k_BT\log p(x)-k_BT\log Z. \] Note that the first term on the right (the surprisal multiplied by the temperature and Boltzmann’s constant) still depends on \(x\). The second does not: \(-k_BT\log Z\) is a property of the whole ensemble, not of a particular microstate.

At equilibrium the macroscopic inner energy can be related to the expectation of the microscopic score under the same distribution, \[ U\equiv\left\langle E\right\rangle=\sum_x p(x)\,E(x). \] This is where the ensemble is entering in physics. The ensemble that comes to us from Boltzmann, Maxwell, Gibbs, and Einstein made. The idea is that the system is not in one configuration. The energy we write in the “thermodynamic ledger” is an average over the different occupations. Substitute the identity for \(E(x)\), \[\begin{align} U &= \sum_x p(x)\bigl(-k_BT\log p(x)-k_BT\log Z\bigr) \nonumber\\ &= -k_BT\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) - k_BT\log Z. \end{align}\] The second term comes out of the sum because \(\log Z\) does not depend on \(x\), and \(\sum_x p(x)=1\).

The two remaining pieces already have names. The configuration-independent term is the Helmholtz free energy, \[ F=-k_BT\log Z, \] it is often known as the “free energy” or “available energy”. It is the energy that we can use for work. The average of \(-\log p\) is the dimensionless Shannon functional \(H=-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)\). Multiplying by Boltzmann’s constant produces physical entropy, \(S=k_B H\), with units of energy per degree. Then \[ U=F+TS\qquad\text{or}\qquad F=U-TS. \] Helmholtz originally wrote \(A=U-TS\), with \(A\) for Arbeit, the work still available. This module uses \(F\) for the same quantity, because week 5 needs \(A(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) for the log-partition of an exponential family. But Helmholtz’s \(A\) and our \(F\) are the same object; \(U\) is the inner energy \(\left\langle E\right\rangle\), not a free energy. The fact that they are different is down to energy that is stored as entropy. It’s in this term that we can find Black’s latent heat.

From the physics and thermodynamics perspective \(U\) is the energy in the system, \(TS\) is the “cut” that entropy takes from that total energy. It is the tied up in the uncertainty of the occupation, unavailable as work. Then \(F\) is what the thermodynamic bath still allows you to do. Ggiven the Gibbs occupation, the macroscopic split \(F=U-TS\) is an identity.

The \(T\)-first reading puts energy first and treats entropy as a correction. The \(\beta\)-first reading treats \(\log Z\) as a generating function: \(U=-\partial_\beta\log Z\) and \(S=\beta(U-F)\), which we compute from \(Z\) later. Both are the identity just derived. Later we will derive the occupation from MaxEnt and identify \(\beta\) as the Lagrange multiplier on the mean-energy constraint; until then the occupation is given and the accounting follows.

Figure: \(U\), \(S\), and \(F\) for the Worksheet 1 three-state system (\(k_B=1\)). The dashed curve is \(-\log Z/\beta\); it sits on \(F\), which is the identity just derived.

The Worksheet 1 three-state system, \(E\in\{0,1,3\}\), is a numerical check of the same algebra with \(k_B=1\). Compute \(p_i=e^{-\beta E_i}/Z\), then \(U=\sum_i p_i E_i\) and \(S=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i\). The two expressions for free energy, \(F=U-S/\beta\) and \(F=-\log Z/\beta\), overlie. If they do not, the signs in the derivation are wrong.

import numpy as np
def gibbs_thermo(energies, beta):
    """U, S, F from a discrete Gibbs occupation ($k_B=1$)."""
    e = np.asarray(energies, dtype=float)
    weights = np.exp(-beta * e)
    Z = weights.sum()
    p = weights / Z
    U = np.sum(p * e)
    S = -np.sum(p * np.log(p))
    F = -np.log(Z) / beta
    return U, S, F, U - S / beta

U, S, F, F_from_US = gibbs_thermo([0.0, 1.0, 3.0], beta=1.0)

Gaussian: Expected Quadratic Energy

[edit]

We’ve already introduced the univariate Gaussian as an energy-based model with quadratic energy. We also looked at animations of elastic collisions and argued that the velocities were given by the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution that has the form of a Gibbs distribution. We can apply the Helmholtz split to this system in closed form. Take \[ E(x)=\frac{x^2}{2} \] and a bath at inverse temperature \(\beta\) (here \(k_B=1\), so \(T=1/\beta\)). The Gibbs density is \[ p(x)=\frac{e^{-\beta x^2/2}}{Z}=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2\pi}}\,\exp\Bigl(-\beta\frac{x^2}{2}\Bigr), \] which is \(\mathcal{N}(0,T)\). The partition function is the Gaussian integral \[ Z=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\beta x^2/2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\beta}}. \]

The inner energy is the expectation of the quadratic, \[ U=\left\langle E\right\rangle=\int p(x)\,\frac{x^2}{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle x^2\right\rangle. \] Under \(p=\mathcal{N}(0,T)\) we have \(\left\langle x^2\right\rangle=T\), so \(U=T/2\). That is the equipartition theorem for a single quadratic degree of freedom: each such term in the energy contributes \(k_BT/2\) at equilibrium. Maxwell’s three Cartesian components — a few slides back — are three independent copies of this quadratic. Kinetic energy \(\tfrac32 k_BT\) is \(d=3\). The Jacobian that produces speed is why the speed density is not itself Gaussian.

The free energy is the generating-function term from the derivation, \[ F=-T\log Z=-T\log\sqrt{2\pi T}=-\frac{T}{2}\log(2\pi T). \] Entropy follows from \(S=(U-F)/T\), \[ S=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi T). \] That is the differential entropy of \(\mathcal{N}(0,T)\), in nats, with \(k_B=1\). Here \(S\) is simply the name we gave to \(-k_B\left\langle\log p\right\rangle\) when we took the expectation of \(E(x)\). The continuous functional can be negative and is not invariant to a change of units. The algebra we need is already visible: \[ F+TS=-\frac{T}{2}\log(2\pi T)+T\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi T)\Bigr)=\frac{T}{2}=U. \] The \(\tfrac12\) in \(U\) and the \(\tfrac12\) sitting in \(T\cdot\tfrac12\) — the \(e\) inside \(\tfrac12\log(2\pi e T)\) — cancel, leaving \(F=-T\log Z\). Spreading the Gaussian (raising \(T\)) does not change the shape of the well \(E(x)=x^2/2\); it changes how far the occupation climbs the walls, and therefore how much of \(U\) is locked in \(TS\).

Figure: The energy is the same parabola at every temperature. Raising \(T\) spreads \(p=\mathcal{N}(0,T)\) and the occupation climbs the walls: \(\left\langle E\right\rangle=T/2\) rises linearly.

Figure: Closed form for \(E(x)=x^2/2\) with \(k_B=1\). Inner energy \(U=T/2\) is the straight line; \(TS\) takes a growing cut; \(F=U-TS\) is negative and decreases.

import numpy as np
def gaussian_quadratic_thermo(T):
    """U, S, F for $E(x)=x^2/2$ at temperature $T$ ($k_B=1$)."""
    U = T / 2.0
    Z = np.sqrt(2.0 * np.pi * T)
    F = -T * np.log(Z)
    S = (U - F) / T
    return U, S, F

T = 1.0

If the energy is a sum of independent quadratics, \(E(\mathbf{x})=\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^d x_i^2\), the partition function factors and \[ U=\frac{d\,T}{2},\qquad Z=(2\pi T)^{d/2},\qquad F=-\frac{d T}{2}\log(2\pi T). \] Each coordinate contributes its own \(T/2\) to the inner energy and its own \(\tfrac12\log(2\pi e T)\) to the entropy. That is why an ideal gas of \(N\) particles in three dimensions has kinetic energy \(\tfrac32 N k_BT\): \(3N\) quadratic terms in the Hamiltonian, each holding \(k_BT/2\). The derivation did not change. We took the expectation of a bigger \(E\).

The air thermometer is a bulb of gas whose volume, at constant pressure, is the reading. Thomson already had Gay-Lussac’s wall: if that volume is linear in temperature it vanishes at about \(-273^\circ\). The quadratic well we just split has no box, so it cannot be that thermometer. Maxwell’s three Cartesian components are three copies of \(E=v_i^2/2\): the kinetic half of an ideal-gas particle is already on the board. What is missing is the configuration integral. A free particle in a container of volume \(V\) has, up to constants of mass and \(h\) that we do not need, \[ Z_1 = V\,(2\pi T)^{3/2}. \] The Gaussian integrals we just evaluated are the momentum half; \(V\) is the position half. For \(N\) particles, \(Z \propto V^N T^{3N/2}\), so \(F=-T\log Z\) depends on \(V\). Pressure is a Helmholtz derivative, not a new potential: \[ P=-\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{T,N}=\frac{NT}{V}. \] At constant \(P\), \(V\propto T\). Gay-Lussac’s wall is \(T=0\) on the same scale whose variance we just read off Maxwell’s Gaussians. Thomson in 1848 treated \(-273^\circ\) as an accident of air. After Joule it is the floor of the engine scale; after Maxwell it is also the temperature at which those Gaussians have collapsed to a delta, and a finite pressure would require zero volume.

We do not need the Gibbs free energy \(G=F+PV\) for this. \(G\) is the potential when pressure is the control variable. Charles’s law is already in \(PV=NT\). Coupled multivariate Gaussians are not required either: independent coordinates suffice, and that is what Maxwell wrote. The one extra Helmholtz fact is the derivative with respect to \(V\). We name it. We do not compute Stirling, \(h^3\), or chemical potential today.

After Helmholtz: The Entropy Game

The physics story has arrived at \(F=U-TS\). What follows is the information reading that Week 2 did not reach: guessing, MacKay’s weighing problem, and die coarse-graining. Grouping is the live close; the chain-rule / Shannon axiom block is parked in Week 4.

The Information Game

[edit]

Before writing a formula for entropy, play a game. One player thinks of an object drawn from a known finite list. The other may ask only yes/no questions. How many questions do you need in the worst case? That count is already a measure of information: it is how much uncertainty you must remove to name the object.

With a list of size \(N\) and equal prior probability on each item, an ideal strategy halves the search space at every step. After \(k\) answers you can distinguish at most \(2^k\) hypotheses, so you need \[ k \ge \log_2 N \] questions in the worst case. Twenty questions reach a million possibilities: \(2^{20} = 1\,048\,576\). A fair yes/no answer therefore carries one bit of information. That is Shannon’s unit before Shannon’s formula: information as the number of balanced binary distinctions required to resolve uncertainty.

If the prior is not uniform, the best question is no longer a literal half-split of the count of remaining objects. It is a half-split of their probability. The average number of questions under optimal play becomes the Shannon entropy \(H = -\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i\). We will meet that formula next; the game already shows what the number is counting.

MacKay’s Balance and Balls

[edit]

David MacKay opens the compression half of Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms with a weighing puzzle (MacKay, 2003, p. Exercise~4.1). State the puzzle first. Do not yet say that the instrument is ternary. The information-game habit is to ask how we should proceed — what we must name — and then to try the instrument as if it had two readings, like a yes/no question. Keep the mechanism (what we put on the pans) on a different slide from the maths (how many bits that use can return). Only after that sequence do we notice that the balance has a third reading.

Figure: A two-pan balance and twelve identical balls.

You are given twelve balls, identical in appearance. Exactly one differs in weight from the others; you do not know whether that odd ball is heavier or lighter. A two-pan balance compares two equal-sized groups. Design a strategy that finds the odd ball and the direction of the defect.

Resist jumping to a particular grouping. Count first: how many states of the world. Then try the instrument as a two-outcome device, because that is the game we already know. Keep what we put on the pans separate from the information budget those pans can return.

Under a uniform prior over those twenty-four possibilities, the Shannon uncertainty we must remove is \[ H_{\mathrm{start}} = \log_2 24 \approx 4.58 \text{ bits}. \] At the end we need a single named hypothesis: \(H_{\mathrm{end}} = 0\). The deficit to close is therefore about \(4.58\) bits. That difference — start minus end — is the information the experiment must supply. We have not yet said what the balance returns.

Try Two Outcomes

A two-outcome instrument, used optimally, returns at most \(\log_2 2 = 1\) bit per measurement. The information budget then says \[ 2^4 = 16 < 24 \le 32 = 2^5, \] so five uses can suffice on paper and four cannot. Whether a physical balance achieves that one bit is a different question: it depends on what we put on the pans.

\[ \log_2 24 \approx 4.58,\qquad 2^4 = 16 < 24 \le 32 = 2^5 \]

Write two columns if you can: left, the information budget; right, the pans. The left column does not know about equal group sizes, leftover balls, or borrowing a ball of known mass. Those constraints live on the right. The rest of this block is the right-hand column, scored afterwards by the left.

The move 6 vs 6 is the binary-search instinct. If we already knew the odd ball was heavier, it would be a perfect split of twelve balls. We do not know that. A down-left reading means ``one of the left six is heavy, or one of the right six is light’’: twelve of the original twenty-four hypotheses remain. One bit toward the 24, and no ball has been eliminated. Mechanism and maths have already come apart: the pans moved, the pile of balls did not shrink.

The information gain can be perfect for the two-outcome model — an even split of 24 — while the mechanism fails to name a set of six suspect balls. What we carry forward is six maybe-heavy and six maybe-light. If we now pretend it was heavier and keep only the down pan, we have thrown away half of the remaining hypotheses by assumption, not by measurement.

This is the sequence people sketch on the board: 6 vs 6, then 3 vs 3. It is the right mechanism for a different puzzle — twelve balls, one heavier. Hold that sequence, because the next move is where the pans refuse to cooperate.

With three suspects we have two mechanical choices. Weigh 1 vs 1 and leave one off: the left-off ball is the odd one if the pans balance. That reading is not in the two-outcome story we are trying to tell. Or weigh 2 vs 2 by padding with a ball already known to be normal: A,B against C plus a normal. The pans are now full, so in the heavier-only story they must tip, and we have not used a third symbol. We have changed the split.

\[ \left(\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{1}{3}\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad H = h_2\!\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right) \approx 0.92 < 1 \text{ bit} \]

If A,B,C are the three remaining heavier-suspects and N is known normal, the reading A,B down leaves two hypotheses and has probability \(2/3\); C,N down names C and has probability \(1/3\). Binary entropy \(h_2(2/3)\approx 0.92\) bits. The deficit \(1-0.92\) is exactly the price of the mechanical constraint ``equal numbers on the pans, and no third reading’’. We did not lose those bits because the mathematics of two outcomes changed. We lost them because the mechanism could not realise an equiprobable two-way split of three objects.

1 vs 1 of two remaining heavier-suspects returns the missing bit: the down pan is the odd ball, if the heavier-side story is true. The sequence of acts was 6 vs 6, 3 vs 3, 2 vs 2 with a known, then 1 vs 1. The sequence of gains was 1, 1, \(0.92\), 1 — already not four bits. And this named a ball only inside the heavier-side story.

To finish the original task we must name the direction as well as the ball. The obvious mechanism is candidate versus known normal. If the two-outcome story were true, this use would return the last bit: heavy or light. But if we followed the down pan all the way and the odd ball was actually light, the candidate may be normal — and then the pans balance. The third reading has been sitting in the mechanism whenever a known ball appears. We cannot keep it out without lying about what the device does. That is the cue to abandon the two-outcome model, not to patch it again.

The moral is not that the binary budget is wrong. It is that an information budget is silent about pans. Equal group sizes, leftover balls, and borrowed normals are constraints on the channel we actually have. Score every weighing twice: what the device did, then how many bits that split was worth.

Three Readings

A two-pan balance has a ternary alphabet. The reading ``level’’ is not an annoyance to be designed out; it is a symbol that can carry information. The first move of the two-outcome story, 6 vs 6, sets the probability of that symbol to zero: with exactly one odd ball among the twelve, the pans cannot balance. We spent a mechanism choice to turn a three-outcome device into a two-outcome device, and then paid further mechanism taxes (the known ball) to keep the third symbol from coming back.

\[ \log_2 3 \approx 1.58,\qquad 3^2 = 9 < 24 \le 27 = 3^3 \]

A binary question yields at most one bit. If the three readings are equiprobable, one weighing contributes \[ \log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits}. \] After \(k\) weighings the strategy tree has at most \(3^k\) leaves, so it can name at most \(3^k\) hypotheses. Compare budgets: \[ \begin{align*} 3^2 &= 9 < 24,\\ 3^3 &= 27 \ge 24. \end{align*} \] Two weighings cannot suffice no matter how clever the groupings. Three weighings can: there is spare capacity of three unused leaves (\(27-24=3\)). In bits, \[ 3\log_2 3 \approx 4.75 > \log_2 24 \approx 4.58. \] The gap is small. That is why the puzzle feels tight. It is the same comparison we already made with \(\log_2 2\), with a larger per-use budget.

The first weighing that realises the ternary budget puts four against four and leaves four off. Level then means the odd ball is in the four left off; a tip couples a group of four with a direction, as 6 vs 6 did, but now the third symbol is alive. We do not give the rest of the tree. The design principle is the one already present in the information game: choose each measurement so that its outcomes are as nearly equiprobable as possible under the current posterior. That maximises expected information gain per weighing and is what makes the \(3^3 \ge 24\) budget achievable in practice. Adding a known-normal ball, or arranging a weighing that can never balance, is the same kind of mechanism tax we already computed for \(h_2(2/3)\).

We stop at the existence argument. Constructing the adaptive tree — which balls on which pan at each step — is MacKay’s exercise, and solving it is the best way to feel what ``informative experiment’’ means. What the lecture owns is the split between ledgers: \(\log_2 24\) versus \(\log_2 2\) or \(\log_2 3\) is the information account; 6 vs 6, 4 vs 4, and padding with a known ball are the mechanical account. The two are not the same, and the known-ball tax is the exhibit.

Coarse-Graining a Die

[edit]

Take a fair six-sided die, \(X\in\{1,\ldots,6\}\) with \(p(x)=1/6\), so \[\begin{align} H(X)=\log_2 6 = 1 + \log_2 3 \approx 2.585 \text{ bits}. \end{align}\] Ask questions in two stages: first learn which group the face falls in, then resolve the face inside the group.

\[ H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})=1 \text{ bit}, \qquad H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}})=\log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits} \]

Let \(G_{\mathrm{parity}}=0\) on even faces and \(1\) on odd. Then \(w=(1/2,1/2)\), so \(H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})=1\). Conditional on the group, three faces remain, each with probability \(1/3\), so \(H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}})=\log_2 3\). The stages add: \[\begin{align} H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})+H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}}) = 1+\log_2 3 = \log_2 6 = H(X). \end{align}\]

\[ w=\Bigl(\tfrac16,\tfrac56\Bigr), \qquad H(G_{\mathrm{six}})=h_2\Bigl(\tfrac16\Bigr)\approx 0.65 \text{ bits} \]

Let \(G_{\mathrm{six}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{X=6\}}\). The group weights are unbalanced, \(w=(1/6,5/6)\), so the binary entropy of the first answer is \[\begin{align} H(G_{\mathrm{six}}) = h_2\Bigl(\tfrac16\Bigr) = -\tfrac16\log_2\tfrac16-\tfrac56\log_2\tfrac56 \approx 0.650 \text{ bits}, \end{align}\] one bit. After a yes'' the face is known ($H=0$). After ano’’ five faces remain (\(H=\log_2 5\)). Averaging, \[\begin{align} H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{six}}) = \tfrac16\cdot 0 + \tfrac56\log_2 5 \approx 1.935 \text{ bits}. \end{align}\] Again \(H(G_{\mathrm{six}})+H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{six}})=H(X)\). Note that ``\(\ge 4\)?’’ on a fair die is also a balanced cut (\(\{1,2,3\}\) vs \(\{4,5,6\}\)), so it is one bit — the unbalanced example has to be chosen carefully.

Grouping Composition

[edit]

\[ H(X)=H(w)+\sum_i w_i\, H(p_i) \]

Partition the outcomes of \(X\) into groups labelled by \(i\), with group probabilities \(w_i\) and within-group distributions \(p_i\). Then \[\begin{align} H(X)=H(w)+\sum_i w_i\, H(p_i). \end{align}\] The die examples are the case check: \(H(w)\) is the entropy of the coarse question; \(\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) is what remains once the group is known. If \(G=g(X)\) is the group label, this identity is exactly the chain rule \[\begin{align} H(X)=H(G)+H(X\mid G), \end{align}\] because \(G\) is a function of \(X\) (so \(H(G\mid X)=0\)) and \(H(X\mid G)=\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\).

In the reverse direction, observing \(X\) fixes \(G\) with no leftover uncertainty. The mutual information \(I(X;G)=H(G)-H(G\mid X)=H(G)\) is exactly the price of the coarse question. After you know the group, the remaining work is \(H(X\mid G)\) — which of the fine outcomes inside that group. Coarse-graining keeps \(H(G)\) as your description and discards \(H(X\mid G)\): \[\begin{align} \text{information lost by coarse-graining} = H(X\mid G) = \sum_i w_i\, H(p_i). \end{align}\]

The die paid \(\log_2 6\) whichever cut came first. That invariance under regrouping is not an accident of base-two logarithms; it is part of what characterises Shannon entropy among candidate uncertainty measures (Khinchin-information57?). Categorical accounts make the same demand precise (Baez-entropy11?). For this lecture it is enough that staged questions add, so coarse-graining has a well-defined price.

Coarse-Graining as Abstraction

[edit]

Daniel Bernoulli did not integrate Laplace’s curve for every corpuscle. He replaced the swarm with a coarse mechanical story that still predicts pressure — exactly keeping a group-level description and discarding within-group path detail. Thermodynamic macrostates are the same move with better bookkeeping: the entropy \(S\) is the uncertainty still unresolved inside the macrostate. In intelligent systems the hierarchy is familiar: raw observations are partitioned into features, features into concepts. At each level you retain \(H(w)\) for the labels you use and lose \(\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) for the distinctions you refuse to make. Which partition you choose is Jaynes’ and Wigner’s anthropomorphic choice of degrees of freedom — coming next in this review.

Static Bookkeeping Versus a Physical Ensemble

[edit]

The decomposition \(H=H(w)+\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\) is pure bookkeeping on a single distribution. The die roll has no temporal dynamics because we used the identity only as accounting. Interpreting \(p\) as ``the distribution of the system’s state’’ adds a dynamical hypothesis: something like stationarity plus ergodicity, so that an ensemble average equals what one trajectory shows over long time. Equilibrium thermodynamics makes the same move. The equilibrium density is stationary under the microscopic dynamics, and thermodynamic entropy is then a time-independent number of the form \[\begin{align} S = H(w) + \sum_i w_i H(\pi_i) \end{align}\] when each group has relaxed to its own conditional stationary law \(\pi_i\) and only the coarse weights remain to carry slow change. Dynamics and groups — local equilibrium, lumpability, broken ergodicity, and the decay of KL — return in weeks 3–4 and 8.
