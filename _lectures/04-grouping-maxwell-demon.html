Questions, a Balance, and a Die

[edit]

The Information Game

[edit]

The information game is the following idea. One player thinks of an object drawn from a known finite list. For example, “I’m thinking of an animal.” The other may ask only yes/no questions. For example “Does your animal have four legs?” If we assume that the choice is uniform across that list, and if we can ask optimal questions, what number of questions do you need to guarantee the answer? How does information theory help us with this?

Figure: A balanced yes/no tree for four animals. Each question halves the remaining list, so two answers name one of \(2^2=4\) hypotheses.

With a list of size \(N\) and equal prior probability on each item, an ideal strategy halves the search space at every step. After \(k\) answers you can distinguish at most \(2^k\) hypotheses, so you need \[ k \ge \log_2 N \] questions in the worst case. Sometimes the game is called “twenty questions.” Twenty questions would reach a million possibilities: \(2^{10}=1024\approx 10^3\), so \(2^{20}=2^{10}\times 2^{10}\approx 10^6\). Exactly \(2^{20}=1\,048\,576\). A problem that drives an equiprobable yes/no answer delivers to you one bit of information. That is the most information you can gain at ech turn. This gives us another interpretation of Shannon’s bit, it is the amount of information you gain if you manage to answer a balanced yes/no or true/false question.

If the prior is not uniform, the best question is no longer a literal half-split of the count of remaining objects. It is a half-split of their probability. The average number of questions under optimal play becomes the Shannon entropy \(H = -\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i\).

MacKay’s Balance and Balls

[edit]

David MacKay opens the compression half of Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms with a weighing puzzle (MacKay, 2003, p. Exercise~4.1).

Figure: A two-pan balance and twelve identical balls.

You are given twelve balls, identical in appearance. Exactly one differs in weight from the others; you do not know whether that odd ball is heavier or lighter. You are given a two-pan balance. Design a strategy that finds the odd ball and the direction of the defect.

How many outcomes does that give us? How much information can we get per measurement? And how many measurements do we (therefore) require?

Under a uniform prior over those twenty-four possibilities, the Shannon uncertainty we must remove is \[ H_{\text{start}} = \log_2 24 \approx 4.58 \text{ bits}. \] At the end we would like a single hypothesis: \(H_{\mathrm{end}} = 0\). This means that the information deficit is around \(4.58\) bits. We need to design a set of experiments that will supply this information.

Try Two Outcomes

A two-outcome instrument, used optimally, returns at most \(\log_2 2 = 1\) bit per measurement. The information budget then says \[ 2^4 = 16 < 24 \le 32 = 2^5, \] so five weighings would work, but four cannot. Whether we can organise the physical balance to achieve that one bit per measurement is a separate question and associated with the mechanism.

\[ \log_2 24 \approx 4.58,\qquad 2^4 = 16 < 24 \le 32 = 2^5 \]

Write two columns if you can: left, the information budget; right, the pans. The left column does not know about equal group sizes, leftover balls, or borrowing a ball of known mass. Those constraints live on the right. The rest of this block is the right-hand column, scored afterwards by the left.

The move 6 vs 6 is a binary-search instinct that comes to us from the information game. If we already knew the odd ball was heavier, it would be a perfect split of twelve balls. We do not know that. A down-left reading means "one of the left six is heavy, or one of the right six is light’’: twelve of the original twenty-four hypotheses remain. One bit toward the 24, and no ball has been eliminated.

Three Readings

A two-pan balance has a ternary alphabet, the level reading can also give us information. This is an instrument that can give us \(\log_2 3\) bits of information per measurement. By starting with measuring 6 vs 6 we set the probability of a level answer to zero: with exactly one odd ball among the twelve, the pans cannot balance.

\[ \log_2 3 \approx 1.58,\qquad 3^2 = 9 < 24 \le 27 = 3^3 \]

A binary question yields at most one bit. If the three readings are equiprobable, one weighing contributes \[ \log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits}. \] After \(k\) weighings the strategy tree has at most \(3^k\) leaves, so it can name at most \(3^k\) hypotheses. Compare budgets: \[ \begin{align*} 3^2 &= 9 < 24,\\ 3^3 &= 27 \ge 24. \end{align*} \] Two weighings cannot suffice no matter how clever the groupings. Three weighings can: there is spare capacity of three unused leaves (\(27-24=3\)). In bits, \[ 3\log_2 3 \approx 4.75 > \log_2 24 \approx 4.58. \] The gap is small. That is why the puzzle feels tight. It is the same comparison we already made with \(\log_2 2\), with a larger per-use budget.

Coarse-Graining a Die

[edit]

Take a fair six-sided die, \(X\in\{1,\ldots,6\}\) with \(p(x)=1/6\), so \[\begin{align} H(X)=\log_2 6 = 1 + \log_2 3 \approx 2.585 \text{ bits}. \end{align}\] In the information game we may ask any yes/no we can name. The pans already showed that a physical instrument will not always realise the split the budget wants. The cube is the same lesson on a smaller object. Its sample space has six atoms, not \(2^k\). A grouping is a partition the mechanism affords — looking at the face through some physical cut. The lead cut combines \(\{1,2,3\}\) against \(\{4,5,6\}\). Odd versus even is not the next question inside that grouping: it is a different cut of the original six. After a \(3\)–\(3\) split, three faces remain. Three cannot be split by a fair yes/no, just as three remaining balls could not sit evenly on the pans. Nested 1-bit questions would need a power of two — an eight-sided die, which would still live in the game’s world. This cube does not.

\[ H(G_{\text{low}})=1 \text{ bit}, \qquad H(X\mid G_{\text{low}})=\log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits} \]

Let \(G_{\mathrm{low}}=0\) on \(\{1,2,3\}\) and \(1\) on \(\{4,5,6\}\). Then \(w=(1/2,1/2)\), so \(H(G_{\mathrm{low}})=1\). Conditional on the group, three faces remain, each with probability \(1/3\), so \(H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{low}})=\log_2 3\). The stages add: \[\begin{align} H(G_{\mathrm{low}})+H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{low}}) = 1+\log_2 3 = \log_2 6 = H(X). \end{align}\] The first answer names the group. The residual \(\log_2 3\) is the information the mechanism threw away by combining three faces into one physical cut. That residual is not a second 1-bit question the cube still owes us.

\[ h_2\Bigl(\tfrac13\Bigr)\approx 0.92 < 1 \text{ bit} \]

Suppose we have learned \(G_{\mathrm{low}}=0\), so the face is in \(\{1,2,3\}\). Asking odd or even now splits \(\{1,3\}\) against \(\{2\}\). The answers are not equiprobable: \(w=(2/3,1/3)\), so the question is worth \(h_2(1/3)\approx 0.92\) bits, not one bit. That is the same tax as three remaining balls padded with a known normal. Successive balanced 1-bit questions form a binary tree of equal splits. That tree has \(2^k\) leaves. A six-sided die cannot sit on it, because the instrument has six faces. An eight-sided die can: first \(\{1,2,3,4\}\) versus \(\{5,6,7,8\}\), then a \(2\)–\(2\) split inside the named half, then a \(1\)–\(1\) split — three fair coins, \(H=\log_2 8=3\) bits exactly. That object would still live in the information game. This cube does not. So we do not invent a nested question the mechanism cannot ask. We go back to all six faces and name a different grouping the cube does afford.

\[ H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})=1 \text{ bit}, \qquad H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}})=\log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits} \]

Let \(G_{\mathrm{parity}}=0\) on even faces and \(1\) on odd, as a partition of the original six, not of a group we already named. Then \(w=(1/2,1/2)\), so \(H(G_{\mathrm{parity}})=1\), and \(H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{parity}})=\log_2 3\) as before. The stages add to \(\log_2 6\) again. Low/high and odd/even are two equally good 1-bit groupings the cube affords. They keep different distinctions. They do not nest, because the instrument is not a free questioner. Asking both would give four cells \(\{1,3\}\), \(\{2\}\), \(\{5\}\), \(\{4,6\}\) — not four equiprobable atoms, and not two bits. Which grouping you choose is which physical cut you look through. The next snippet will name that cut as an abstraction. The point today is that mechanism, not the information budget, chose the menu.

\[ w=\Bigl(\tfrac16,\tfrac56\Bigr), \qquad H(G_{\mathrm{six}})=h_2\Bigl(\tfrac16\Bigr)\approx 0.65 \text{ bits} \]

Let \(G_{\mathrm{six}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{X=6\}}\). The group weights are unbalanced, \(w=(1/6,5/6)\), so the binary entropy of the first answer is \[\begin{align} H(G_{\mathrm{six}}) = h_2\Bigl(\tfrac16\Bigr) = -\tfrac16\log_2\tfrac16-\tfrac56\log_2\tfrac56 \approx 0.650 \text{ bits}, \end{align}\] one bit. After a yes'' the face is known ($H=0$). After ano’’ five faces remain (\(H=\log_2 5\)). Averaging, \[\begin{align} H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{six}}) = \tfrac16\cdot 0 + \tfrac56\log_2 5 \approx 1.935 \text{ bits}. \end{align}\] Again \(H(G_{\mathrm{six}})+H(X\mid G_{\mathrm{six}})=H(X)\). The low/high cut is one bit because the two groups are equiprobable. The six-or-not cut is not.

Grouping Composition

[edit]

Often we do not need to know the exact outcome. If all I care about is whether the die shows five or above, I can stop once that is settled. If I need a fair 50/50 decision and only have a die, I can call \(\{1,2,3\}\) “low” and \(\{4,5,6\}\) “high”; the die, coarse-grained, is a fair coin. In both cases we have replaced the fine variable \(X\) (the face) with a coarse variable \(G=g(X)\) (the group), and \(G\) has its own entropy: \(H(G)=\log_2 2 = 1\) bit for the coin, and \(H(G)=H(\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{1}{3})\approx 0.918\) bits for “five or above.” That number is the uncertainty in the coarse-grained state, and it is all we pay if we stop there.

Figure: Two coarse-grainings of a fair die. The top level is the coarse question, with branch widths set by the group probabilities \(w_i\); the bottom level is the residual detail. Stopping at the squares costs \(H(G)\). Going all the way down costs \(H(G)+H(X\mid G)\), which is \(\log_2 6\) for either cut.

\[ H(X)=\underbrace{H(w)}_{\text{coarse state}}+\underbrace{\sum_i w_i\, H(p_i)}_{\text{residual detail}} \]

Partition the outcomes of \(X\) into groups labelled by \(i\), with group probabilities \(w_i\) and within-group distributions \(p_i\). Then \[\begin{align} H(X)=H(w)+\sum_i w_i\, H(p_i). \end{align}\] The first term is the entropy of the coarse-grained state; the second is the average uncertainty left once the group is known. For “five or above,” \(H(w)=H(\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{1}{3})\approx 0.918\) and the residual is \(\tfrac{2}{3}\log_2 4+\tfrac{1}{3}\log_2 2\approx 1.667\); for the coin, \(1+\log_2 3\). Both sum to \(\log_2 6\). Different cuts split the bill differently, but the bill is the same.

If \(G=g(X)\) is the group label, the identity is exactly the chain rule \[\begin{align} H(X)=H(G)+H(X\mid G), \end{align}\] because \(G\) is a function of \(X\) (so \(H(G\mid X)=0\)) and \(H(X\mid G)=\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\).

Figure: The entropy budget of a die under different coarse-grainings. Each bar is a sequence of questions, each segment the entropy paid at that stage. The third bar refines “five or above” into pairs \(\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\{5,6\}\) and then into faces. You may stop after any segment; the cost so far is the entropy of the coarse-grained state you have reached.

Coarse-grainings can themselves be coarse-grained. Refine “five or above” (\(G\)) into pairs \(G'\in\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\{5,6\}\}\) and then into faces. Each stage is a grouping of the next, so the chain rule applies at every level: \[\begin{align} H(X)=H(G)+H(G'\mid G)+H(X\mid G'). \end{align}\] Numerically \(0.918+0.667+1=\log_2 6\). The middle term is \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) because “below five” still has two pairs to choose between (1 bit) while “five or above” is already a single pair (0 bits). The practical reading: the entropy of whatever coarse-grained state you stop at is the running total up to that point, \(H(G')=H(G)+H(G'\mid G)\), and refining further simply adds the next term. Nothing is double-counted and nothing is lost.

In the reverse direction, observing \(X\) fixes \(G\) with no leftover uncertainty. The mutual information \(I(X;G)=H(G)-H(G\mid X)=H(G)\) is exactly the price of the coarse question. After you know the group, the remaining work is \(H(X\mid G)\) — which of the fine outcomes inside that group. Coarse-graining keeps \(H(G)\) as your description and discards \(H(X\mid G)\): \[\begin{align} \text{information lost by coarse-graining} = H(X\mid G) = \sum_i w_i\, H(p_i). \end{align}\] Using the die as a coin keeps one bit and throws away \(\log_2 3\).

Every bar in the budget figure ends at \(\log_2 6\), whichever cut came first and however many stages we used. That invariance under regrouping is not an accident of base-two logarithms; it is part of what characterises Shannon entropy among candidate uncertainty measures (Khinchin, 1957). Categorical accounts make the same demand precise (Baez et al., 2011). For this lecture it is enough that staged questions add, so a coarse-grained state has a well-defined entropy and coarse-graining has a well-defined price.

Grouping Is a Choice of Abstraction

[edit]

The idealised information game fails when we turn to the mechanism. But we do have some control. We can coarse-grain, and reduce the search space. For example, the low/high grouping is a choice of abstraction. We keep a two-valued group label and throw away which of the three faces occurred. Coarse graining in thermodynamics can be seen similarly. We cannot track Laplace’s curves for every molecule, instead we keep macroscopic numbers. Those macroscopic numbers split into extensive quantities that scale with the system (e.g. \(N\), \(V\), energy, entropy) and intensive quantities that do not (e.g. temperature, pressure). What we’ve seen is that these entropies can compose, so that the coarse-graining is coming from the form of entropy (as a function). This means that accounting for information works through the groupings, if the macrostate is \(H(w)\); the unresolved motion inside it is \(\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\).

Entropy of a Gaussian Velocity Histogram

[edit]

Histograms as Riemann Sums

Approximating a density by its value at the centre of each bin is a piecewise-constant (midpoint-rule) approximation to the integral. We call the corresponding sums Riemann sums. Writing \(p_i\approx f(x_i)\Delta\) inside the entropy gives \[\begin{align} H = -\sum_i f_i\Delta\log f_i - \log\Delta\sum_i f_i\Delta \approx h - \log\Delta, \end{align}\] with both sums converging to integrals as \(\Delta\to 0\).

If we sample isotropic three-dimensional velocities and histogram one Cartesian component with equal-width bins. The discrete Shannon entropy of the occupancy is \[\begin{align} H = -\sum_i p_i\log p_i, \qquad p_i = n_i/N. \end{align}\] Note that empty bins don’t contribute to the entropy. For a smooth density and a fine equal-width partition, \(p_i\approx f(x_i)\Delta\) and \[\begin{align} H \approx h - \log \Delta, \qquad h = \tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2). \end{align}\] The figure below reports both numbers. Note that they agree up to sampling and tail truncation. From this we can see that the differential entropy, \(h\), is the \(\Delta\)-independent remainder. It is not the entropy of the histogram.

Figure: Equal-width histogram of one Cartesian velocity component. Discrete \(H\) matches \(h-\log\Delta\); \(h\) itself is the remainder after the bin-width term is subtracted.

Pushed Bins Keep the Same Entropy

[edit]

Discrete Shannon entropy depends only on the probability masses of a partition. Keep the cells and relabel the axis: the masses \(\{p_i\}\) are unchanged, so \(H\) is unchanged, while a density histogram \(p_i/\Delta_i\) changes because the widths change.

The Maxwell speed map \(s=|\mathbf{v}|\) is not itself 1–1 — equal Cartesian cubes that sit on one spherical shell are a grouping, and grouping drops entropy. The figure avoids that. Partition speed by equal Cartesian volume, i.e. equal-width bins in \(u=s^3\). The map \(u\mapsto s=u^{1/3}\) is strictly increasing on \(s\ge 0\). Pushing the bin edges stretches them by \[\begin{align} \Delta s_i \approx \frac{1}{3s_i^2}\,\Delta u. \end{align}\] Drawn as probability bars the occupancy is invariant. Drawn as a density against \(s\) the bars change shape. The two pictures share one \(H\).

Figure: Equal-volume shells in 3D velocity, written two ways. Left: probability masses. Right: the same masses as a density on the speed axis. The bins change shape; \(H\) does not.

The Continuous Limit Drops \(\log\Delta\)

[edit]

Refine the equal-width partition of a Gaussian component. For each bin width \(\Delta\), \[\begin{align} H(X^\Delta) = -\sum_i p_i\log p_i \approx h - \log\Delta. \end{align}\] Plotting \(H\) against \(-\log\Delta\) gives a line of slope one, offset by the constant \(h=\tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)\). The discrete entropy diverges as the bins shrink because each mass \(p_i\approx f\Delta\) contributes an extra \(-\log\Delta\). Differential entropy is defined to be the \(\Delta\)-independent remainder. The continuous functional therefore ignores a term that is driving to \(+\infty\). That is why \(h\) can be negative, and why it is not a code-length bound.

Figure: Discrete entropy of an equal-width Gaussian histogram against \(-\log\Delta\). The points track \(h-\log\Delta\); \(h\) is the finite part. The continuous limit discards a term that diverges.

Entropy of the Maxwell Speed Law

[edit]

The same isotropic Gaussian sample has speed \(s=|\mathbf{v}|\) with density \[\begin{align} f(s) = 4\pi s^2 \bigl(2\pi\sigma^2\bigr)^{-3/2} e^{-s^2/2\sigma^2} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\frac{s^2}{\sigma^3}\,e^{-s^2/2\sigma^2}, \qquad s\ge 0. \end{align}\] That is the Maxwell speed law. Its differential entropy is \[\begin{align} h(s)=\log\bigl(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\bigr)+\gamma-\tfrac12, \end{align}\] where \(\gamma\) is Euler’s constant. A single Cartesian component has \(h(v_x)=\tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)\). For \(\sigma=1\) these are approximately \(0.996\) and \(1.419\) nats. They disagree because they are entropies of different random variables, each written against Lebesgue measure in its own coordinate. An equal-\(\Delta s\) histogram of the sampled speeds tracks \(h(s)-\log\Delta s\), just as the Gaussian histogram tracked \(h(v_x)-\log\Delta v\).

Figure: Equal-width histogram of speed from the same 3D Gaussian sample. Discrete \(H\) tracks \(h(s)-\log\Delta\). The Maxwell \(h(s)\) is not the Gaussian \(h(v_x)\).

Two Discretisations of the Same Speeds

[edit]
The previous figure kept one partition and only relabelled the axis, so \(H\) could not change. Now we rebin. Take the same speed samples and the same number of cells, and compare

These are different collections of masses \(\{p_i\}\), so they have different discrete entropies. Uniform bins in \(s\) are Lebesgue measure on the speed axis. Equal-volume bins are Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb{R}^3\), expressed in the speed coordinate. Choosing a discretisation is choosing a measure.

Bin Widths Are a Radon-Nikodym Derivative

[edit]

For a partition that is not equal-width, the bin-width correction is no longer a single \(\log\Delta\). Probability conservation in cell \(i\) still reads \(p_i \approx f\,\Delta_i\), so \[\begin{align} H = -\sum_i p_i\log p_i \approx h - \sum_i p_i\log\Delta_i, \qquad h \approx H + \sum_i p_i\log\Delta_i. \end{align}\] Take the equal-volume cells of the previous snippets, written once in \(u=s^3\) and once in \(s\). The masses \(\{p_i\}\) (hence \(H\)) are the same. The two differential entropies differ by the average log-ratio of widths, \[\begin{align} h(s)-h(u) \approx \sum_i p_i\log\frac{\Delta s_i}{\Delta u_i} \to \mathbb{E}\bigl[\log\bigl|{\mathrm{d}s}/{\mathrm{d}u}\bigr|\bigr]. \end{align}\] That ratio \(\Delta s_i/\Delta u_i\) is called the discrete Radon-Nikodym derivative of Lebesgue-on-speed with respect to Lebesgue-on-volume. In the continuum limit it becomes the Jacobian \(1/(3s^2)\). Probability conservation \(f_s(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}s = f_u(u)\,{\mathrm{d}}u\) is the same statement.

The Maxwell speed density relative to three-dimensional Lebesgue is the shell factor \(4\pi s^2\): \[\begin{align} f_s(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}s = f(\mathbf{v})\,{\mathrm{d}}^3v, \qquad \frac{{\mathrm{d}}^3v}{{\mathrm{d}}s} = 4\pi s^2. \end{align}\] The Gaussian \(h(v_x)\) and the Maxwell \(h(s)\) disagree because they are different coordinates and because each \(h\) is taken against a different undeclared Lebesgue measure.

Figure: Width ratio of the pushed equal-volume cells, against the Jacobian \(1/(3s^2)\). The average log-ratio converts \(h(u)\) into \(h(s)\). That ratio is the discrete Radon-Nikodym derivative.

Jaynes: Promote the Bins to a Measure

[edit]

\[ h_m(f)=-\int f\log\frac{f}{m}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=-\text{KL}\left( f\,\|\,m \right) \]

Differential entropy \(h=-\int f\log f\,{\mathrm{d}}x\) pretends the coordinate measure is part of the data. The histogram arithmetic showed that it is not: \(H\) is a functional of masses, and the passage to a density subtracts \(\sum_i p_i\log\Delta_i\), whose continuum limit is the log of a reference density \(m\). Jaynes called \(m\) the limiting density of discrete points — the binning measure, promoted to a density (Jaynes, 1963).

Once \(m\) is written down, \[\begin{align} h_m(f) = -\int f\log\frac{f}{m}\,{\mathrm{d}}x = -\text{KL}\left( f\,\|\,m \right). \end{align}\] Taking \(m\) uniform (Lebesgue) in the Cartesian component \(v_x\) recovers the ordinary Gaussian remainder \(h(v_x)\). Taking \(m\) uniform in three-dimensional velocity, and writing it in the speed coordinate, gives \(m(s)\propto s^2\) — the shell volume that had been hiding in the Jacobian. The two Maxwell–Boltzmann entropies disagreed because each \(h\) used a different undeclared \(m\). Relative entropy makes the choice explicit. The Jacobian cancels inside \(\text{KL}\left( f\,\|\,m \right)\) under a common invertible map; the discrete KL has no divergent \(-\log\Delta\) term.

This names \(m\). Maximising \(h_m\) at fixed mean energy — Jaynes’ maximum entropy principle — is a later step.

Maxwell’s Demon, Compartments, Two Ledgers

[edit]

Maxwell’s Demon

[edit]

Maxwell’s demon is a thought experiment described by James Clerk Maxwell in his book, Theory of Heat (Maxwell, 1871) on page 308.

But if we conceive a being whose faculties are so sharpened that he can follow every molecule in its course, such a being, whose attributes are still as essentially finite as our own, would be able to do what is at present impossible to us. For we have seen that the molecules in a vessel full of air at uniform temperature are moving with velocities by no means uniform, though the mean velocity of any great number of them, arbitrarily selected, is almost exactly uniform. Now let us suppose that such a vessel is divided into two portions, A and B, by a division in which there is a small hole, and that a being, who can see the individual molecules, opens and closes this hole, so as to allow only the swifter molecules to pass from A to B, and the only the slower ones to pass from B to A. He will thus, without expenditure of work, raise the temperature of B and lower that of A, in contradiction to the second law of thermodynamics.

James Clerk Maxwell in Theory of Heat (Maxwell, 1871) page 308

He goes onto say:

This is only one of the instances in which conclusions which we have draw from our experience of bodies consisting of an immense number of molecules may be found not to be applicable to the more delicate observations and experiments which we may suppose made by one who can perceive and handle the individual molecules which we deal with only in large masses

Figure: Maxwell’s demon was designed to highlight the statistical nature of the second law of thermodynamics.

Balls are allowed to pass the green membrane from right to left only if they are cold, and from left to right only if they are hot. The displayed entropy is the Shannon entropy of the velocity histogram (a coarse-grained proxy, not full thermodynamic entropy).

Maxwell’s demon allows us to connect thermodynamics with information theory (see e.g. Hosoya et al. (2015);Hosoya et al. (2011);Bub (2001);Brillouin (1951);Szilard (1929)). Landauer (1961) described a fundamental connection between information erasure and energy consumption .

Compartments Around the Membrane

[edit]

The canvas already implements Maxwell’s sorting as a dynamical rule. A ball on the left crosses only when its speed exceeds \(v_\star\); a ball on the right crosses only when its speed is at most \(v_\star\). Hot therefore accumulates on the right and cold on the left. That is the JS convention, and we keep it.

The new move is to refuse a single temperature on each side. Insert two extra partitions, distant from the membrane. Between the left wall and the left partition sits a bulk: many collisions, few crossings. Between the right partition and the right wall sits another bulk. Against the membrane, on each side, sits a kinetic layer of thickness a mean free path or so. In the bulks we may argue for local equilibrium — the same timescale split as the grouping identity, \(p_i\) relaxing quickly to \(\pi_i\). In the layers we may not. What hits the gate is a selected tail or body; what just crossed has not remixed. The neighbourhood of the membrane is not at equilibrium.

Figure: The vessel with two extra partitions. Distant bulks may sit at local equilibrium, \(T_L\) on the left and \(T_R\) on the right. The kinetic layers against the membrane cannot: the gate selects a tail one way and a body the other. Arrows follow the JS convention — hot left to right, cold right to left — so the right bulk heats.

Each distant bulk is a group in the sense we already have. We keep a few intensive and extensive numbers and refuse the molecular faces. Local equilibrium is the claim that collisions inside the group are fast compared with exchange across its boundary, so the within-group law has settled to \(\pi_i\). That claim is plausible far from the membrane. It is not plausible in the kinetic layer. The gate is a non-reciprocal filter: the left-going stream and the right-going stream are different velocity sets. Arrivals are conditioned on that filter, and a ball that has just crossed has not yet collided enough times to forget how it entered. Extending a Maxwellian up to the membrane is the same mistake as treating \(\{1,2,3\}\) as already mixed when the cube has only just shown the group label.

Two Ledgers at the Membrane

In the simulation the membrane does not move. A ball on the left is allowed through only when its speed exceeds \(v_\star\); a ball on the right is allowed through only when its speed is at most \(v_\star\). Between crossings, elastic collisions in each bulk redistribute energy, so a ball that is cold now can become hot later and then cross. The question is not whether sorting begins — it does — but which currents through the gate can vanish together.

For the closed vessel to sit in a non-equilibrium steady state, every conserved current through the membrane must vanish. Particle number is conserved, so the number of balls crossing left to right must equal the number crossing right to left, otherwise the bulk densities keep changing. Energy is conserved, so the energy those streams carry must also balance, otherwise one bulk keeps heating and the other cooling.

That pair of flux balances is not detailed balance. Detailed balance would require each velocity class that crosses to be matched by its reverse. The gate refuses: a hot left-to-right crossing has no matching hot right-to-left crossing, because hot balls on the right are reflected. The allowed streams are a high-energy set one way and a low-energy set the other. Equal counts are then not equal energies.

Figure: Two ledgers at the same gate. For a closed box, a steady bulk number requires equal counts both ways. The hot stream carries more energy per ball than the cold stream, so those equal counts are not an energy balance. A steady bulk energy would need the lower pair of arrows to match as well.

Suppose, illegally, that each kinetic layer is the same Maxwellian as its distant bulk, at temperatures \(T_L\) and \(T_R\). Conditioning on \(v>v_\star\) selects a high-energy set; conditioning on \(v\le v_\star\) selects a low-energy set. Matching the two number fluxes then leaves a net energy flux into the right-hand side. Matching the energy fluxes instead leaves a net number flux. The only Maxwellian pair that closes both ledgers is \(T_L=T_R\) with no sort — and at equal temperatures the filter itself drives a current, so that pair is not stationary either.

A closed box can therefore sit still only if the velocity distributions at the gate are not the bulk Maxwellians. The extra partitions were not decoration: they mark the contour where the local-equilibrium coarse graining stops being legal. Collisions rebuild the tails in the bulks; the membrane continually sculpts the layers.

Take equal chamber areas and a 2D ideal gas (the billiard world). On each side the velocity density is Maxwellian, \[ f(\mathbf{v}\mid\beta)=\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta v^2\right),\qquad \beta=\frac{1}{k_B T}, \] with mean kinetic energy \(k_B T\) per ball (two quadratic degrees of freedom). The one-way effusion flux through a unit aperture is \(\Phi_{\mathrm{tot}}=n/\sqrt{2\pi\beta}\). Restricting to the hot or cold set gives \[\begin{align} \Phi^{\mathrm{hot}}(n,\beta) &= \frac{n}{\pi}\left[v_\star e^{-\beta v_\star^2/2}+\int_{v_\star}^\infty e^{-\beta v^2/2}\,\mathrm{d}v\right],\\ \Phi^{\mathrm{cold}}(n,\beta) &= \Phi_{\mathrm{tot}}(n,\beta)-\Phi^{\mathrm{hot}}(n,\beta). \end{align}\] Particle-number balance across the membrane is \[ \Phi^{\mathrm{hot}}(n_L,\beta_L)=\Phi^{\mathrm{cold}}(n_R,\beta_R). \] Total ball number \(N=N_L+N_R\) and total energy \(E=N_L k_B T_L+N_R k_B T_R\) are conserved. For the simulation parameters (\(v_\star=3\), mean kinetic energy \(12.5\) in code units, equal populations \(N_L=N_R\)) this fixes the temperatures uniquely under the Maxwellian-at-the-gate assumption. It does not close the energy ledger.

Figure: Left: temperatures from balancing number flux at equal populations, forcing Maxwellian bulks all the way to the gate. Right: entropy of that two-Maxwellian state minus the single-temperature equilibrium. The red point is the simulation threshold \(v_\star=3\). These numbers close only one ledger.

With \(k_B=1\), equal populations, \(v_\star=3\) and total energy matching the simulation’s mean kinetic energy \(12.5\), number-flux balance gives \(T_L\approx 1.94\) and \(T_R\approx 23.1\). The ideal-gas Shannon entropy of a 2D Maxwellian chamber (position Stirling term plus velocity differential entropy) \[ S(N,\beta)=N\left[2+\log\frac{A}{N}-\log\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\right] \] falls by about \(24\) nats relative to the single-temperature state at the same \(N\) and \(E\). Read that drop as the cost of the illegal coarse graining, not as a machine. Hot accumulates on the right, cold on the left, and the joint distribution over \((\mathrm{side},\mathbf{v})\) looks more concentrated than the unconstrained Maxwellian — provided we pretend the kinetic layers are Maxwellian too. They are not. The true non-equilibrium state depletes the left hot tail and the right cold body; collisions refill those sets in the bulks. The local-temperature calculation remains the right first cut on direction. It is not a closed book.

Where Can the Entropy Sit?

[edit]

Once the compartments are drawn, the demon is no longer a single puzzle. It is a choice of which coarse graining is legal, and therefore of where the missing entropy is allowed to sit.

If we keep Maxwellians all the way to the gate, particle-flux balance and energy-flux balance cannot hold together. The calculation that produced \(T_L\approx 1.9\), \(T_R\approx 23\) already used that illegal extension: it closed only the number ledger. The first resolution is to refuse the extension. The kinetic layers are not groups of the same kind as the bulks; their entropy is not \(H(\pi_i)\) at a local \(T\).

If the gate is a physical trapdoor, the door is a degree of freedom in the same bath. It jitters, it fails to stay open for the tail and shut for the body, and the rectification dies (Smoluchowski, 1914). Feynman’s pawl is the same object.

If the gate is a policy that remembers who may pass, the record is on the ledger. Szilard isolates one bit of that record; Landauer prices erasing it (Bennett, 1982; Landauer, 1961; Szilard, 1929).

If we refuse compartments altogether and write one equilibrium ensemble for the closed box, the sorting we see on the canvas is a fluctuation. Maxwell already said the second law need not apply to a being who handles individual molecules (Maxwell, 1871).

Those are different books, not competing slogans. The next worksheet is to coarse-grain Maxwell: which of those descriptions you are allowed to write for each compartment, and what entropy each description may claim.

The Demon Sorts Groups

[edit]

Maxwell’s sorting rule partitions velocity space into coarse sets — hot enough to cross'' versuscold enough to cross’’ — and applies different gates to each set. That is week 2’s grouping again, now stacked on the spatial compartments: a deterministic label \(G=g(\mathbf{v})\) plus residual uncertainty inside each label. Local equilibrium — timescale separation between collisions and crossings — is the claim we already restricted to the distant bulks. Extending it through the kinetic layers is what made number balance and energy balance incompatible. When that extension fails, the true steady state is no longer a pair of perfect Maxwellians (tails deplete). Information engines and demons play on the tension between moving probability among groups (\(H(w)\)) and the within-group bath term \(\sum w_i H(p_i)\) that thermodynamics prices.

Kemeny–Snell lumpability is the precise condition under which a partition of a Markov chain induces another Markov chain on the groups. When it fails, the macro description needs memory of how the system entered the group. Name the issue; do not develop projection-operator formalism here.
