Exponential Family

[edit]

On a finite state space the constrained MaxEnt distribution is an exponential family. With sufficient statistics \(T_k(x)=f_k(x)\) and base density \(h_0\) of the counting measure \(\#\) on the allowed outcomes, \[\begin{align} p(x\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}) &= \exp\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot T(x) - \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr)\, h_0(x),\\ \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) &= \log \sum_x \exp\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot T(x)\bigr), \end{align}\] where \(h_0(x)=\mathbf{1}[x\in\mathcal{X}]\) (one on allowed labels, zero elsewhere). The Lagrange multipliers from the Week 5 proof are the negative natural parameters, \(\theta_k=-\lambda_k\). The minus sign keeps high-energy states down-weighted when \(\theta_1=-\beta<0\).

A probability is always a density with respect to some reference measure \(\mu\): the Radon–Nikodym derivative \({\mathrm{d}}P/{\mathrm{d}}\mu\). Every entropy-like quantity in the course is the same expression, \[\begin{align} H_\mu(P)=-\int\log\frac{{\mathrm{d}}P}{{\mathrm{d}}\mu}\,{\mathrm{d}}P, \end{align}\] with a different \(\mu\) each time. Lecture 4 already said that choosing a discretisation is choosing a measure; the counting measure \(\#\) is the case so natural that nobody notices the choice has been made.

Separate two uses of the word Lebesgue. The Lebesgue integral \(\int f\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu\) is a single integration machine that works for any measure \(\mu\): a sum is a Lebesgue integral against \(\#\), and the ordinary calculus integral is a Lebesgue integral against Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\). Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) itself is one particular choice of \(\mu\): length, area, or volume, so \(\lambda([a,b])=b-a\). Integrating against \(\#\) gives a sum, \(\int f\,{\mathrm{d}}\#=\sum_x f(x)\), and the density of \(P\) with respect to \(\#\) is the probability mass function \(p(x)=P(\{x\})\). Shannon entropy is exactly \(H_\#(P)=-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)\).

Counting measure is a prior: every outcome gets weight 1. On a finite set of \(N\) outcomes write \(u=\#/N\) for the uniform distribution. Then \[\begin{align} H(P)=\log N-\mathrm{KL}(P\|u). \end{align}\] So Shannon entropy is a negative KL divergence against the uniform that \(\#\) defines. Three immediate consequences: (i) \(H\ge 0\), because each atom has counting measure 1 so \(p(x)\le 1\); (ii) \(H\) is unchanged by any relabelling of the outcomes (\(\#\) is invariant under bijections); (iii) on a countably infinite set \(\#\) has infinite total mass, there is no uniform \(u\), and entropy need not be finite.

The same template covers the continuous and relative cases. Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) is the continuum counterpart: differential entropy is \(H_\lambda\), can be negative, and changes under nonlinear reparametrisation because \(\lambda\) does. Two distinct effects meet here and should stay separate. Under a change of variable the Jacobian \(|{\mathrm{d}}y/{\mathrm{d}}x|\) appears — that is Lecture 4’s density-of-states factor (for example \(v^2\) in the speed coordinate). Separately, putting counting measure on a grid of spacing \(\Delta\) and rescaling by \(\Delta\) converges to \(\lambda\) as \(\Delta\to 0\); a binned Shannon entropy then carries an additive \(-\log\Delta\), which is the log of that rescaling factor between \(\#\) and \(\lambda\), not a Jacobian. Why not just use counting measure on \(\mathbb{R}\)? Because \(\#\) and \(\lambda\) are mutually singular: \(\lambda(\{x\})=0\) for every point, while a discrete law puts positive mass on points, so neither measure admits a density with respect to the other. A mixed law — daily rainfall exactly zero with probability \(0.3\), otherwise continuous — has a density only with respect to something like \(\delta_0+\lambda\). Keep that aside in the notes; do not board it live.

KL divergence uses a probability \(Q\) as reference, \[\begin{align} \mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)=\int\log\frac{{\mathrm{d}}P}{{\mathrm{d}}Q}\,{\mathrm{d}}P, \end{align}\] provided \(P\) is absolutely continuous with respect to \(Q\) (\(P\ll Q\)). If \(P\) puts mass anywhere \(Q\) does not, the Radon–Nikodym derivative fails and \(\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)=+\infty\). When both densities are written against a common base \(\mu\), the ratio of densities cancels \(\mu\), which is why KL (and mutual information) has no \(-\log\Delta\) divergence and is unchanged when the same invertible map is applied to both arguments.

Maximum entropy relative to \(\mu\) gives \({\mathrm{d}}P/{\mathrm{d}}\mu=\exp(\theta\cdot T-\psi)\). The factor \(h_0\) in the exponential family is the density of that base measure when sums or integrals are written against a default chart. Today \(\nu=\#\) on the allowed set and \(h_0={\mathrm{d}}\nu/{\mathrm{d}}\#=\mathbf{1}[x\in\mathcal{X}]\), so \(\psi\) is a log-sum. Even in discrete cases the base need not be plain counting: Poisson uses \(h_0(x)=1/x!\) on \(\mathbb{N}\), binomial uses \(\binom{n}{x}\) — combinatorial multiplicity as the discrete counterpart of the \(v^2\) density of states. We write \(h_0\) rather than \(h\) to avoid colliding with differential entropy and with the regulator map \(A=h(S)\) later in the module.

Each of these is MaxEnt for its moment constraints, so each belongs to the exponential family. The canonical ensemble is the mean-energy case already recovered in Week 5. A Gaussian is MaxEnt for a fixed mean and variance. A two-level system with energies \(0\) and \(\varepsilon\) is a Bernoulli with natural parameter \(\theta=-\beta\varepsilon\). The distribution bank that follows is where both measure morals land: Bernoulli / categorical use plain counting (\(h_0=1\)); binomial / Poisson / multinomial use combinatorial \(h_0\) as discrete density of states.

Common Distributions as Exponential Families

[edit]

Week 1 named five families without writing them in natural coordinates: Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, multinomial/categorical, and Gaussian. Each is MaxEnt for its moment constraints relative to an appropriate base measure \(h_0\), so each belongs to the exponential family \[ p(x\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})=\exp\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot T(x)-\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr)\,h_0(x). \] The slides below record, for each family, the natural parameter(s) \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), the sufficient statistic \(T\), the cumulant generating function \(\psi\), and — the point of this block — which \(h_0\) is in force. Two emphases run through the tour. First, when \(h_0=1\) (Bernoulli; categorical \(n=1\)), the base is counting measure: every allowed label has prior weight one, the same unnoticed prior as \(H=\log N-\mathrm{KL}(P\|u)\). Second, when \(h_0\) is non-constant (binomial \(\binom{n}{k}\), Poisson \(1/k!\), multinomial \(n!/\prod_j x_j!\)), that factor is the discrete density of states: same moment constraints relative to plain \(\#\) would give a different family. Gaussian uses Lebesgue — the continuum counterpart of \(\#\).

Bernoulli as Exponential Family

[edit]

A Bernoulli(\(\pi\)) trial with \(P(X=1)=\pi\) is already in exponential-family form once we take the logit as the natural parameter, \[ \theta=\log\frac{\pi}{1-\pi},\qquad T(x)=x,\qquad \psi(\theta)=\log(1+e^{\theta}). \] The base density is \(h_0(x)=1\): counting measure on the two labels. That is the unnoticed prior from the counting-measure slide — each of \(\{0,1\}\) has weight one, so Shannon \(H\) on a Bernoulli is \(\log 2-\mathrm{KL}(P\|u)\) with \(u\) uniform on those two outcomes. Recovering the mean parameter is the logistic map: \(\pi=\sigma(\theta)=e^{\theta}/(1+e^{\theta})\), and \(\psi'(\theta)=\pi\). The two-level thermal system with energies \(0\) and \(\varepsilon\) is the same family with \(\theta=-\beta\varepsilon\).

Binomial as Exponential Family

[edit]

Binomial\((n,\pi)\) is \(n\) i.i.d. Bernoulli trials counted. The natural parameter is the same logit as the Bernoulli, \[ \theta=\log\frac{\pi}{1-\pi},\qquad T(k)=k, \] but the cumulant generating function scales with the number of trials, \[ \psi(\theta)=n\log(1+e^{\theta}), \] and the base measure carries the combinatorial multiplicity \(h_0(k)=\binom{n}{k}\). That multiplicity is the discrete density of states — the same role \(v^2\) played for Maxwell–Boltzmann. MaxEnt under a mean-count constraint relative to plain counting measure does not recover the binomial; MaxEnt relative to \(\binom{n}{k}\,{\mathrm{d}}\#\) does. Same \(\theta\) as Bernoulli; different prior weight on the count \(k\). Mean and variance follow by differentiation: \(\psi'(\theta)=n\pi\), \(\psi''(\theta)=n\pi(1-\pi)\).

Poisson as Exponential Family

[edit]

Poisson(\(\lambda\)) has probability mass \[ P(k)=\frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}. \] In exponential-family coordinates the rate becomes a natural parameter through \(\theta=\log\lambda\), the count is the sufficient statistic \(T(k)=k\), and the cumulant generating function is simply \[ \psi(\theta)=e^{\theta}=\lambda. \] The factorial in the denominator is the base measure \(h_0(k)=1/k!\) with respect to counting measure on \(\mathbb{N}_0\). This is the sharpest discrete illustration of the Weeks 4–5 story: the exponential tilt \(\exp(\theta\,T-\psi)\) is only half the model; the base \(h_0\) is the other half. MaxEnt under a fixed mean count relative to plain counting measure (\(h_0=1\)) yields a geometric distribution. MaxEnt relative to \(h_0=1/k!\) yields Poisson. Same constraint, different measure, different family — the discrete counterpart of Lebesgue versus \(v^2\mathrm{d}v\). Differentiating recovers the familiar moments: \(\psi'(\theta)=\lambda\) and \(\psi''(\theta)=\lambda\) (mean equals variance).

Multinomial as Exponential Family

[edit]

A categorical distribution on \(K\) exclusive labels is a multinomial with a single trial (\(n=1\)). Write the outcome as a one-hot vector \(\mathbf{x}\) with \(\sum_{j=1}^K x_j=1\). Because of the simplex constraint we use \(K-1\) free natural parameters, \[ \theta_j=\log\frac{\pi_j}{\pi_K},\qquad j=1,\ldots,K-1, \] with sufficient statistics \(T(\mathbf{x})=(x_1,\ldots,x_{K-1})\) and cumulant generating function \[ \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\log\Bigl(1+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}e^{\theta_j}\Bigr) \] (the log-sum-exp that recovers \(\pi_K=1/\bigl(1+\sum_j e^{\theta_j}\bigr)\)). For \(n\) independent trials the counts \(\mathbf{x}\) are multinomial: the same \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) and \(T\), but \[ \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})=n\log\Bigl(1+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}e^{\theta_j}\Bigr) \] and base measure \(h_0(\mathbf{x})=n!/(x_1!\cdots x_K!)\). Softmax regression is this family with features in place of the raw one-hot.

Gaussian as Exponential Family

[edit]

The univariate Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\) is MaxEnt under fixed mean and variance, so it belongs to a two-parameter exponential family. Expanding the quadratic energy gives natural parameters \[ \theta_1=\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2},\qquad \theta_2=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}, \] sufficient statistics \(T(y)=(y,y^2)\), and cumulant generating function \[ \psi(\theta_1,\theta_2)=-\frac{\theta_1^2}{4\theta_2}-\frac12\log(-2\theta_2)-\frac12\log(2\pi). \] The base measure is Lebesgue — the continuous counterpart of counting measure \(\#\) — and the \((2\pi)\) factor sits in \(\psi\) (equivalently in a constant piece of \(h_0\)). Differentiating recovers the moments: \(\nabla\psi=(\mu,\mu^2+\sigma^2)\). With known variance the family collapses to one parameter, \(\theta=\mu/\sigma^2\), \(T(y)=y\), and \(\psi(\theta)=\tfrac12\sigma^2\theta^2\) (up to an additive constant independent of \(\theta\)). The multivariate case replaces \(\theta_1\) by \(\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\theta_2\) by \(-\tfrac12\Sigma^{-1}\) acting on \(yy^\top\).

\(\psi(\theta)\) as Cumulant Generating Function

The Quiz 2 debrief already read \(\log Z\) as available energy, \(F=-k_BT\log Z\). In exponential-family coordinates the same function is \(\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\log Z(-\boldsymbol{\theta})\). Its first derivative is the mean of \(T\). Differentiating again will give \(\mathrm{Cov}[T]\), but that identity is earned after the entropy form, not asserted here.

Entropy of an Exponential Family

[edit]

Once \(p\) is written as an exponential family, Shannon entropy collapses to an algebraic identity in \(\psi\) and \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\). That identity is the bridge to Fisher information: the same \(\log p\) whose expectation is \(-H\) has a score whose second moment is \(\nabla^2\psi\).

Write \(\log p(x\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot T(x)-\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\log h_0(x)\). When the base is counting measure on the allowed set (\(h_0=1\)), Shannon entropy collapses to \[\begin{align} H(\boldsymbol{\theta}) &=-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[\log p(X\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})] =\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[T(X)] =\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}, \end{align}\] with \(\boldsymbol{\eta}=\nabla\psi\). In general the base survives as \[\begin{align} H(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[\log h_0(X)], \end{align}\] so the short form \(H=\psi-\theta\cdot\eta\) is the \(h_0=1\) special case (Bernoulli, categorical, Jaynes’ die), not a licence to drop \(1/k!\) from a Poisson. Equivalently, when \(h_0=1\), \(H=-\psi^*(\boldsymbol{\eta})\) where \(\psi^*\) is the Legendre conjugate of \(\psi\). That is the form that makes the next differentiation step cheap.

Differentiate \(\log p=\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot T-\psi\) under the expectation: \[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(x\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})=T(x)-\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})=T(x)-\boldsymbol{\eta}. \] The score has mean zero. Fisher’s information matrix is the second moment of the score, \[ G_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[\partial_i\log p\,\partial_j\log p]=\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[T]_{ij}. \] Differentiating \(\boldsymbol{\eta}=\nabla\psi\) once more yields the same matrix as a Hessian: \(G=\nabla^2\psi\). So the cumulant-generating property is the Fisher metric on an exponential family — earned from the entropy calculation, not postulated.

Fisher Metric from the Exponential Family

Class test timer (Fisher geometry): 10:35

Fisher (1925) called the expected squared score information about a parameter. For a scalar parameter, \[ I(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p(x\mid\theta)\right)^2\right]. \] On an exponential family that is exactly \(\nabla^2\psi=\mathrm{Cov}[T]\), already boarded. Shannon’s \(H\) measures uncertainty in a random variable; Fisher’s \(I\) measures how much a sample tells you about \(\theta\). Conflating the two words is the first mistake to block — and the exponential family is where both appear from one \(\log p\).

Three readings of one matrix: covariance of the sufficient statistics, Fisher’s expected squared score, and the Hessian of the cumulant generating function. Fréchet–Rao–Cramér identifiability (\(\mathrm{cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\succeq G^{-1}\)) is the estimation reading. That matrix is the Fisher information metric on the statistical manifold.

The Fisher matrix defines infinitesimal information distance. Larger \(G\) means a given parameter step is more distinguishable. Integrating \(\mathrm{d}s\) along a path of equilibria would give Crooks’ thermodynamic length; that development is dropped from the module. Today the metric itself is the object.

Amari’s dual flatness: exponential families admit affine coordinates in \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) and in \(\boldsymbol{\eta}=\nabla\psi\), with dual potentials whose Hessians are inverse Fisher metrics. The Legendre dual is what they already computed for entropy: \(\psi^*(\eta)=\theta\cdot\eta-\psi=-H\) when \(h_0=1\) (otherwise subtract \(\mathbb{E}[\log h_0]\) from \(H\)). The Pythagorean theorem for KL on dual flats stays thin today so Shannon and the Good Regulator keep their clock.

Shannon’s Communication Theorem

Class test timer (Shannon): 11:05

Shannon’s communication theorem (noisy-channel coding) is the information-theoretic twin of Carnot: it forbids reliable rates above capacity and does not construct a code. Capacity \(C=\max_{p(x)}I(X;Y)\) is the no-go number; the capacity-achieving input distribution is the prescription. Week 5 already made mutual information first-class via soft partitions; today that \(I\) is the rate currency on a channel.

Scaffolding, Not Outcomes

[edit]

The chain rule \(H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y|X)\) is the algebraic source of multi-information. Mutual information \(I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)\) is the pairwise case; we do not yet treat \(n>2\). Channel capacity \(C\) is a no-go on rate; the capacity-achieving input distribution is the prescription. The data-processing inequality is the no-go on \(I\): processing cannot create information. Cover and Thomas Theorem 2.8.1 is the reading; the proof waits for week 8, when \(I\) is first-class.

Good Regulator Overview

[edit]

Overview only. Full Conant–Ashby development — concavity argument, existence of a deterministic optimum, and the two caveats in depth — is the Week 8 review. Today’s job is the setup, the notation, and the slogan with fences. Diagrams follow Joery’s oneshot figure in the good-regulator notes: disturbance \(D\), system state \(S\), action \(A\), reguland \(Z\).

Conant and Ashby (Conant and Ashby, 1970) often label the regulator \(R\). We write \(A\) for the action so the policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\) is first-class. The reguland — the variable whose entropy successful regulation minimises — is \(Z\). The system state is \(S\); the disturbance is \(D\). The cumulant generating function earlier today was \(\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\log Z(-\boldsymbol{\theta})\); that \(Z\) was the partition function, not the reguland — different objects, same letter in the physics tradition.

A disturbance \(D\) causes events in a system \(S\); the regulator counters with an action \(A\); the pair produces a reguland \(Z=\phi(S,A)\) (Conant and Ashby, 1970). Cause-controlled regulation acts on \(D\) (or on an inferred model of \(D\)). Error-controlled regulation acts on the state \(S\) caused by \(D\), so by the data-processing inequality \(I(A;D)\le I(S;D)\). Both diagrams are on today’s board; the existence proof that an \(H(Z)\)-optimal policy may be taken deterministic waits for Week 8.

Successful regulation, for Conant and Ashby, minimises the entropy of the reguland: \(\min_\pi H(Z)\). Among optima there exists a deterministic policy \(A=h(S)\), equivalently \(H(A\mid S)=0\). That is the sense in which the regulator is a model'' --- deliberately weak. Do not collapse the slogan into\(A\) contains a rich model of \(S\)’’: a constant action also has \(H(A\mid S)=0\). Do not confuse low \(H(Z)\) with a desirable outcome. Requisite variety in Shannon form will use the same letters: residual uncertainty satisfies \(H(Z)\ge H(D)-I(D;A)\) when \(A\) is the regulatory response. Week 8 reviews the theorem properly and applies it to capability claims.

Ashby’s law of requisite variety in Shannon form (Ashby, 1956), written in today’s letters: residual uncertainty in the reguland is at least the disturbance entropy minus the information the action carries about the disturbance. Requisite variety asks how much relevant \(I\) is required; the Good Regulator Theorem asks how that \(I\) may be organised. Full development of both, with the concavity argument, is Week 8.
