Worksheet 3 — Maxwell Review

A quick reminder of where lecture 4 left things. Away from the membrane, a mean free path or more, each side of the box can be treated as a Maxwellian at its own temperature. Right next to the membrane it can’t. If we pretend every molecule arriving at the gate comes from the bulk Maxwellian on its side, we find that the number crossing each way and the energy crossing each way can’t both balance. Worksheet 3 asked which description is right for each part of the box, and where the missing entropy is allowed to go. Next I want to fill in three ideas that the dialogues reached for but that lecture 4 didn’t teach.

Liouville, Detailed Balance, and the Trapdoor

Many of the Worksheet 3 dialogues usded Liouville’s theorem, the detailed balance we suggested, or Smoluchowski’s trapdoor. Today we’ll review a little about what those different ideas say and how they relate to our coarse graining perspective.

The first question is about the rule itself. Maxwell’s being lets fast molecules through one way and slow ones the other. Is that a rule that Newton’s laws, on their own, could ever carry out? This is the question that Liouville’s theorem can address. The second question is about equilibrium. If the whole box, gate included, is at one temperature, what must be true of the traffic between any two states? That’s detailed balance. The third question considers a mechanism, suppose we try to build the gate out of real parts, specifically a little hinged flap sitting in the same gas. Why doesn’t it work? That’s Smoluchowski’s trapdoor.

Figure: Phase space of a single pendulum: each point is one complete state, angle \(q\) and momentum \(p\). The red cloud is 40,000 possible states of the same pendulum, our uncertainty about it, each followed forward in time. Grey curves are lines of constant energy, and every point travels along its own curve, clockwise (arrows). The closed ovals are swings; the wavy lines at top and bottom are the pendulum going right over the top. The left and right edges are the same angle. The cloud’s true area, measured from its edge, is the same in all three panels, so the fine-grained entropy hasn’t moved. The red ink looks bigger by \(t=40\) only because the thread is thinner than a pixel. What does grow is the number of coarse cells (shaded) the thread touches, and so the entropy computed from the cells.

Liouville’s theorem is a statement about what mechanics does to uncertainty. Take a state of a mechanical system — positions \(\mathbf{q}\) and momenta \(\mathbf{p}\) for every particle — and suppose we don’t know it exactly. We know it lies somewhere in a region of phase space. Let each point in that region evolve under Newton’s laws (or Hamilton’s equations, which are the same laws written more tidily). The region moves, it stretches, it folds back on itself. But its volume doesn’t change.

The figure shows this for a single pendulum. Phase space is the space of complete states. For a pendulum the state is two numbers: the angle \(q\) it hangs at and its momentum \(p\) (mass times angular velocity). Knowing both fixes the whole future, so each point in the plane is one possible pendulum, and the motion of the pendulum is the motion of that point. Hamilton’s equations say \(\dot q = p\) and \(\dot p = -\sin q\) (in units where mass, length and gravity are 1). So when \(p\) is positive the point moves right, and gravity pulls \(p\) back towards zero: the flow goes round clockwise.

Energy \(E=\tfrac{1}{2}p^2-\cos q\) is conserved, so each point stays on its own grey curve. The closed ovals round the centre are ordinary swings: the bottom of the swing (\(q=0\)) is where \(|p|\) is largest, and the turning points are where the oval crosses \(p=0\). The wavy lines along the top and bottom are a pendulum with enough energy to go right over the top and keep rotating. The angle wraps round, so the left and right edges of each panel are the same place (\(q=\pm\pi\), pointing straight up), and the boundary between the two kinds of motion is the separatrix through those points.

The red cloud is not one pendulum but 40,000 possible starting states of it. We can read it as our uncertainty: we know the pendulum is somewhere in the disc, but not where. The cloud starts as a disc on the left of the picture: the pendulum is swinging right, about 90 degrees from the bottom. Each point then moves along its own oval. But a pendulum’s period grows with the size of its swing, so points on the outer ovals take longer to go round than points on the inner ones. The outer edge of the cloud falls behind the inner edge, the disc is sheared into a crescent (\(t=6\)), and then into a long thin thread wound round and round (\(t=40\)). Its area is exactly what it was at the start. The panel titles give that area, measured from the edge of the cloud, and it agrees to three decimal places at all three times.

Why, then, does the red look bigger at \(t=40\)? Because the thread becomes thinner than a pixel, and a pixel can only be red or not. Counting red pixels gives an area about twice the true one by \(t=40\). The picture is itself a coarse graining, and it shows the same effect as the shaded grid: the region we can see grows while the region the states actually occupy does not.

That has a direct consequence for entropy. If our uncertainty is spread uniformly over the cloud, its Shannon entropy is the log of the cloud’s volume. Same volume, same entropy. More generally, for any density \(\rho_t\) carried along by Hamiltonian flow, the fine-grained entropy \(-\int \rho_t \log \rho_t\) is constant in time.

So where does the second law come from? From the grid. We can’t follow the thread; we only see which cells it is in. The entropy computed from the cell occupancies rises as the thread spreads across more cells. This is the Gibbs and Ehrenfest account of the second law: what increases is a coarse-grained entropy, the one that forgets the fine detail. That fits the grouping picture we have been using since the die in Week 2: the macrostate is the group, and the detail within each group is what we have chosen not to track.

The reason volume can’t shrink is that mechanics is reversible. If I hand you the state of the system now, you can flip all the momenta, run the same laws forward, and recover where it came from. So two different starting points can never arrive at the same end point: if they did, the backward run would have to choose between two pasts. Any rule that sent two distinct states to one would squeeze phase-space volume, and mechanics never does that.

This gives us a test we can apply to the demon’s gate. Don’t ask whether it is clever, or what it knows. Just ask: does it ever send two different states to the same one?

Figure: What happens to a molecule at the gate, drawn in (side, velocity) space. Molecules about to arrive are in the strong-coloured quadrants: A and B on the left heading right (fast and slow), C and D on the right heading left. An open hole lets everything through; a solid wall bounces everything back. Both send each arriving region to its own leaving region. Maxwell’s gate sends A and C to the same place (fast, leaving to the right) and B and D to the same place (slow, leaving to the left). Two quadrants end up empty. The gate halves the phase-space volume.

Here is the gate drawn in phase space. Look only at the direction across the gate: which side a molecule is on, and its velocity \(v\) across the gate (positive means heading right). Molecules about to hit the gate are on the left moving right, or on the right moving left. I’ve split each of those by speed, giving four regions: A (left, fast), B (left, slow), C (right, fast), D (right, slow).

With an open hole every molecule carries on through. A ends up on the right moving right, C ends up on the left moving left, and so on. Four regions in, four distinct regions out. With a solid wall every molecule bounces: its velocity flips and it stays on its own side. Again four in, four distinct out. Both are perfectly good mechanics.

Now apply Maxwell’s rule. A fast molecule from the left (A) is let through, so it leaves to the right, moving right, fast. A fast molecule from the right (C) is bounced, so it also leaves on the right, moving right, fast. A and C land in the same place. In the same way B (slow from the left, bounced) and D (slow from the right, let through) both end up on the left moving left, slow. Four regions in, two out. Half of phase space is never visited after an encounter with the gate.

Look at one of the fast molecules leaving to the right. Did it come through the gate, or bounce off it? The state of the gas no longer tells you. That is exactly the many-to-one map that Liouville forbids. So the gate’s rule is not something mechanics, on its own, can do. The rule is fine as a thought experiment, and it does sort the gas: the simulation in lecture 4 shows that, and so does the flux calculation. What Liouville tells us is that the rule cannot be the whole of the physics. Something else has to enter the story.

Figure: Left: on its own the gate sends two different pasts to one present. Middle: if the gate keeps a record of what it did, the combined state of gas plus record is one-to-one again, and Liouville is satisfied. Right: to run the demon in a cycle the record has to be wiped, which merges two memory states into one. That merge can only be paid for by pushing entropy into the surroundings, at least \(k_BT\ln 2\) of heat per bit.

If the gas alone can’t hold the history, maybe something else can. Suppose the gate keeps a record each time it acts: “let through” or “bounced”. Now the joint state of the gas and the record is one-to-one again. A fast molecule leaving to the right with the record “bounced” came from C; with “let through” it came from A. No volume has been lost. It has been moved from the gas into the record.

This is why Liouville sits so close to Landauer’s principle. A finite memory fills up. To keep the demon running in a cycle we have to reset the record to blank, and that reset is itself a two-to-one map: “let through” and “bounced” both go to “blank”. The merge is back, this time in the memory. The only way a reset can happen in a world governed by mechanics is if the lost volume is pushed into something else, usually a heat bath. Landauer’s figure of at least \(k_BT\ln 2\) of heat per erased bit is the size of that push (Landauer, 1961). Bennett used this to put the demon’s cost at erasure rather than at measurement (Bennett, 1982).

Earman and Norton argue that this resolution assumes some of what it sets out to prove (Earman and Norton, 1999, 1998). That debate is real, and I don’t want to present the erasure story as settled. But the phase-space picture is not in dispute: the gate’s rule, on its own, throws away volume, and the volume has to go somewhere.

Figure: Two three-state systems with the same stationary distribution (one third each). On the left every flow is cancelled by its own reverse: detailed balance. On the right the probabilities still don’t change in time, because each state gains as much as it loses, but the clockwise flows are twice the anticlockwise ones and a net current goes round the loop. That is a steady state, not an equilibrium.

Detailed balance is a statement about transitions between states. Write \(\pi_i\) for the equilibrium probability of state \(i\) and \(k_{ij}\) for the rate of jumping from \(i\) to \(j\). The probability flowing from \(i\) to \(j\) per unit time is \(\pi_i k_{ij}\). Detailed balance says that for every pair, \[ \pi_i k_{ij} = \pi_j k_{ji}. \] Note that this is a balance of probability flows, not of rates. If \(j\) is much less likely than \(i\), the rate back from \(j\) has to be correspondingly higher.

It is easy to confuse detailed balance with stationarity, and the figure shows why they differ. Stationarity only asks that each state’s total inflow equals its total outflow, so the probabilities stop changing. On the right of the figure that holds, but probability is circulating clockwise round the loop. You can have a steady current with unchanging probabilities. Detailed balance rules that out. It is what equilibrium means at the level of individual transitions, and it follows from reversible microscopic laws together with a thermal stationary distribution. (When velocities are involved the reverse of a transition also flips the velocities: the reverse of “fast molecule moves left to right” is “fast molecule moves right to left”.)

Now look at the gate again. A fast molecule crossing left to right is allowed. Its reverse, a fast molecule crossing right to left, is bounced. A slow molecule crossing right to left is allowed; its reverse is bounced. The gate is built from transitions whose reverses it forbids. That is the right-hand panel: a steady state with a current running through it, not an equilibrium. If the gas and the gate are really at one temperature, detailed balance has to hold, so once we include the gate’s own workings the reverse moves must come back. If they don’t, something outside the box is driving it.

In lecture 4 we called the condition “as many molecules cross left to right as right to left” flux balance. That is stationarity across the membrane. It is not detailed balance, and the gate can satisfy one while violating the other.

Let me be careful about how this connects to lecture 4. There we treated each side as a Maxwellian at its own temperature and asked whether the flows across the gate could balance. They can’t, for both number and energy at once: a fast molecule carries more energy than a slow one, so when equal numbers cross each way more energy goes right than comes back. That told us the steady state is not two clean Maxwellians. The tails near the gate are continually being eaten away and refilled by collisions.

That is a statement about which description of the gas is right. It is not, by itself, a proof that the demon fails. The gas really does sort under the gate’s rule: the simulation shows it, and the entropy of the gas really does fall. What Liouville and detailed balance add is the reason the sorting can’t be free. The gate’s rule merges states, so the merge has to be paid for somewhere. And at a single temperature the reverse transitions it forbids have to reappear once the gate is made of real physics. Which brings us to the trapdoor.

Figure: Smoluchowski’s trapdoor. A light flap on a soft spring is meant to open only when a fast molecule hits it from the left. But a flap light enough to be pushed by one molecule is light enough to be shaken by the gas around it. It has its own thermal energy, rattles open at random (faint positions), and lets molecules back the wrong way.

Smoluchowski asked the engineering question in 1912. Replace the demon with a mechanism: a small flap over the hole, held shut by a weak spring, that a molecule coming from the left can push open but one from the right can’t. No intelligence, no measurement, no memory. Surely that sorts the gas?

It doesn’t, and the reason has nothing to do with information. The flap is itself a physical object in contact with the gas. For one molecule to push it open it has to be very light and the spring very soft. But then the flap is shaken by the gas just like any other light object. Equipartition gives it \(\tfrac{1}{2}k_BT\) of kinetic energy in its swing and another \(\tfrac{1}{2}k_BT\) stored in the spring, on average, just as every molecule has \(\tfrac{1}{2}k_BT\) per direction of motion. It rattles. Sometimes it is open when a molecule arrives from the right, and lets it through the wrong way. Sometimes it slams shut and knocks a molecule back. Once we include the flap in the description, detailed balance holds for gas plus flap together, and the net sorting averages to zero. Feynman’s ratchet and pawl, in the Feynman Lectures (Volume I, chapter 46), is the same argument for extracting work rather than sorting.

There is a tempting fix: keep the flap cold so that it doesn’t rattle. That does work for a while. But a cold flap in a warm gas is a second heat bath at a different temperature, and what we have built is a small heat engine running between the flap and the gas. It stops when the flap has warmed up. We haven’t beaten the second law; we’ve brought in a second reservoir.

So the trapdoor is not a Liouville-and-memory story. Each time the flap shuts, the two-to-one merge from the gate picture is being absorbed by the flap’s own jiggling, which is passed straight back to the gas as heat. There is no record to fill or wipe. If we want to coarse-grain this system we can’t just add a “door open / door closed” label to our compartments. We have to add the flap as a real degree of freedom, with its own position, momentum, and temperature, and check detailed balance for the enlarged system.

Good Regulator Overview

[edit]

Overview only. Full Conant–Ashby development — concavity argument, existence of a deterministic optimum, and the two caveats in depth — is the Week 8 review. Today’s job is the setup, the notation, and the slogan and its two caveats. Diagrams follow Joery’s oneshot figure in the good-regulator notes: disturbance \(D\), system state \(S\), action \(A\), reguland \(Z\).

Conant and Ashby (Conant and Ashby, 1970) often label the regulator \(R\). We write \(A\) for the action so the policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\) is first-class. The reguland — the variable whose entropy successful regulation minimises — is \(Z\). The system state is \(S\); the disturbance is \(D\). The cumulant generating function earlier today was \(\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\log Z(-\boldsymbol{\theta})\); that \(Z\) was the partition function, not the reguland — different objects, same letter in the physics tradition.

Figure: Cause-controlled regulation (left) acts on the disturbance \(D\); error-controlled regulation (right) acts on the state \(S\) that \(D\) caused.

A disturbance \(D\) causes events in a system \(S\); the regulator counters with an action \(A\); the pair produces a reguland \(Z=\phi(S,A)\) (Conant and Ashby, 1970). Cause-controlled regulation acts on \(D\) (or on an inferred model of \(D\)). Error-controlled regulation acts on the state \(S\) caused by \(D\), so by the data-processing inequality \(I(A;D)\le I(S;D)\). Both diagrams are shown above; the existence proof that an \(H(Z)\)-optimal policy may be taken deterministic waits for Week 8.

Successful regulation, for Conant and Ashby, minimises the entropy of the reguland: \(\min_\pi H(Z)\). Among optima there exists a deterministic policy \(A=h(S)\), equivalently \(H(A\mid S)=0\). That is the sense in which the regulator is a model'' --- deliberately weak. Do not collapse the slogan into\(A\) contains a rich model of \(S\)’’: a constant action also has \(H(A\mid S)=0\). Do not confuse low \(H(Z)\) with a desirable outcome. Requisite variety in Shannon form will use the same letters: residual uncertainty satisfies \(H(Z)\ge H(D)-I(D;A)\) when \(A\) is the regulatory response. Week 8 reviews the theorem properly and applies it to capability claims.

Ashby’s law of requisite variety in Shannon form (Ashby, 1956), written in today’s letters: residual uncertainty in the reguland is at least the disturbance entropy minus the information the action carries about the disturbance. Requisite variety asks how much relevant \(I\) is required; the Good Regulator Theorem asks how that \(I\) may be organised. Full development of both, with the concavity argument, is Week 8.

Goals as Radon–Nikodym

Conant and Ashby minimise the entropy of the reguland. We might ask the question, how does that relate to the more usual approach in reinforcement learning where we maximise a reward function. In ongoing work (with Joery de Vries) we suggest that the long-run state–action occupancy is pushed through a fixed channel \(K(z\mid s,a)\) to an outcome law \(p_\pi\) on \(Z\). Minimising \(H(Z)\) is concave over the occupancy polytope, so a deterministic policy is optimal.

If the “good” outcomes are not the whole alphabet, then we introduce a goal measure \(\nu\), a “measure of success” as it were. This must be absolutely continuous with respect to the base measure \(\lambda\). The change of base is \[ H_\nu(Z)=H_\lambda(Z)+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}(Z)\right]. \] The second term is the goal: writing \(r=-\log(\mathrm{d}\nu/\mathrm{d}\lambda)\) makes it \(-\mathbb{E}[r(Z)]\). So ordinary reward maximisation is entropy minimisation under a reference (the measure of success). This is the same change-of-measure we reviewed when considering speed and velocity of a Maxwellian gas. Our interpretation is that Conant and Ashby’s good set \(G\) is the special case that puts smaller \(\nu\)-volume on \(G\) than on \(G^c\), so the goal term pays for landing in \(G\).

The Measure of Success as a Coarse Graining

This block extends “Goals as Radon–Nikodym”. The identity \(H_\nu(Z)=H(Z)-\mathbb{E}[r(Z)]\), with \(r=-\log(\mathrm{d}\nu/\mathrm{d}\lambda)\), was stated as a change of measure. Here we read it as the grouping formula from Week 4. Two lessons follow. First, coarser success is easier: the more outcomes you are willing to lump together, the less entropy is left to remove and the less requisite variety the regulator needs. Second, any reward function can be read backwards as a coarse graining. It says how many finer outcomes each observed outcome stands for, and the regulator is assumed to have no grip inside each one.

Conant and Ashby minimise the entropy of the reguland, but the reguland is a description, not a fact of nature. Suppose the world has a fine description \(X\) and we measure success on a summary \(Z=T(X)\): a temperature band rather than a temperature, “patient discharged” rather than a full physiological state. Because \(T\) is deterministic, \(H(T(X))\le H(X)\) for every policy. This is the same DPI-style fact we met with \(h_0\) in Week 6. So the coarser the measure of success, the lower the entropy the regulator has to remove. At the extreme, a single cell (“anything is fine”) gives \(H(Z)=0\) under every policy. Every regulator is then optimal, including the constant one. That is caveat 1 seen from the other side: the constant action \(A=a_0\) is a good regulator exactly when the measure of success is coarse enough not to care.

Figure: Minimum achievable entropy of the success class against cell width, for regulators with \(m\) evenly spaced actions that observe \(D\). Each doubling of tolerance saves one bit, exactly as each doubling of action variety does.

A toy thermostat. The disturbance \(D\) is uniform on \(\{0,\dots,7\}\), so \(H(D)=3\) bits. The regulator sees \(D\) and picks one of \(m\) evenly spaced corrective shifts, and the outcome is \(X=(D+A)\bmod 8\). We call the run a success at the level of bands of width \(w\), \(C=\lfloor X/w\rfloor\). Brute force over all deterministic policies gives \[ \min_h H(C)=\max\left(0,\ \log_2\tfrac{8}{w}-\log_2 m\right). \] Doubling the tolerance \(w\) saves exactly one bit, and so does doubling the action variety \(m\): the two trade bit for bit. This is requisite variety in Shannon form, \(H(Z)\ge H(D)-I(D;A)\), applied to the coarse variable. A coarse measure of success shrinks the effective \(H(D)\), because disturbances that land in the same cell no longer need different responses. It also bounds how fine the “model” \(h:S\to A\) has to be. The regulator only needs to tell apart states whose correct responses differ at the level of \(C\). The measure of success sets the resolution of the model the theorem forces.

Week 4 gave the grouping formula \(H=H(w)+\sum_i w_i H(p_i)\). Write it with the fine description \(X\) grouped by the outcome \(Z\): \[ H(X)=H(Z)+\sum_z P(Z=z)\,H(X\mid Z=z). \] Now suppose each outcome \(z\) is a cell containing \(\nu(z)\) fine states, and the regulator has no control inside a cell. The MaxEnt description of “no control” is uniform within the cell, so \(H(X\mid Z=z)=\log\nu(z)\). Define \(r(z)=-\log\nu(z)\). Then \[ H(X)=H(Z)-\mathbb{E}[r(Z)]=H_\nu(Z), \] which is exactly the goal-measure identity from the previous block, with \(\nu\) as the cell-volume measure and \(\lambda\) as counting on \(Z\). So minimising entropy relative to the measure of success is minimising the entropy of the fine world, on the assumption that the regulator only acts on which cell it lands in. The reward of an outcome is minus the entropy left inside it. High reward means a small cell: landing there certifies a precise fine state. Low reward means a big cell: landing there leaves the world mostly unconstrained. This is Week 6’s density of states again: \(\nu\) plays the role of \(h_0\), Boltzmann’s \(W\), and \(\mathbb{E}[\log h_0]\) was “the entropy lost by recording only \(k\)”.

Figure: Plain \(H(Z)\) cannot tell always-succeed from always-fail. Entropy relative to the measure of success can: its minimum is at \(q=1\) and its maximum is at \(q=0.1\), the uniform distribution over the ten fine states.

Ten fine states, one of them good. Coarse-grain into \(G\) (one state) and \(G^c\) (nine states), so \(r(G)=0\) and \(r(G^c)=-\log_2 9\approx-3.17\) bits. With \(q=P(Z\in G)\):

Under the counting measure, “always fail” (\(q=0\)) and “always succeed” (\(q=1\)) are equally good regulators, because both are perfectly predictable. That is caveat 2. The measure of success breaks the tie: always failing leaves \(\log_2 9\) bits in the fine world, always succeeding leaves none. The maximum of \(H_\nu\) is at \(q=0.1\), where the fine state is uniform on all ten: \(\log_2 10\approx3.32\) bits. The reward gap, \(\log_2 9\) bits, is just the log of the ratio of the cell sizes.

The construction runs both ways. Given a coarse graining with cell volumes \(\nu\), the reward is \(r=-\log\nu\). Given any reward \(r\), the measure \(\nu(z)=e^{-r(z)}\) can be read as the volume of a cell of hidden fine states that outcome \(z\) stands for. Several familiar facts about rewards become facts about coarse graining.

  • Shift invariance. Adding a constant to \(r\) multiplies every volume by the same factor. That is a change of unit, so the optimal policy does not move. This is why reward shifts do not matter in RL.
  • The good set. Conant and Ashby’s \(G\) is the two-cell case. The size of the reward for success is the log of how much bigger failure is than success.
  • Reward scale. Multiplying \(r\) by \(\beta\) raises the volume ratios to the power \(\beta\). A sparse reward of 1 versus 0 (in nats) says success is a cell \(e\approx2.7\) times smaller than failure. That is a mild preference; \(\beta=10\) makes it about 22,000 times smaller.
  • Additive rewards over time. If \(Z=(Z_1,\dots,Z_T)\) and \(r(z)=\sum_t r(z_t)\), then \(\nu(z)=\prod_t\nu(z_t)\). The cell of a trajectory is the product of the per-step cells, as if each step had its own independent fine degrees of freedom.
  • Constant reward. Every cell has the same volume, so \(H_\nu\) and \(H\) differ by a constant. We are back to plain Conant and Ashby: predictability with no preference.

Not every reward comes from a literal partition of a finite set, because \(e^{-r}\) need not be an integer. That is why the course states the goal as a measure \(\nu\ll\lambda\) and the reward as a Radon–Nikodym derivative. The volumes are like the Poisson’s \(1/k!\) in Week 6: a volume, not a count.

The between-cell term \(H(Z)\) is bounded by \(\log|\mathcal{Z}|\). The within-cell term \(-\mathbb{E}[r(Z)]\) is not. So if one policy’s expected reward exceeds another’s by more than \(\log|\mathcal{Z}|\), it has lower \(H_\nu\) whatever the outcome entropies are: \[ H_\nu(\pi)-H_\nu(\pi')=\big[H(Z_\pi)-H(Z_{\pi'})\big]-\big[\mathbb{E}_\pi r-\mathbb{E}_{\pi'}r\big]<\log|\mathcal{Z}|-\log|\mathcal{Z}|=0. \] This is the “volume threshold” on the RL recovery slide. When cell volumes differ by much more than the number of cells, the regulator gains far more by landing in a small cell than by being predictable about which cell it lands in. Entropy minimisation then becomes reward maximisation. When reward gaps are small compared with \(\log|\mathcal{Z}|\), the entropy term matters: the regulator will trade some reward for predictability. In both regimes \(H_\nu\) is concave in the occupancy, because \(H\) is concave and \(\mathbb{E}[r]\) is linear. So the minimum is at a vertex of the occupancy polytope, a deterministic policy. That is the same structure that gives deterministic optimal policies in MDPs.

The identity \(H(X)=H_\nu(Z)\) needs the fine state to be uniform inside each cell. If the regulator does have some grip inside a cell, \(H(X\mid Z=z)\le\log\nu(z)\), and minimising \(H_\nu\) minimises an upper bound on the fine-grained entropy. This is Jaynes’ reading of MaxEnt from Week 5: uniform within cells is the honest description of what we have declared we will not track. So a reward function carries three choices, usually hidden: what counts as an outcome (the partition), how big each outcome is (\(\nu\)), and what the regulator is assumed unable to control (MaxEnt inside each cell). Changing any one of them changes what “good regulation” means.

Taken together: Conant and Ashby’s criterion, \(\min H(Z)\), is only as meaningful as the description \(Z\). Coarse-graining the description makes regulation easier, in bits you can count. A reward function is a compact way of specifying a coarse graining with unequal cells, and its value at each outcome is minus the entropy that outcome leaves unresolved. Reward maximisation is the large-gap limit of entropy minimisation relative to that coarse graining.

Data-Processing Inequality

[edit]

Mutual information is the pairwise case of multi-information: \(I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)=H(Y)-H(Y|X)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)\). Week 2 defined it from the chain rule and stated the data-processing inequality. We prove it now, because this week’s object is \(I\).

The data-processing inequality (Cover and Thomas, 1991, Thm 2.8.1) is the no-go on \(I\). If \(X\to Y\to Z\) is Markov, then \(I(X;Z)\le I(X;Y)\). The first expansion of \(I(X;Y,Z)\) uses the chain rule and drops \(I(X;Z\mid Y)\) by the Markov property. The second expansion is at least \(I(X;Z)\) because conditional mutual information is non-negative. Equality holds when \(I(X;Y\mid Z)=0\) — \(Y\) is a sufficient statistic of \(X\) for \(Z\), or \(Z\) already captures everything \(Y\) knew about \(X\).

This is the same grammar as \(I+H=C\). Conservation says you cannot have both high stored correlation and high free uncertainty without bound. Data processing says you cannot increase stored correlation by further processing. A claim that an intelligent system will “just process more” and thereby recover information that a Markov stage has already destroyed is a DPI violation.

KL Decay and Grouping

[edit]

\[ \text{KL}\left( p\,\|\,\pi \right)=\text{KL}\left( w\,\|\,w_\pi \right)+\sum_i w_i\, \text{KL}\left( p_i\,\|\,\pi_i \right) \]

For a Markov process with stationary distribution \(\pi\), \(\text{KL}\left( p_t\,\|\,\pi \right)\) is nonincreasing — the data-processing inequality applied to the evolving law. (Physics literature often calls the same functional relative entropy.) Decompose \(p\) into group weights \(w\) and conditionals \(p_i\), and likewise \(\pi\) into \(w_\pi\) and \(\pi_i\). Then \[\begin{align} \text{KL}\left( p\,\|\,\pi \right)=\text{KL}\left( w\,\|\,w_\pi \right)+\sum_i w_i\, \text{KL}\left( p_i\,\|\,\pi_i \right). \end{align}\] The first term is how far the macro weights sit from equilibrium weights; the second is residual non-equilibrium inside groups. Local equilibrium is the regime \(\text{KL}\left( p_i\,\|\,\pi_i \right)\approx 0\) with \(\text{KL}\left( w\,\|\,w_\pi \right)\) still free to fall. Under deterministic Hamiltonian flow the fine-grained Shannon entropy of the phase-space density is conserved (Liouville); the Gibbs–Ehrenfest account of the second law is that only a entropy increases. Week 2’s die made the static split; this is the same algebra along a trajectory toward equilibrium.

Information engines and finite-time protocols act primarily on the between-group term — changing which macrostate or which bit is occupied — while assuming or enforcing fast decay of the within-group divergences. That is why week 2’s stationary examples and week 6’s paths on the equilibrium manifold belong to one story.

Information Bottleneck

[edit]

The data-processing inequality forbids creating information by processing. It does not say which information to keep. The information bottleneck (Tishby et al., 1999) is the prescription: given an observation \(X\) and a relevance variable \(Y\), choose a representation \(T\) of \(X\) that keeps \(I(T;Y)\) and discards the rest of \(I(X;T)\).

The information bottleneck of Tishby, Pereira and Bialek is a constrained optimisation on mutual information. The encoder \(p(t\mid x)\) is Markov given \(X\), so \(Y \leftrightarrow X \leftrightarrow T\). Data processing then supplies the bound \(I(T;Y)\le I(X;Y)\): no representation of \(X\) can be more informative about \(Y\) than \(X\) itself. The Lagrange multiplier \(\beta\) interpolates. Small \(\beta\) spends almost no \(I\) on the representation. Large \(\beta\) recovers \(T=X\). The self-consistent encoder is an exponential family, \[ p(t\mid x) \propto p(t)\,\exp\bigl(-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p(y\mid x)\,\|\,p(y\mid t)\bigr)\bigr), \] the same Boltzmann form as week 4, now with a KL sufficient statistic. We do not run Blahut–Arimoto. The point is the pair: DPI is the no-go; the bottleneck is how you spend \(I\).

Week 4 asked for the least-committal \(p\) given moments. Here the constraint is a mutual information. Same Lagrange move, different constraint. Later in this session the bottleneck evaluates intelligence claims: a system that keeps only what is relevant for action is making an IB statement. The Good Regulator existence claim \(H(A\mid S)=0\) comes from concavity of entropy; only then may one offer the control interpretation \(\min I(S;A)\) subject to \(H(Z)\le\epsilon\) as an IB-shaped reading — an interpretation after the theorem, not the theorem itself. Requisite variety and the Good Regulator are tools we use in Week 8; the information bottleneck itself is the relevant-compression theorem.
